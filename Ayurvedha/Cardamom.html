Ela
Cardamom — Queen of Spices
Elettaria cardamomum (L.) Maton  ·  Amomum subulatum Roxb. (Black Cardamom)

Fig. 1 — Elettaria cardamomum growing in the Western Ghats understorey; pale flower spikes emerge from basal runners close to the ground  ·  Fig. 2 — Opened green cardamom pod revealing the cluster of dark, intensely aromatic seeds — the seat of its therapeutic potency

❧

Ancient trade routes carried the fragrance of Ela from the Western Ghats to the courts of ancient Rome, the kitchens of the Arab world, and the pharmacopeias of every great healing tradition. Dubbed the Queen of Spices — pepper being king — cardamom has been simultaneously the world's most expensive spice by weight and one of Ayurveda's most versatile medicines: a digestive, a breath freshener, a cardiac tonic, a respiratory remedy, and a nervine — all contained within one small ribbed green pod.

01

Botanical Profile

True cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) is a tall, rhizomatous perennial herb of the Zingiberaceae family — the ginger family — native to the moist evergreen forests of the Western Ghats of southern India, primarily in Kerala, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu. The plant grows to 2–4 m tall with long, lance-shaped leaves arranged alternately on erect pseudostems arising from a central rhizome. The flowering and fruiting stems grow separately from the base, close to the ground — a characteristic prostrate inflorescence unique among the spice plants of India.

The flowers are pale white with a violet-veined lip, borne in loose racemes on long panicles from January to May. The fruits — the commercially valuable pods — are ovoid to oblong, 1–2 cm, pale green, ribbed, and contain 15–20 small black or dark-brown seeds in three internal chambers. These seeds are the medicinal and culinary part. The essential oil content of the seeds is 2–10%, the highest volatile oil concentration of any spice plant in the ginger family.

Phytochemistry & Composition

The pharmacological activity of cardamom resides almost entirely in its essential oil fraction — the most concentrated and complex volatile oil in the spice world. The seeds contain 2–10% essential oil, up to 40% starch, 10% fixed oils, proteins, and minerals including manganese, iron, zinc, and magnesium. The essential oil is dominated by 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol) and α-terpinyl acetate — two terpene esters that together account for 65–85% of the oil's composition and drive the respiratory, antimicrobial, and digestive actions.

The characteristic sweet-floral fragrance of green cardamom comes primarily from linalyl acetate and linalool; the camphoraceous quality from 1,8-cineole; and the fresh spicy note from sabinene and β-pinene. Cardamom seeds are also notably rich in antioxidant flavonoids (quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol glycosides) and phenolic acids (caffeic, ferulic, p-coumaric acids) — active at much lower concentrations than the essential oil but contributing meaningfully to its anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective profile.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Ela holds a place of exceptional prestige in Ayurveda — classified among the Trikatu and Trijataka groups, included in nearly every major digestive, respiratory, and cardiac formulation, and described in all the great Nighantus with rare superlative praise. Charaka includes it in the Eladi Varga — naming the group after Ela itself — and prescribes it in Chikitsasthana for cough, asthma, dysuria, nausea, and cardiac conditions. Sushruta includes it in Eladi Gana and commends it for mouth diseases, urinary disorders, and wasting conditions.

"Ela sukshma hima madhura vata-pitta-kaphashamani, chardi-trishna-visham-kasa-shwasa-mutrakriccha-nashani" — Bhavaprakasha Nighantu, Karpuradi Varga — Core classical description of Sukshmela (small cardamom)

The shloka describes small Ela as cold in potency, sweet in taste, a balancer of all three doshas — and specifically a destroyer of vomiting, thirst, toxic states, cough, asthma, and painful urination. This tridoshic quality is exceedingly rare among spices — most warm spices aggravate Pitta — and is attributed to the unique combination of Madhura (sweet) taste and Sheeta (cold) virya (potency) that distinguishes cardamom from its relatives ginger, pepper, and clove. Ashtanga Hridaya includes Ela in Hridya (cardiac-strengthening) preparations and Chakrapanidatta comments on its use in hrillasa (nausea and cardiac unease).

04

Key Health Benefits

The Only Tridoshic Spice — Rare Among All Culinary Herbs
Of all commonly used spices, cardamom is unique in its ability to pacify Vata, Pitta, and Kapha simultaneously — a consequence of its unusual combination of sweet taste with cold potency, a pairing seen in almost no other pungent spice. Ginger, black pepper, and clove are all warm and Pitta-aggravating; turmeric is predominantly bitter-cooling. Ela's triple-dosha action makes it the most versatile therapeutic spice in Ayurvedic formulation — included in digestive, cardiac, respiratory, urinary, and neurological preparations with equal confidence.
Digestive & Carminative
Cardamom's primary classical indication. The essential oil stimulates digestive enzyme secretion (amylase, lipase, protease), accelerates gastric emptying, and relieves smooth-muscle spasm in the gut. Clinical studies confirm significant reduction in indigestion, bloating, flatulence, and nausea. Used classically as dipana (digestive stimulant) and pachana (digestive) — simultaneously stimulating and soothing.
Respiratory & Anti-tussive
1,8-Cineole — the dominant constituent — is one of the most well-validated mucolytics and bronchodilators in plant medicine, licensed as a pharmaceutical in Europe for bronchitis and COPD. In Ayurveda, Ela is a primary ingredient in Sitopaladi Churna and Talisadi Churna for cough and asthma. Inhalation of cardamom essential oil significantly improves nasal airflow and reduces respiratory secretion viscosity.
Cardiovascular & Anti-hypertensive
Randomised clinical trial (Verma et al., 2009) of 3 g cardamom powder daily for 12 weeks in newly diagnosed stage-1 hypertensives showed significant reduction in systolic and diastolic BP with improvement in fibrinolytic activity. Mechanism: linalool-mediated calcium channel antagonism and ACE-inhibiting phenolic acids. Classical Ayurveda calls Ela hridya — heart-strengthening.
Antimicrobial & Oral Health
One of the oldest breath-freshening remedies — chewing cardamom pods after meals is described in ancient Indian texts and is still practised across South and West Asia. α-Terpinyl acetate and 1,8-cineole show potent activity against oral pathogens including Streptococcus mutans (cavity-causing), Candida albicans, and halitosis-producing anaerobes. Effective against H. pylori — the bacterium underlying peptic ulcers.
Anti-diabetic & Metabolic
Animal studies demonstrate significant reduction in fasting blood glucose, improvement in insulin sensitivity, and reduction in HbA1c with cardamom supplementation. Quercetin and luteolin inhibit α-glucosidase. A human trial in pre-diabetic women showed cardamom supplementation significantly reduced serum triglycerides and LDL cholesterol — supporting its classical use in prameha (metabolic/diabetic conditions).
Anxiolytic & Neuroprotective
Linalool and linalyl acetate — present in both the inhaled aroma and ingested oil — modulate GABA-A receptor activity producing anxiolytic effects validated in rodent models. Classical Ayurveda recommends cardamom milk (Ela Ksheerpaka) as a nervine tonic for anxiety, insomnia, and debility. Aroma inhalation of cardamom essential oil during chemotherapy reduces nausea and anxiety in clinical nursing studies.
Diuretic & Urinary Support
Classical indication: mutrakriccha nashana (destroyer of painful urination). Cardamom's mild diuretic activity combined with its antimicrobial 1,8-cineole makes it effective for urinary tract irritation, dysuria, and cystitis. Sheeta virya (cold potency) soothes the burning sensation associated with Pitta-type UTIs that most warming diuretics would aggravate.
Antioxidant & Liver Protection
Caffeic acid, ferulic acid, and flavonoid glycosides in cardamom seeds show significant DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activity. Cardamom supplementation in rats exposed to hepatotoxic agents significantly preserved liver enzyme profiles and reduced lipid peroxidation. Caffeic acid specifically prevents NF-κB–driven hepatic inflammation — relevant to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), an expanding clinical application.

Classical Formulations

Ela is one of the most widely incorporated spices in classical Ayurvedic compound preparations — appearing in hundreds of formulas across all therapeutic categories. Its tridoshic nature makes it a universal adjuvant and flavour corrective (yogavahi) that also enhances the bioavailability and tolerability of more aggressive drugs in the formula.

How to Use Ela

Cardamom is one of the safest and most versatile medicinal spices — appropriate for daily culinary use as a food medicine and for targeted therapeutic applications in higher doses. The seeds deliver the full active profile; the pods protect the seeds but contain only negligible activity themselves. Fat-soluble essential oil constituents are better absorbed with warm milk or ghee; water-soluble phenolic compounds are extracted well in decoctions and infusions.

Safety & Precautions

Cardamom is among the safest medicinal spices — generally recognised as safe (GRAS) by the FDA for food use, with no documented toxicity at culinary or standard therapeutic doses. It has a multi-millennial record of daily use as both food and medicine across India, the Middle East, and Scandinavia. A few specific considerations apply at therapeutic doses.

  • Pregnancy: Culinary doses are entirely safe and cardamom is routinely used in Indian cooking throughout pregnancy. High therapeutic doses (above 3 g seed powder daily) have historically been used as uterine stimulants in classical obstetric protocols — avoid therapeutic-dose supplementation in the first trimester without practitioner guidance.
  • Gallstones: Cardamom is a classical choleretic (bile stimulant). In individuals with known gallstones, stimulating bile flow could trigger biliary colic — use cautiously and inform treating physician before starting therapeutic doses.
  • Anti-coagulant interaction: At very high doses, cardamom's mild platelet-aggregation inhibiting effects could theoretically potentiate warfarin or aspirin — relevant only at supplemental doses far above culinary use. Inform physician if taking blood thinners.
  • Essential oil internal use: Cardamom essential oil should not be taken internally without specific practitioner guidance. External and inhalation use is safe. The concentrated oil is potent enough to cause mucosal irritation if consumed undiluted.
  • Drug interaction (anti-hypertensives): Cardamom's mild BP-lowering and calcium-channel-modulating effects at therapeutic doses may potentiate anti-hypertensive medications — beneficial synergy for most patients but monitor BP if using cardamom supplementation alongside prescription anti-hypertensives.
  • Allergy: Cross-reactivity with other Zingiberaceae (ginger, turmeric) is rare but documented. Those with known ginger allergy should conduct a patch test and monitor for oral allergy symptoms when consuming cardamom therapeutically.
  • Quality & adulteration: Pre-ground cardamom powder is frequently adulterated. For therapeutic use, always purchase whole green pods and grind seeds fresh. Look for AGMARK-certified or single-origin Kerala/Karnataka cardamom. Bleached white cardamom pods have had the essential oil volatilised and hold minimal medicinal value.
