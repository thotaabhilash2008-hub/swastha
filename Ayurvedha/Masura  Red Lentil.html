Masura
Red Lentil — The Ancient Nourisher
Lens culinaris Medik.  ·  Lens esculenta Moench

❧

Known in Sanskrit as Masura — mentioned in the Charaka Samhita among the foremost of Shimbi Dhanya (pulse grains) — the red lentil has sustained civilisations for over 9,000 years, its humble split form concealing one of nature's most complete plant protein matrices and a rich tradition of both dietary medicine and topical Ayurvedic therapy.

01

Botanical Profile

Masura (Lens culinaris) is a slender, erect annual herb of the Fabaceae (Leguminosae) family, cultivated across South Asia, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean since at least 7000 BCE — placing it among humanity's oldest cultivated crops. The plant grows 20–40 cm tall with pinnately compound leaves ending in a tendril, small white to pale violet pea-like flowers, and characteristic flat, lens-shaped pods containing one to two seeds.

The name "lentil" itself derives from the Latin lens — for the seed's distinctive biconvex shape, which later lent its name to optical lenses. The red or orange colour of the split lentil is the inner cotyledon revealed after dehulling; the whole lentil is typically brown or greenish. India is the world's largest consumer and a major producer, with masur dal (split red lentil) being among the most widely consumed pulses in Ayurvedic dietetics.

Nutritional Composition

Red lentils are among the most nutritionally dense legumes, delivering exceptional protein with a near-complete amino acid profile (high in lysine, deficient only in methionine — complemented by grains), significant dietary fibre including resistant starch and prebiotics, and a remarkable spectrum of micronutrients. Per 100 g of cooked red lentils they provide approximately 9 g protein, 20 g complex carbohydrates, 8 g dietary fibre, and under 0.4 g fat — with virtually no sodium and a low glycaemic index of ~21.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Masura is classified in the Shimbi Dhanya Varga (pulse grains group) in Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, and Ashtanga Hridayam. Charaka describes it among the best of pulses for those with digestive strength (deepana) and as beneficial in skin diseases, eye disorders, and as a blood purifier. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu records it under Dhanya Varga with specific properties — Kashaya (astringent) and Madhura (sweet) in taste, Laghu (light) and Ruksha (dry) in quality, and Sheeta (cool) in potency.

The classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha — "Masura kashayamadhuro laghurushtho vistambhi cha, chakshurhyo vatapitta-hrit kaphamehashonitapranut" — describes Masura as slightly constipating (vistambhi), beneficial to the eyes (chakshurhya), alleviating Vata and Pitta, and useful in Kapha disorders, diabetes (meha), and bleeding conditions (rakta-pitta).

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern nutritional science and pharmacology have extensively validated Masura's role in cardiovascular health, glycaemic control, gut microbiome support, and skin care — corroborating classical Ayurvedic applications documented over 2,500 years of use.

Cardiovascular Health
Regular lentil consumption is associated with significant reductions in LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and blood pressure. The combination of soluble fibre, potassium, folate (reducing homocysteine), and polyphenols creates a multi-pathway cardioprotective effect supported by multiple prospective cohort studies.
Glycaemic Control & Diabetes
With a glycaemic index of ~21 and high resistant starch content, red lentils significantly blunt postprandial blood glucose and insulin spikes. Classical use in Prameha (diabetes/metabolic disorders) is directly supported by clinical evidence showing improved HbA1c with regular dal consumption.
Digestive & Gut Health
The prebiotic fibre and resistant starch of Masura feeds beneficial Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species in the colon. Regular consumption is associated with improved gut transit, reduced constipation (despite classical vistambhi designation at high intake), and production of beneficial short-chain fatty acids.
Skin Health & Topical Use
Classical Ayurveda prominently uses Masura flour paste (ubtana) for skin brightening, complexion enhancement, and treatment of acne, oily skin, and hyperpigmentation. Modern evidence confirms lentil flour's mild exfoliant, sebum-regulating, and tyrosinase-inhibiting properties relevant to melanin reduction.
Anaemia & Iron Deficiency
With ~7 mg iron per 100g raw, red lentils are among the richest plant sources of non-haem iron. The accompanying folate and Vitamin C (when prepared with tomatoes or lemon) significantly enhances iron bioavailability — making masur dal a classical dietary prescription for Pandu (anaemia) in Ayurveda.
Pregnancy & Foetal Health
Masura's folate content (479 mcg/100g raw — exceeding the daily requirement) makes it exceptional for pregnant women and those planning conception. Folate is critical for neural tube closure, and classical Ayurvedic texts specifically recommend Masura in post-partum care and for nourishing dhatus (body tissues).
Weight Management
The high protein and fibre content of red lentils produces significant satiety, reducing overall caloric intake. Being Laghu (light) in Ayurveda — yet highly satiating — Masura occupies a unique position as a complete food appropriate for those managing obesity and Kapha-related metabolic sluggishness.
Eye Health & Vision
Classical texts specifically designate Masura as chakshurhya — beneficial to the eyes. The zinc, folate, and polyphenol content contribute to macular protection, and zinc is a critical cofactor in retinal function and Vitamin A metabolism, supporting the traditional Ayurvedic use for eye care.

How to Use Masura

Masura is remarkable in both its internal dietary use and its external therapeutic applications in Ayurveda. As a food, it requires minimal preparation — splitting and dehulling makes red lentils the fastest-cooking legume, requiring no soaking. Externally, the flour is a pillar of classical Ayurvedic lepa (paste) therapy for skin conditions.

Safety & Precautions

Masura is among the safest and most universally tolerated foods in both Ayurvedic and modern dietary frameworks. However, certain classical cautions and modern considerations deserve attention, particularly around preparation methods and specific health conditions.

  • Vistambhi (constipating) effect: Classical texts describe Masura as mildly vistambhi at high intake. Always cook thoroughly with digestive spices (cumin, asafoetida, ginger) and consume with ghee or oil to mitigate dryness and prevent gas or constipation.
  • Uric acid & gout: Lentils contain moderate purines (~127 mg/100g). Those with active gout or elevated uric acid should moderate consumption to ½ cup per day and prioritise well-cooked, de-foamed lentils, which have reduced purine load.
  • Antinutrients: Raw lentils contain phytic acid and lectins that reduce mineral absorption. Soaking (for whole lentils), sprouting, or thorough cooking significantly reduces these — always cook lentils fully; never eat raw.
  • Vata constitution: Pure Vata types or those with severe IBS/bloating should use Masura with care — the Ruksha (dry) guna can aggravate Vata-type constipation and bloating. Adding ghee and cooking with asafoetida is essential.
  • Kidney disease: Those with advanced kidney disease may need to limit lentils due to potassium and phosphorus load. Consult a nephrologist regarding pulse consumption in CKD stages 3–5.
  • Thyroid conditions: Like most legumes, lentils contain goitrogens that may mildly interfere with thyroid hormone synthesis when consumed in very large quantities raw. Cooked lentils in normal dietary quantities pose no concern.
  • Allergic reactions: Rare but documented. Lentil allergy is more common in South Asian and Mediterranean populations. Cross-reactivity with peanut and chickpea is possible — discontinue if any allergic symptoms occur.
  • Topical use: The flour paste (ubtana) is gentle but may cause mild irritation on very sensitive or broken skin. Always patch-test before full facial application.
