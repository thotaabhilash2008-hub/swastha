❧

Called Aragvadha in Sanskrit — "disease killer" — this magnificent deciduous tree graces Indian roadsides and temple gardens with cascading curtains of gold each spring. Known to Charaka as the safest and most reliable of all Virechana (purgative) herbs, its black pulp-filled pods have been prescribed for constipation, fevers, and skin disease for over two millennia — gentle enough for children and the elderly yet potent enough to feature in classical panchakarma protocols.

01

Botanical Profile

Amaltas (Cassia fistula L.) is a medium to large deciduous tree of the Fabaceae family, typically growing 10–20 metres tall with a spreading crown and smooth, grey-brown bark that becomes fissured with age. Native to the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, it is now cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide for both its ornamental beauty and medicinal value.

The leaves are pinnately compound, with 4–8 pairs of ovate-oblong leaflets, each 7–12 cm long and bright green, turning yellow before falling. From April to June — before or alongside new foliage — the tree erupts in spectacular pendulous racemes 30–50 cm long, each densely crowded with bright golden-yellow five-petalled flowers, giving rise to its universal common names: Golden Shower and Indian Laburnum. The fruit is its most famous feature: a long, cylindrical, indehiscent pod 30–60 cm in length and about 2 cm in diameter, dark chocolate-brown to black when ripe, with numerous seeds separated by sweet, dark, sticky pulp — the primary medicinal part.

Phytochemical Composition

The phytochemistry of Cassia fistula is exceptionally rich and well-documented, reflecting centuries of traditional use validated by modern analysis. The fruit pulp — the most medicinally active part — owes its potent but gentle laxative action chiefly to anthraquinone glycosides, particularly rhein, sennoside, and fistulin. The bark and heartwood are rich in tannins and catechins, the flowers in flavonoids, and the leaves in anthraquinones and reducing sugars.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Aragvadha holds a position of singular honour in Ayurvedic therapeutics as the foremost safe purgative — described by Charaka as "mriduvirechaka" (gentle laxative) and specifically prescribed for those too weak for stronger purgation: the elderly, children, pregnant women (cautiously, in small doses), and convalescents. It leads the Aragvadhadi Gana — a classical group of 20 herbs sharing purgative and anti-inflammatory properties — mentioned in Charaka Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam, and Sushruta Samhita.

Classical shloka — "Aragvadho mriduh shlakshno guru sheeto vishapaham, kushthaghno krimighno medo medaghno jwarahara param" — proclaims it gentle, smooth, heavy, cold in potency, antitoxic, antifungal (for skin diseases), anti-parasitic, anti-obesity, and supreme among fever remedies. The fruit pulp features in Aragvadhadi Kashaya, Vilwadi Lehyam, and numerous classical compound formulations for skin disorders and constipation.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has extensively validated the traditional applications of Cassia fistula — confirming its laxative, antimicrobial, antifungal, hepatoprotective, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory activities through in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies across multiple global research centres.

Gentle Laxative & Bowel Regulator
The fruit pulp is Ayurveda's premier safe purgative — suitable for the elderly and children. Anthraquinone glycosides (rhein, fistulin, sennoside) stimulate peristalsis of the large intestine and retain water in the colon, easing evacuation without griping, cramping, or electrolyte loss seen with harsher laxatives.
Skin Disease & Antifungal
Classically categorised as Kusthaghna (destroyer of skin diseases), leaf and bark extracts show strong activity against dermatophytes including Trichophyton and Microsporum. Used in ringworm, eczema, psoriasis, and chronic inflammatory skin conditions both orally and topically via leaf paste.
Antipyretic & Fever Management
Described as jwarahara param — supreme among fever remedies — the fruit pulp's sheeta virya (cold potency) is used in high fevers, especially those of Pitta origin. Alcohol extracts of the fruit demonstrate significant antipyretic activity in animal models, validating its use in typhoid fever in folk medicine.
Hepatoprotective & Liver Support
Multiple studies demonstrate that ethanolic extracts of the root bark significantly protect hepatocytes against CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity — reducing serum AST, ALT, and ALP levels while restoring liver architecture. Emodin and aloe-emodin appear to be the primary hepatoprotective constituents.
Antimicrobial & Anti-infective
Broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity has been confirmed against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Salmonella typhi, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Candida albicans. Oxyanthracene derivatives and gallic acid are the principal active agents. Leaf juice is applied to infected wounds and ulcers across folk traditions.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Methanolic extract of the leaf significantly inhibits carrageenan-induced paw oedema and acetic acid-induced writhing in rodent models — attributed to flavonoids (kaempferol, luteolin) and phytosterols. Classical application: bark decoction for inflammatory joint pain and rheumatic conditions.
Anti-diabetic Potential
Ethanolic leaf and pod extracts show dose-dependent blood glucose reduction in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats, comparable in some studies to glibenclamide. The mechanism appears to involve both insulin secretagogue activity and α-glucosidase inhibition. Human clinical data is still emerging.
Antioxidant & Immunomodulatory
High phenolic and flavonoid content confers strong free-radical scavenging activity across DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays. The flower extract is particularly rich in antioxidants. Immunostimulant activity has been demonstrated in macrophage activation assays, supporting its classical use in debilitating fevers and chronic infections.

How to Consume Amaltas

The fruit pulp is the safest and most commonly used part for internal medicine. Unlike harsh anthraquinone laxatives, the pulp of Amaltas is gentle enough for children and the elderly when used at appropriate doses. External preparations using leaves and bark are widely used in folk medicine without significant safety concerns.

Safety & Precautions

Amaltas fruit pulp has an excellent safety profile for short-term laxative use — regarded by Charaka as the safest purgative available. However, like all stimulant anthraquinone laxatives, it carries specific contraindications and must not be used habitually for long periods without medical supervision.

  • Pregnancy: Use with extreme caution and only under physician supervision. Small doses of fruit pulp are used in classical Ayurveda for pregnancy-related constipation, but stimulant laxative action could theoretically increase uterine contractility — avoid in first trimester.
  • Active diarrhea or dysentery: Strictly contraindicated during acute diarrhea, bloody stool, or intestinal inflammation — anthraquinones will aggravate the condition.
  • Inflammatory bowel disease: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are contraindications for any anthraquinone laxative, including Amaltas fruit pulp.
  • Habitual / long-term laxative use: Daily use beyond 4 weeks may cause laxative dependency, pseudomelanosis coli (harmless mucosal darkening), and electrolyte imbalances — particularly potassium loss — increasing risk in cardiac patients on digoxin or diuretics.
  • Renal conditions: Chronic kidney disease patients should use with caution; anthraquinone metabolites are renally excreted and may accumulate.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate cardiac glycosides (digoxin) and antiarrhythmic drugs by aggravating hypokalaemia. Allow a 2-hour gap with oral medications, as laxative action may reduce absorption.
  • Seeds: The seeds of Cassia fistula are not used therapeutically and may be mildly toxic — do not consume raw seeds. The fruit pulp (not the seeds) is the medicinal part.
  • Kapha constitution & obesity: Guru (heavy) and snigdha (unctuous) properties may aggravate Kapha — use in shorter courses in those with Kapha-dominant constitution or metabolic syndrome.
  • Children's dosage: Suitable for children at reduced doses (1–5 g pulp based on age and weight) but should be supervised — one of the rare Ayurvedic laxatives considered safe for paediatric use.
