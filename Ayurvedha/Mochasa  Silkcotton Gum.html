Known in Sanskrit as Mochasa or Shalmali niryasa — the exudate of the Shalmali tree — this deep reddish-brown gum resin flows naturally from the bark of the majestic Silk Cotton Tree (Bombax ceiba), revered since Vedic times as a tree of sacred power. Classical Ayurveda esteems Mochasa above all as a Grahi (binding, astringent), Shothahara (anti-inflammatory), and Raktastambhana (haemostatic) agent — a remedy of the blood, the bowel, and the bone.

01

Botanical Profile

The Silk Cotton Tree (Bombax ceiba, syn. Salmalia malabarica) is one of the most imposing deciduous trees of the Indian subcontinent — a massive, buttressed giant reaching 25–30 m, with a distinctively spiny grey trunk in youth and smooth grey bark in maturity. The tree is native to tropical Asia and is widely distributed across India from the foothills of the Himalayas to the southern peninsula, as well as in Southeast Asia, tropical Australia, and China.

In winter, the tree sheds all its leaves and erupts into one of nature's most spectacular displays — large, brilliant scarlet or crimson flowers with five waxy petals and hundreds of stamens, blooming on bare branches from January to March. The fruit is a woody capsule filled with silky white cotton — the eponymous "silk cotton" — surrounding the small seeds. Mochasa is the dried gum resin that exudes from incisions or natural cracks in the bark, collected and processed into dark reddish-brown, brittle, glassy chunks used in medicine.

Phytochemical Composition

The gum resin Mochasa has been found to contain a complex of tannins, catechins, gallic acid derivatives, and characteristic anthocyanin pigments (responsible for its red colour), along with polysaccharide gums and various phenolic compounds. The bark and roots of Bombax ceiba additionally yield a rich profile of isoflavones, sesquiterpenes, saponins, and phytosterols — many of which underpin its well-documented anti-inflammatory, wound-healing, and sexual tonic actions.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

The Shalmali tree holds an honoured place across the classical Ayurvedic corpus. Charaka classifies its gum (niryasa) under Sandhaniya (tissue-healing) drugs and employs it prominently in Atisara Chikitsa (treatment of diarrhoea) and Raktapitta Chikitsa (treatment of bleeding disorders). Sushruta places Shalmali in the Shalmali Gana — a group of astringent, haemostatic plants — and uses its bark decoction and gum paste in surgical wound management. Vagbhata echoes its Grahi and Raktastambhana actions in Ashtanga Hridayam.

Bhavaprakasha Nighantu describes Mochasa in the Karpuradi Varga, noting: "Shalmali niryasa kashayo grahi madhura snigdho balya shukralo cha" — the gum is astringent, binding, sweet, unctuous, strengthening, and promoter of shukra (reproductive fluid). Dhanvantari Nighantu and Raja Nighantu similarly praise its use in atisara (IBS, loose motions), pradara (menorrhagia), and shukradosha (spermatorrhea).

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research on Bombax ceiba and its isolated constituents has strongly corroborated Mochasa's classical applications, demonstrating anti-diarrheal, haemostatic, anti-inflammatory, wound-healing, aphrodisiac, and antioxidant activities across in vitro, animal, and limited clinical studies.

Anti-diarrheal & IBS Management
Mochasa is Ayurveda's foremost Grahi drug — binding excessive secretions in the gut and reducing intestinal motility. Tannin-protein complexes coat the gut lining, reducing inflammation and fluid loss. Classical combination with Kutaja bark (Holarrhena) is a gold standard formulation for chronic diarrhoea and IBS.
Haemostasis & Bleeding Disorders
Its Raktastambhana (blood-stopping) action is well-recognised in both Charaka and Sushruta. Used internally in menorrhagia (Raktapradara), haemoptysis, rectal bleeding, and haematuria; externally as a styptic powder applied to wounds. Tannin-mediated vasoconstriction and protein precipitation promote clot formation.
Wound Healing & Tissue Repair
Classified as Sandhaniya (tissue-uniting) by Charaka, Mochasa promotes granulation tissue formation and wound contraction. Bark paste and gum powder are applied to chronic ulcers, burns, and surgical wounds. Gallic acid and ellagic acid components provide concurrent antimicrobial protection.
Male Reproductive Tonic
As a Shukrala (promoter of reproductive fluid), Mochasa is a core ingredient in classical formulations for spermatorrhea, oligospermia, and premature ejaculation. The isoflavone C-glycosides shamimin and isoshamimin demonstrate CNS-modulating and adaptogenic activity, supporting both reproductive and nervous system health.
Gynaecological Disorders
Used prominently in Pradara Chikitsa (management of excessive menstrual flow and leucorrhoea). Cold decoction of Mochasa with Lodhra is a classical prescription for menorrhagia. Its sheeta virya (cold potency) and Pitta-pacifying action reduce the inflammatory component of uterine bleeding.
Joint & Bone Health
Root of Bombax ceiba is a documented folk remedy for osteoarthritis, bone fractures, and joint pain. Its anti-inflammatory phytosterols and ursolic acid reduce synovial inflammation, while its Sandhaniya property supports bone union. Applied externally as a root paste for swollen joints and fractures in tribal medicine across India.
Urinary Tract Health
Mochasa is used in Mutrakricchra (dysuria) and haematuria — blood in the urine — where its combined astringent and anti-inflammatory actions soothe the urinary epithelium and reduce bleeding. A decoction of gum with Gokshura (Tribulus) is a traditional Ayurvedic prescription for these conditions.
Antioxidant & Anti-inflammatory
Catechin, quercetin, luteolin, and gallic acid collectively provide potent free-radical scavenging activity. In vivo studies demonstrate significant inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis and cyclooxygenase activity by bark and gum extracts — validating Mochasa's classical role in Shotha (oedema) and inflammatory conditions.

How to Consume Mochasa

Mochasa is used both internally and externally. The dried gum chunks must first be powdered or dissolved depending on the preparation. Due to its strongly astringent nature and cold potency, internal use is best guided by an Ayurvedic practitioner, particularly for chronic or systemic conditions.

Safety & Precautions

Mochasa is generally considered safe for therapeutic use at recommended classical doses and is not known to contain any hepatotoxic alkaloids. Its main risk profile relates to its strongly astringent and cold nature — excessive use can cause constipation, dry mucous membranes, or Vata aggravation. It is a well-tolerated medicine with a long history of safe clinical use in Ayurveda.

  • Constipation: As a Grahi (binding) drug, Mochasa can cause or worsen constipation if used in excess or without the context of loose motions. Avoid in cases of chronic constipation or dry bowel.
  • Vata aggravation: Its ruksha (dry) and kashaya (astringent) qualities may aggravate Vata with excessive use — watch for dryness, bloating, or joint stiffness; reduce dose if these appear.
  • Pregnancy: Root and bark of Bombax ceiba have uterotonic activity in some animal studies — avoid internal use of these parts in pregnancy. The gum resin (Mochasa) is generally considered safe at low doses but should be used only with practitioner guidance.
  • Iron absorption: High tannin content may reduce dietary iron absorption — avoid taking Mochasa preparations alongside iron supplements or iron-rich meals.
  • Hypotension: High-dose bark extracts show hypotensive activity in animal studies — use cautiously in those on antihypertensive medications.
  • Drug interactions: May reduce absorption of co-administered medications if taken together — take Mochasa preparations at least 1–2 hours apart from pharmaceutical drugs.
  • Quality and adulteration: Mochasa is frequently adulterated in the market with inferior resins or gums — source from verified Ayurvedic pharmacies with standardised products.
  • Duration of use: Not intended for indefinite continuous use; classical recommendation is cyclical treatment as prescribed; extended unsupervised use is not advisable.
