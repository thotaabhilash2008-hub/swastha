One of the five celestial trees of the Hindu mythological Svarga, Mandara adorns Indra's paradise and is celebrated in Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, and Ashtanga Hridayam alike — its vivid scarlet blooms emerging from bare, thorned branches like flames from ancient wood, while its bark, leaves, roots, and flowers quietly serve a vast pharmacopoeia spanning vata-kapha shamana, wound healing, nervous disorders, and gynaecological conditions.

01

Botanical Profile

Mandara (Erythrina variegata / E. indica) is a medium to large deciduous tree of the Fabaceae (Leguminosae) family, native to the tropical and subtropical coasts of the Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific Islands. It typically grows 15–25 m tall, with a wide-spreading crown and a deeply furrowed grey-brown trunk armed with short, conical thorns.

The leaves are large and trifoliate — three broad, rhomboid-ovate leaflets — bright green on the upper surface, glaucous below, and among the most recognisable in Indian traditional plant knowledge. Its flowers are perhaps its most dramatic feature: dense, erect racemes of brilliant scarlet-orange, pea-shaped blossoms appearing between January and March, entirely on bare branches before the leaves flush, giving the tree a spectacular torch-like appearance. The fruit is a long, constricted pod containing bright red seeds. The tree is sacred to Shiva and is a key feature of coastal and riparian landscapes across peninsular India.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

The genus Erythrina is phytochemically one of the richest in the Fabaceae, with over 200 alkaloids isolated — the largest known complement in any plant genus. E. variegata yields a distinctive matrix of isoquinoline alkaloids, flavonoids, pterocarpans, isoflavones, and lectins, varying significantly by plant part. The bark is richest in alkaloids; the seeds and heartwood in flavonoids and lectins.

The erythrina alkaloids (erysotrine, erythraline, erythroidine) are particularly noteworthy — they act as curare-like neuromuscular blocking agents and have formed the basis of research into antiepileptic and anxiolytic pharmacology. The flavonoid fraction — including erythrinin A & B, alpinumisoflavone, and erycristagallin — demonstrates significant antimicrobial, anti-osteoporotic, and estrogenic activity.

Ayurvedic Significance

Mandara holds dual identity in classical Ayurveda — it is simultaneously one of the five divine trees (Panchavruksha) of Indra's paradise and a prominent medicinal plant referenced across all three foundational classical texts. Charaka lists it in Vata-kapha shamana group drugs; Sushruta classifies its bark in Nyagrodhadi gana (wound-healing group) and uses it in Shodana (purification) formulations; Ashtanga Hridayam includes it in management of krimiroga (parasitic/infective conditions) and skin diseases.

The classical shloka describes Mandara as — "Mandarasya phalam tiktam kashayam katu cha smrutam, kaphavatashamakam, vranaharam, krimignam" — bitter, astringent and pungent in taste, pacifying Kapha and Vata, healing wounds, and destroying parasites. Its bark is considered Shothahara (anti-inflammatory), Vedanasthapana (analgesic), and Rasayana (rejuvenative) when processed correctly.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological investigation of Mandara has substantiated many classical applications, validating anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antimicrobial, antifungal, sedative, antiparasitic, and bone-protective activities through rigorous in vitro, in vivo, and increasingly clinical studies of its bark, leaf, seed, and root extracts.

Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Bark and leaf extracts demonstrate significant inhibition of inflammatory mediators — COX-1/2, lipoxygenase, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Classically used as bark paste over inflamed joints and arthritic swellings; animal models confirm dose-dependent reduction in carrageenan-induced paw edema comparable to reference drugs.
Sedative & Neuro-protective
Erythrina alkaloids — particularly β-erythroidine and erysotrine — act on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, producing sedative and anxiolytic effects. Used in traditional medicine for insomnia, anxiety, and epilepsy; bark decoction is a classical remedy for apasmara (epilepsy) and neuralgia across multiple regional traditions.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Erythrinin A & B and erycristagallin show potent activity against MRSA, Candida species, Aspergillus, and dermatophytes. Bark paste is a classical wound dressing used to prevent infection; leaf decoction is applied externally to fungal skin conditions. Notably active against drug-resistant clinical isolates in recent studies.
Bone Health & Anti-osteoporotic
Alpinumisoflavone and related isoflavones exhibit phytoestrogenic and osteoblast-stimulating activity — increasing bone mineral density in ovariectomised animal models and inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Makes Mandara a promising candidate in post-menopausal bone loss management.
Gynaecological & Uterine Support
Root bark decoction is classically used in artava kshaya (menstrual irregularity), dysmenorrhea, and as a galactagogue. The phytoestrogenic isoflavone content provides a plausible biochemical basis. Used across traditional medicine systems of Kerala, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu in female reproductive disorders.
Wound Healing
Sushruta's classification of Mandara in Nyagrodhadi gana — the wound-healing drug group — is substantiated by studies showing accelerated epithelialisation, collagen deposition, and wound contraction with bark extract formulations. Leaf paste on ulcers and bark decoction for wound washing are widely practised ethnomedicinal uses.
Antiparasitic & Anthelmintic
Classical karma of Mandara includes krimighna — destruction of parasites. Hypaphorine and certain alkaloids show antiparasitic activity against helminths and protozoal pathogens. Bark decoction is used in intestinal parasitosis; leaf extract demonstrates inhibition of Leishmania and Plasmodium species in research models.
Hepatoprotective Activity
Ethanolic leaf and bark extracts significantly attenuate paracetamol- and CCl₄-induced liver toxicity in animal models, normalising AST, ALT, and ALP levels. Mechanism involves antioxidant free-radical scavenging, membrane stabilisation, and upregulation of hepatic glutathione — supporting classical use in liver conditions and jaundice.

How to Use Mandara

All parts of Mandara are medicinally active, with the bark being the most widely used in classical formulations. Internal preparations must be strictly physician-directed, as erythrina alkaloids require precise dosing — their therapeutic window is relatively narrow. External applications are generally safer and used extensively in regional folk traditions.

Safety & Precautions

Mandara is a potent medicinal plant whose internal use demands expert supervision. Erythrina alkaloids have a relatively narrow therapeutic index — at excessive doses they produce respiratory paralysis in animal models. Classical Ayurveda employs shodhana (purification) of bark before internal use; self-administration of unpurified preparations is inadvisable.

  • Pregnancy: Strongly contraindicated internally. The plant's uterine-stimulating properties and phytoestrogenic compounds pose significant risk of miscarriage or preterm labour.
  • Neuromuscular conditions: Erythrina alkaloids block nicotinic receptors — do not use alongside neuromuscular blocking agents, anaesthetics, or myasthenia gravis management drugs without specialist review.
  • Respiratory compromise: High doses of alkaloids can depress respiratory drive. Use with caution in any patient with respiratory insufficiency.
  • Seeds — high toxicity: The bright red seeds are highly toxic and should never be consumed. Keep away from children. Seed lectins are potently haemagglutinating.
  • Pitta-dominant constitutions: Avoid in active bleeding disorders, hyperacidity, inflammatory Pitta conditions, and peptic ulcers — the plant's ushna virya aggravates Pitta significantly.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate CNS depressants, sedatives, benzodiazepines, and anti-epileptic medications. Avoid concurrent use without medical supervision.
  • Shodhana requirement: Classical texts mandate purification (heating in cow's milk or ghee) of bark before internal use to reduce alkaloid toxicity. Raw, unpurified internal preparations are unsafe.
  • Topical precaution: Bark sap contact with eyes causes irritation. Prolonged topical application on broken skin may cause sensitisation in some individuals.
  • Duration: Limit supervised internal use to 4–6 weeks at a time. Liver function monitoring is advisable with extended use.
