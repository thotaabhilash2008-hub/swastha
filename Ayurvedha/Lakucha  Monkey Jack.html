Lakucha
Monkey Jack — The Sour Wild Breadfruit
Artocarpus lakoocha Roxb.  ·  Artocarpus lacucha Buch.-Ham.

Fig. 1 — Globose syncarpous fruits of Lakucha with rough, tuberculate yellow-green surface  ·  Fig. 2 — Broad-canopied Lakucha tree bearing heavy fruits amid its large, leathery leaves

❧

Known in Sanskrit as Lakucha — and playfully called Monkey Jack in English — this wild relative of the jackfruit has held an intimate place in Ayurvedic practice for over two millennia. Its intensely sour flesh, antipathy to Kapha, and generous yield of bioactive oxyresveratrol make it one of the most pharmacologically documented fruits of the Moraceae family, revered equally in classical texts and modern phytochemistry.

01

Botanical Profile

Lakucha (Artocarpus lakoocha) is a large, evergreen or semi-deciduous tree of the Moraceae (mulberry) family, closely allied to jackfruit (A. heterophyllus) and breadfruit (A. altilis). Native to the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, it grows naturally in the moist deciduous and semi-evergreen forests of the Himalayan foothills, Bangladesh, Thailand, and the Malay Peninsula, at altitudes up to 1,000 m.

The tree can reach 15–20 m in height with a spreading canopy, dense with large, alternate, broadly elliptic leaves (20–45 cm long) that are rough-textured above and softly hairy beneath. Both male and female flowers are borne in separate catkin-like heads on the same tree. The fruit — botanically a syncarpium (composite fruit) — is globose to slightly flattened, 7–13 cm across, with a rough, tuberculate, greenish-yellow surface when ripe. Its flesh is cream-coloured, coarse-fibred, pleasantly sour to acidic in flavour, and encloses 1–8 seeds enclosed in a papery testa. The heartwood of the trunk is a prized bright yellow, used historically as a natural dye.

Phytochemistry

Lakucha is among the richest plant sources of oxyresveratrol — a hydroxylated analogue of resveratrol — found at extraordinarily high concentrations in the heartwood and bark. This single compound has attracted substantial research interest for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-melanogenic, neuroprotective, and anti-diabetic properties. Beyond oxyresveratrol, the plant yields a constellation of stilbenoids, flavonoids, prenylated compounds, and triterpenoids that together underpin its wide pharmacological profile.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Lakucha is mentioned prominently in Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridaya, and Bhavaprakasha Nighantu — most frequently in the context of digestive disorders, skin diseases, and Kapha–Pitta conditions. Bhavaprakasha classifies it under Amradi Phala Varga (sour fruit group). Charaka commends its heartwood decoction in Krimighna (anti-parasitic) formulations. Sushruta employs it in the cleansing of wounds and skin ulcers. It is an ingredient in Lakuchadyarishta, a classical fermented preparation for splenic disorders and abdominal tumours.

The classical shloka in Bhavaprakasha notes: "Lakucho grahi kashayamlo vatapittakaphapradam, rucyah kaphanilahara kriminam cha vinashanam" — describing it as astringent-sour in taste, grahi (absorbing/drying), appetite-enhancing, and destroying Kapha, Vata and worms.

04

Key Health Benefits

Lakucha has attracted growing scientific attention, particularly for its heartwood-derived oxyresveratrol — one of the most potent natural tyrosinase inhibitors known, rivalling synthetic skin-lightening agents. Multiple in vitro and animal studies have validated classical Ayurvedic applications, from anti-parasitic and anti-obesity effects to wound healing and hepatoprotection.

Anti-melanogenic & Skin Brightening
Oxyresveratrol from Lakucha heartwood is among the most potent natural tyrosinase inhibitors discovered — significantly surpassing kojic acid and arbutin in enzyme inhibition assays. Widely used in Thai and Southeast Asian cosmeceuticals for hyperpigmentation, melasma, and skin brightening without the cytotoxicity of synthetic agents.
Anti-obesity & Lipase Inhibition
Classical Ayurveda prescribes Lakucha for Sthoulya (obesity) — a claim validated by studies showing significant pancreatic lipase inhibition by lakoochin A and B, analogous to orlistat. The heartwood extract reduces dietary fat absorption by preventing triglyceride breakdown in the gut, making it a candidate for anti-obesity formulations.
Antioxidant & Anti-inflammatory
Heartwood and bark extracts consistently demonstrate strong DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging — substantially exceeding synthetic standards at comparable concentrations. Anti-inflammatory activity is documented via COX-2 inhibition, IL-6 suppression, and NF-κB pathway modulation, supporting use in chronic inflammatory conditions.
Anti-parasitic & Antimicrobial
Charaka's designation of Lakucha as Krimighna (anti-worm) is supported by demonstrated activity against intestinal helminths and protozoa. Bark and heartwood extracts inhibit E. coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, and several dermatophytes — explaining its classical use in skin infections and gastrointestinal parasites.
Digestive & Splenic Support
Lakucha fruit is deepaniya (appetite-stimulating) and grahi (gut-absorbing), used classically in loss of appetite, dyspepsia, and sluggish digestion. Lakuchadyarishta — the classical fermented preparation — is specifically indicated for Pleeha roga (splenic disorders), abdominal distension, and Gulma (abdominal tumour-like masses).
Neuroprotective Potential
Oxyresveratrol has demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects in models of ischaemia-reperfusion injury, Parkinson's, and Alzheimer's pathology — crossing the blood-brain barrier more readily than resveratrol. It inhibits MAO-B, reduces amyloid-beta aggregation, and attenuates oxidative neuronal damage in cell and animal models.
Anti-diabetic Effects
Bark and fruit extracts show significant alpha-glucosidase and alpha-amylase inhibition — slowing post-prandial glucose absorption — alongside insulin-sensitising effects in streptozotocin-induced diabetic models. Oxyresveratrol activates AMPK pathways, promoting glucose uptake in skeletal muscle analogous to metformin's mechanism.
Wound Healing & Hepatoprotection
Sushruta recommends Lakucha bark decoction for wound irrigation and ulcer cleansing. Modern studies confirm accelerated wound contraction and epithelialisation with topical bark extract in excision wound models. Lupeol and triterpenoids from the heartwood provide significant hepatoprotective activity, reducing CCl₄-induced liver damage markers in animal studies.
05

Traditional & Culinary Uses

Beyond medicine, Lakucha occupies a meaningful culinary place across Eastern India, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Southeast Asia. The sour, fibrous flesh of ripe fruits is consumed fresh, pickled, made into chutneys, or sun-dried for year-round use. In Bengal, raw Lakucha is a treasured ingredient in fish curries — its acidity functioning like tamarind or raw mango. Young leaves are cooked as a pot herb. Seeds are boiled or roasted and eaten in times of scarcity or as a minor food source.

The heartwood's brilliant yellow-orange pigment — primarily oxyresveratrol — has historically been used as a natural dye for silk and cotton, imparting warm golden tones prized in Buddhist robes across Thailand and Myanmar. The wood itself is hard, durable, and moderately heavy, used in furniture, agricultural implements, and construction in forest communities.

How to Use Lakucha

Different parts of the Lakucha tree are used for different therapeutic purposes — heartwood preparations predominate in modern phytotherapy due to their exceptional oxyresveratrol content, while the fruit and bark are the primary classical medicinal parts used in Ayurvedic practice. All internal therapeutic preparations should be taken under a qualified practitioner's supervision.

Safety & Precautions

Lakucha has a long history of safe culinary and medicinal use at moderate doses. Its heartwood preparations are generally well-tolerated in short-term therapeutic applications; however, the high concentration of bioactive stilbenoids warrants caution in specific populations and with prolonged use. The fruit's high acidity is the primary concern in dietary use.

  • Pitta constitution & hyperacidity: The fruit's intensely sour-hot nature can aggravate Pitta. Those with hyperacidity, GERD, peptic ulcers, or gastritis should avoid or strictly limit fruit consumption.
  • Pregnancy: Heartwood and bark preparations in therapeutic doses are not recommended during pregnancy due to insufficient safety data and potential uterine effects.
  • Bleeding disorders: Stilbenoids including resveratrol have anti-platelet activity. Those on anticoagulants (warfarin, aspirin) or with bleeding disorders should exercise caution and consult a physician.
  • Hypoglycaemia risk: The documented alpha-glucosidase inhibition and blood sugar-lowering effects mean individuals on anti-diabetic medications must monitor glucose levels carefully to avoid hypoglycaemic episodes.
  • Latex sensitivity: The milky latex of the bark and stem may cause contact dermatitis in latex-sensitive individuals — wear gloves when handling cut bark or unripe fruit.
  • Oxyresveratrol at high doses: Extremely high doses of isolated oxyresveratrol (not typical dietary exposure) have shown cytotoxic effects in vitro. Standardised extracts should be used only at recommended concentrations.
  • Drug interactions: As a CYP enzyme modulator, oxyresveratrol may interact with medications metabolised by CYP2C9 and CYP3A4. Consult a physician if on multiple medications.
  • Children: Therapeutic (non-culinary) use of heartwood or bark preparations in young children should only be undertaken under specialist supervision.
