Sala
The Sal Tree — Sacred Forest Giant of the Himalayas
Shorea robusta Gaertn. f.  ·  Dipterocarpaceae

Fig. 1 — The towering forest canopy of Shorea robusta — one of India's most dominant and sacred timber trees  ·  Fig. 2 — Sal flowers and the precious oleoresin tapped from bark fissures, the tree's primary medicinal product

🌿

Towering above the forests of the Himalayan foothills and the Gangetic plains, Shorea robusta — the Sal tree — is simultaneously one of India's most economically vital timber trees, a foundational Ayurvedic remedy, and one of the subcontinent's most sacred botanical presences. It was beneath a Sal grove at Kushinagar that the Buddha attained Mahaparinirvana; its resin has sealed wounds, purified spaces, and strengthened bodies for three thousand years of recorded history.

01

Botanical Profile

Shorea robusta Gaertn. f. is a large, semi-evergreen to deciduous tree of the Dipterocarpaceae family — the dipterocarp family that dominates the humid tropical forests of South and Southeast Asia. In India it is the dominant canopy tree of the Sal forests stretching across the Himalayan foothills from Assam to the Punjab, and south through Jharkhand, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh — one of the most ecologically and economically important forest types in South Asia.

Sal trees are massive: mature specimens reach 30–45 m in height with trunk diameters exceeding 2 m, and lifespans of several hundred years. The bark is thick, rough, longitudinally fissured, and grey-brown — oozing a characteristic pale yellow oleoresin (Sal dhoop / Ral) when wounded. Leaves are large (10–25 cm), ovate-oblong, prominently veined, and leathery — briefly deciduous in March-April, rapidly replaced by copper-tinged new flushes. The small, pale-yellow, fragrant flowers appear in March–May in large panicles and produce distinctive winged fruits (samaras) with 2–5 papery wings for wind dispersal.

Every part of the Sal tree is medicinally or economically useful: the oleoresin (Shala Niryasa) is the primary Ayurvedic medicinal product; the seeds yield a high-quality edible fat (Sal fat / Mowrah butter); the leaves are universally used as food plates across eastern and central India; the bark yields tannins; and the timber — hard, durable, termite-resistant — is among India's most prized structural woods.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

The oleoresin of Shorea robusta is the most pharmacologically significant product of the Sal tree and has been most thoroughly investigated. It is a complex mixture of diterpene acids, triterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and phenolic compounds. The bark yields ellagitannins and catechins; the seed fat is rich in stearic and oleic acids with a composition similar to cocoa butter — used as a cocoa butter substitute in the food industry; the leaves contain flavonoids and phenolic acids.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Sala (Shorea robusta) is prominently referenced in all major classical Ayurvedic texts. Charaka Samhita places it among the Kashaya Skandha (astringent group) and uses it in formulations for Atisara (diarrhea), Prameha (urinary/diabetic disorders), and wound management. Sushruta Samhita lists the oleoresin (Shala Niryasa or Sarjanishkranda) as a primary ingredient in Ropana (wound-healing) preparations and Vrana Chikitsa (ulcer treatment). The Ashtanga Hridayam uses it in ear, nose, and throat formulations.

The Bhavaprakasha Nighantu classifies the tree under Karpuradi Varga (resinous substances group), noting: "Sarja kashayo rooksha kaphapittaharah param, vrana shotha visarpashu jeernajware hitah" — Sarja (Sal resin) is astringent, dry, supreme in Kapha-Pitta pacification, beneficial in wounds, oedema, erysipelas, and chronic fever. The Dhanvantari Nighantu and Raja Nighantu both extensively list its applications, and its resin is a component of the classical preparation Sarjarasa Taila.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research on Shorea robusta's oleoresin, bark, and seed fat validates its classical applications across wound healing, antimicrobial activity, anti-inflammatory action, and urinary-metabolic disorders. The tree's diverse parts offer distinct but complementary therapeutic profiles.

Wound Healing & Anti-ulcer
Shala Niryasa (Sal resin) is Ayurveda's premier topical wound-sealing agent — applied to cuts, ulcers, fissures, and surgical wounds. Diterpene resin acids create a protective barrier while ellagitannins and catechins from bark decoction accelerate granulation tissue formation. Studies confirm significant reduction in wound area, increased collagen synthesis, and faster epithelialisation compared to controls.
Anti-diarrheal & Gut Astringent
The bark's high tannin content — ellagic acid, gallic acid, and catechins — is strongly astringent, reducing intestinal secretion, tightening mucosal permeability, and inhibiting gut pathogens. Classical preparations using Sal bark decoction for Atisara (diarrhea), dysentery, and irritable bowel are validated by pharmacological studies showing dose-dependent inhibition of castor-oil and charcoal-induced diarrhea in rodent models.
Anti-diabetic & Pramehaghna
Classical description of Sala as Pramehaghna (urinary disorder-destroying) encompasses its use in diabetes. Bark and leaf extracts demonstrate significant alpha-glucosidase and alpha-amylase inhibitory activity. Quercetin and kaempferol from the leaves improve insulin sensitivity; catechins reduce oxidative stress in pancreatic tissue. Animal studies show meaningful reduction in fasting blood glucose and HbA1c-equivalent markers.
Antimicrobial & Anti-fungal
Sal resin and bark extracts demonstrate broad-spectrum activity against Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA), Streptococcus, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Candida albicans. The diterpene resin acids disrupt microbial membranes while tannins denature surface proteins. Used classically as a fumigant (dhoop) — burning the resin — for environmental antimicrobial purification of spaces.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
α- and β-amyrins and lupeol from bark and resin demonstrate significant COX inhibition and pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression in carrageenan and formalin-induced inflammation models. Used in classical Ayurveda for inflammatory joint conditions, oedema (shotha), and erysipelas (visarpa). The resin is applied as a poultice for arthritic joints and swellings in Adivasi folk traditions across Jharkhand and Odisha.
Ear, Nose & Throat Remedy
Sal resin preparations are used in classical Ayurveda for chronic ear discharge (Karnasrava), sinusitis, and nasal polyps — included in ENT formulations of Ashtanga Hridayam. Antimicrobial and drying (rooksha) properties address chronic otitis media and mucosal congestion. The smoke of burning resin (dhoomapana) is used for throat and respiratory conditions in classical fumigation therapy.
Skin Emollient & Cosmetic
Sal seed fat — with its cocoa butter-like fatty acid profile (stearic 56%, oleic 35%) — is a premium skin emollient, forming an occlusive barrier that retains moisture and soothes dry, chapped, and inflamed skin. It is used in classical Lepa (topical paste) preparations for skin disorders, as a base for medicated ointments, and is now commercially used in natural cosmetics and chocolate confectionery.
Haemostatic & Anti-bleeding
Sal bark and resin's powerful astringency gives it documented haemostatic (bleeding-stopping) properties — applied topically to wounds, bleeding piles, and gum disease. Classical texts describe its use in Raktapitta (bleeding disorders) and Arsha (haemorrhoids). Tannin-mediated vasoconstriction and protein precipitation physically seal bleeding surfaces.
05

Ecological & Cultural Heritage

Shorea robusta is far more than a medicinal tree — it is a cultural, ecological, and spiritual keystone of northern and eastern India. Sal forests cover approximately 11.6 million hectares across India and are among the most biodiverse and ecologically productive forest types in the subcontinent, supporting tiger reserves, elephant corridors, and hundreds of endemic species.

How to Use Sala

The different parts of the Sal tree yield distinct preparations for distinct purposes. The oleoresin is the primary Ayurvedic medicinal product — used both topically and internally in carefully prepared formulations. Bark decoctions are used internally for diarrhea and urinary disorders. Seed fat is used both internally as a food fat and externally as a skin emollient.

Safety & Precautions

Sala has an excellent safety record across centuries of Ayurvedic and folk use. The bark and seed fat are among the safest parts. The oleoresin, while highly effective topically, requires careful internal dosing under supervision — unpurified resin in high doses may cause gastric irritation. Resin-based preparations should use properly processed (shodhita) Shala Niryasa for internal use.

  • Internal oleoresin use: Crude, unpurified Sal resin should not be consumed internally — only classically processed (shodhita) preparations under a qualified Ayurvedic physician's supervision. Topical use of purified resin is safe for most individuals.
  • Vata constitution / Constipation: The rooksha (dry) and kashaya (astringent) properties of the bark may aggravate Vata and worsen constipation in dry-constitution individuals. Use with ghee or sesame oil as anupana if Vata aggravation is a concern.
  • Resin allergy: Individuals sensitive to other tree resins (pine, frankincense, myrrh) may occasionally show cross-sensitivity to Sal resin — perform a small patch test before topical application over large skin areas.
  • Pregnancy: Moderate use of bark decoction and seed fat is generally considered safe. Internal oleoresin preparations during pregnancy should be avoided without specialist supervision.
  • Dhoop fumigation: Burning Sal resin in closed, poorly ventilated spaces may cause respiratory irritation in sensitive individuals, those with asthma, or young children — ensure adequate ventilation during incense use.
  • Drug interactions: Tannin-rich bark preparations may reduce the absorption of iron, zinc, and certain medications if taken simultaneously — maintain a 2-hour separation from other medications and iron supplements.
  • Sustainable sourcing: While not endangered, Sal forests are under significant pressure from deforestation and over-exploitation. Prefer sustainably harvested or cultivated sources; support forest conservation initiatives when using Sal products commercially.
