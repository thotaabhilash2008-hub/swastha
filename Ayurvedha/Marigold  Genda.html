From temple garlands and festival colours to classical wound-healing preparations and modern cosmeceuticals, Genda — the marigold — stands as one of the most culturally embedded and medicinally versatile flowers of India. Equally revered in Ayurveda, Unani, and folk traditions, its vivid orange petals carry potent lutein, quercetin, and carotenoid compounds that science has only recently begun to quantify.

01

Botanical Profile

Genda (Tagetes erecta L.), commonly called African or Mexican Marigold, is an erect, branching annual herb of the Asteraceae (Compositae) family, native to Mexico and Central America but naturalised across the Indian subcontinent for centuries. It grows 30–100 cm tall with pinnately divided, strongly aromatic leaves and large, terminal, double or semi-double flower heads of brilliant yellow, orange, or deep amber. A closely related species, Tagetes patula (French Marigold), is smaller and widely used in companion planting and folk medicine. Calendula officinalis, though unrelated taxonomically, shares the vernacular "marigold" name and many healing properties.

Marigolds flourish in well-drained soil under full sun and are among the most cultivated ornamental and commercial flowers in India. The flowers contain a highly concentrated source of xanthophyll carotenoids — particularly lutein — making them one of the most commercially important natural pigment sources for the poultry, food, and pharmaceutical industries. They bloom through most of the year in tropical India with peak harvest from October to March.

Phytochemical Composition

Marigold flowers are one of the richest known plant sources of carotenoid pigments — particularly lutein and its ester forms — which constitute up to 80–90% of the total xanthophylls in the petals. Beyond pigments, the plant yields a remarkable array of flavonoids (quercetin, isorhamnetin, patuletin), thiophene derivatives unique to the genus Tagetes, terpenoids, phenolic acids, and an aromatic essential oil dominated by piperitone, ocimene, and tagetone.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Though Tagetes erecta is of New World origin, it was assimilated into Indian ethnobotany and Ayurvedic folk practice with remarkable speed after the 16th–17th century arrival, recognised for its resemblance in action to indigenous Asteraceae members. Classical Ayurvedic texts reference Sthulapushpa and Hemapushpa — "the large golden flower" — with properties consistent with Tagetes. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu and later regional formularies document its use in skin diseases, eye disorders, and wound healing.

In Ayurvedic pharmacology, Genda's bitter-pungent taste, hot potency, and light-dry qualities make it a classic Kapha-Vata pacifier. Its anti-inflammatory and wound-healing actions align with Vrana Shotha Hara (reducing inflammation) and Krimighna (antimicrobial) categories. Externally, fresh petal paste is applied to skin ulcers, wounds, and inflammatory swellings; internally, flower decoctions address fevers, digestive disturbances, and eye conditions.

04

Key Health Benefits

Marigold's pharmacological profile is one of the most extensively studied among Indian medicinal flowers. Modern research validates its traditional use across dermatology, ophthalmology, oncology, antimicrobial therapy, and anti-inflammatory applications.

Eye Health & Macular Protection
Lutein and zeaxanthin — concentrated in the macula of the human eye — filter blue light and neutralise free radicals. Multiple clinical studies confirm that lutein supplementation (largely derived from marigold extract) reduces risk of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and cataracts. This is now the primary global commercial use of T. erecta flowers.
Wound Healing & Dermatology
Marigold petal extract accelerates wound closure through enhanced fibroblast migration, collagen synthesis, and re-epithelialisation. Calendula-based creams (closely related species) are clinically proven for dermatitis, eczema, and post-radiation skin reactions. Fresh Genda petal paste is a time-honoured folk remedy for ulcers, burns, and fungal infections.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Quercetin and isorhamnetin inhibit COX-1, COX-2, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α). Ethanolic flower extracts demonstrate anti-inflammatory activity comparable to indomethacin in carrageenan-induced paw edema models. Leaf poultice is applied to swollen joints in folk practice across Rajasthan, UP, and Bengal.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Essential oil and thiophene derivatives show broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans. α-Terthienyl is uniquely phototoxic — activated by UV light to produce singlet oxygen that disrupts microbial membranes, explaining traditional use of flower paste on sun-exposed wounds.
Antioxidant & Skin Brightening
High carotenoid and flavonoid content confers significant DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP antioxidant activity. Topical application reduces melanin overproduction and photo-oxidative damage. Widely used in traditional ubtan (herbal face packs) across India for brightening dull skin and fading post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.
Anticancer Potential
Oleanolic acid, ursolic acid, and quercetin from marigold extracts induce apoptosis and inhibit proliferation in breast, colon, and cervical cancer cell lines in vitro. Lutein's inverse association with lung cancer risk is documented epidemiologically. α-Terthienyl shows phototoxic cytotoxicity against certain tumour cells.
Digestive & Hepatoprotective
Flower decoction is used in folk medicine for gastric ulcers, dyspepsia, and intestinal cramps. Chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid protect hepatocytes from oxidative stress. Roots and whole plant extracts show hepatoprotective activity against CCl₄-induced liver toxicity in animal models, supporting traditional use in jaundice.
Insect Repellent & Nematocide
The essential oil's high tagetone and piperitone content provides effective repellency against mosquitoes (Aedes aegypti), houseflies, and stored-grain pests. Root-secreted thiophenes are potent nematocides — the basis for its wide use as a companion plant in vegetable gardens to suppress root-knot nematode infestations.
05

Cultural & Ritual Significance

No flower is more quintessentially Indian in its cultural omnipresence than Genda. From Navratri, Diwali, and Makar Sankranti to weddings, funerals, and daily temple puja, the marigold garland — genda ki mala — is the universal offering across Hindu, Jain, and Sikh traditions. Its brilliant colour symbolises the sun, auspiciousness, and the divine — worn around the necks of deities and honoured guests alike.

In Bengal, Zendu flowers are inseparable from Durga Puja, forming the iconic yellow-orange borders of clay idols. In Rajasthan and UP, the flower is central to seasonal markets where farmers bring truckloads for festival demand. Marigold petal water (Genda jal) is used in ritual sprinkling and is believed to purify spaces. In Mexican Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos) traditions — reflecting its pre-Columbian Aztec sacred use — marigolds mark the path for returning souls, connecting its ritual significance across two hemispheres.

How to Use Genda

Genda is primarily used externally in skin and wound care, or internally as a mild therapeutic flower tea or decoction. The flowers are also consumed as food colouring and as a food supplement (lutein extract). Use should be supervised for systemic internal applications, especially in pregnant women and those on blood thinners.

Safety & Precautions

Marigold (Tagetes) is generally considered safe for topical and short-term internal use at therapeutic doses. However, it belongs to the Asteraceae family — the same family as ragweed, chrysanthemum, and chamomile — and cross-reactive allergies are well documented. Unlike Adhapushpi, Genda does not contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids and has a favourable safety profile overall.

  • Asteraceae allergy: Individuals allergic to ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum, or Echinacea may experience contact dermatitis or systemic allergic reactions with Tagetes. Always patch-test before topical use.
  • Pregnancy: Internal use of Tagetes erecta should be avoided during pregnancy — the plant has historically been used as an emmenagogue (to stimulate menstruation) and may stimulate uterine contractions. Topical use on unbroken skin is generally considered safe in moderation.
  • Blood thinners: Quercetin and flavonoids in marigold may potentiate the effects of anticoagulant drugs (warfarin, aspirin). Monitor clotting parameters if consuming flower tea alongside these medications.
  • Photosensitivity: α-Terthienyl in the essential oil is photoactivated — avoid applying concentrated essential oil to sun-exposed skin, as it may cause phototoxic burns or hyperpigmentation.
  • Diabetes medication: Marigold extracts have mild hypoglycemic activity. Monitor blood glucose if combining with insulin or oral antidiabetic drugs to avoid hypoglycemia.
  • Eye wash caution: Only use very dilute, properly filtered, and sterile preparations for eye application. Never apply concentrated decoctions or petal juice directly to eyes — risk of irritation and infection.
  • Children: Topical applications are generally well tolerated; avoid internal use without specialist guidance in children under 6 years.
  • Ingestion of essential oil: Neat Tagetes essential oil is NOT for internal use — it contains concentrated tagetone, piperitone, and phototoxic thiophenes that are toxic at small doses.
