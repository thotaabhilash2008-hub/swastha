Patala
Trumpet Flower — The Hollow-Stemmed Climber
Stereospermum suaveolens  ·  Bignonia suaveolens

Fig. 1 — Fragrant pale-pink trumpet flowers of Patala in pendulous clusters  ·  Fig. 2 — Patala tree with its pinnate leaves and furrowed bark in riparian habitat

❧

Revered as one of the ten roots of the Dashamula — Ayurveda's most celebrated group formulation — Patala (Stereospermum suaveolens) is a fragrant flowering tree whose pale-pink trumpet blooms belie extraordinary medicinal depth. Honoured in the Charaka Samhita across multiple therapeutic categories, Patala is a Tridosha-balancing rasayana with particular affinity for the respiratory tract, liver, and nervous system.

01

Botanical Profile

Patala (Stereospermum suaveolens DC., syn. Bignonia suaveolens Roxb.) is a medium to large deciduous tree of the Bignoniaceae family — the trumpet vine family — growing to 15–25 m tall with a spreading crown and deeply furrowed greyish-brown bark. It is distributed throughout the tropical and subtropical regions of the Indian subcontinent, from the Himalayan foothills down to Sri Lanka and eastward into Myanmar.

The leaves are imparipinnate (odd-pinnate), 30–40 cm long, bearing 4–5 pairs of ovate leaflets with acuminate tips. Its flowers are the plant's most spectacular feature — large, fragrant, pale pink to purplish-pink trumpets 5–7 cm long, borne in terminal panicles, with a yellow-streaked throat and crinkled corolla lobes. The fruit is a long, slender, cylindrical capsule (30–100 cm), resembling a drumstick, containing winged seeds. It flowers between January and April, the sweet fragrance carrying considerable distance — giving rise to the specific epithet suaveolens ("sweetly scented").

Phytochemistry & Key Compounds

Phytochemical investigations of Stereospermum suaveolens have identified a rich array of naphthoquinones, iridoids, flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, and stilbenes — a profile that underlies its broad pharmacological actions. The root bark is the most medicinally concentrated part, yielding the signature lapachol and related naphthoquinones that explain many of its anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and antitumour properties.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Patala holds an exceptionally prominent position in classical Ayurveda — listed by Charaka in no fewer than five therapeutic ganas (groups): Shyamadi, Angamardaprashamana (body-ache relieving), Hikkanigraha (hiccup-suppressing), Asthapanopaga (enema-supporting), and Tiktaskandha (bitter group). Most significantly, it is one of the ten roots of the famous Dashamula compound — specifically belonging to the Laghu Panchamula (lesser five roots) group alongside Shalaparni, Prishnaparni, Brihati, and Kantakari.

The classical verse from Ashtanga Hridayam describes Patala as: "Paatalam tiktam kashayam madhuramcha kapha-pitta-vaataharam — svadupakam laghu sheetam" — bitter, astringent, and sweet in taste; pacifying all three doshas; sweet in post-digestive effect; light and cool in quality — a remarkably tridoshic profile rare among herbs.

04

Key Health Benefits

Patala's tridoshic nature and rich phytochemical profile translate into a broad therapeutic range — from respiratory and hepatic conditions to pain management, skin disease, and neurological support.

Dashamula & Tridosha Rasayana
As a constituent of Dashamula — the ten-root formulation central to Ayurvedic practice — Patala contributes tridosha-balancing, rasayana, and vata-anulomana (Vata normalising) properties. Dashamula is the premier post-partum rejuvenative, used to restore depleted Vata after delivery, and is widely prescribed in chronic fever, pain, and systemic weakness.
Respiratory Support
Classical listing in Hikkanigraha (hiccup-suppressing) gana reflects Patala's ability to pacify upward-moving Vata and Kapha in the respiratory tract. Root bark decoction is used in chronic cough, bronchitis, asthma, and dyspnoea. Its bitter-cold properties reduce inflammatory Pitta in the respiratory mucosa while its Kapha-cutting action clears accumulated phlegm.
Hepatoprotective & Liver Tonic
Stereospermoside, oleanolic acid, and lapachol all demonstrate significant hepatoprotective activity in carbon tetrachloride and paracetamol-induced liver injury models — reducing ALT, AST, and bilirubin to near-normal levels. Classical use in hepatomegaly (yakrit vriddhi), jaundice (kamala), and liver inflammation (yakrit shotha) is well-supported pharmacologically.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Patala root bark extracts show significant inhibition of carrageenan-induced paw oedema in animal models. Its listing in Charaka's Angamardaprashamana gana (body-ache relieving drugs) — alongside drugs like Bala and Ashwagandha — underlines its analgesic utility in generalised myalgia, arthralgia, and rheumatic conditions.
Antipyretic & Anti-infective
Lapachol and tecomaquinone give Patala potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity — demonstrated against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Candida albicans, and malarial Plasmodium species. The root bark decoction is a classical remedy for chronic and intermittent fevers (Vishamajwara) and is used in Dashamula Kwatha for systemic febrile states.
Skin Diseases & Wound Healing
Charaka lists Patala in formulations for skin disorders (kushtha). Flower paste and leaf decoctions are used topically in chronic skin inflammation, eczema, and wounds. Its antioxidant and antimicrobial phenolics (caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid) accelerate wound healing while preventing microbial colonisation.
Oedema & Diuretic Action
Classical listing in Asthapanopaga (enema-adjunct drugs) and Shyamadi gana reflects Patala's role in managing fluid accumulation. Its diuretic and oedema-reducing properties are used in ascites (jalodara), anasarca, and post-inflammatory tissue swelling — particularly where Kapha and Pitta doshas are implicated.
Neuroprotective & Adaptogenic
Baicalein and caffeic acid derivatives isolated from Patala demonstrate neuroprotective effects in oxidative stress models — inhibiting lipid peroxidation in neural tissue. In Dashamula preparations, Patala contributes to the group's documented anti-stress, neuro-restorative, and adaptogenic activity used in convalescence and neurological fatigue.

How to Consume Patala

The root bark is the primary medicinal part and is most commonly used as a decoction (kwatha) or as part of classical compound preparations — most notably Dashamula Kwatha, Dashamula Taila, and Dashamula Ghrita. Patala is rarely used as a standalone herb in modern practice; its greatest therapeutic expression is within these classical compounds.

Safety & Precautions

Patala is considered a generally safe and well-tolerated herb with a long classical history of use. Its sheeta (cold) virya and tridoshic action mean it is broadly suitable across constitutions — though its bitter and drying qualities require caution in certain states. As part of Dashamula and other compound formulations, it has been used safely for centuries at classical doses.

  • Pregnancy (first trimester): Though Dashamula is a classical post-partum remedy, avoid unsupervised use in the first trimester — some constituents may stimulate uterine activity. Use only under qualified Ayurvedic supervision during pregnancy.
  • Severe Vata depletion: Its ruksha (dry) and laghu (light) gunas may further deplete an already extremely Vata-depleted (emaciated, very dry) constitution; in such cases use only in ghee-based preparations that counterbalance dryness.
  • Lapachol content: High-dose or prolonged use of the isolated root bark (not as classical compound) may carry the anti-coagulant effects of lapachol — use caution with anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications.
  • Hypoglycaemic synergy: Patala's mild blood-glucose-lowering activity may add to the effect of antidiabetic medications — monitor glucose levels if combining.
  • Quality & substitution: Taxonomic confusion in the market is significant — Stereospermum chelonoides is often sold interchangeably; ensure authenticated root bark from a reliable source. Classical texts also reference Patala as the flower, not always the root — clarify the plant part being used.
  • Children: Can be used in classical paediatric formulations (e.g., Dashamularishtam in small doses) but always under a qualified practitioner's guidance.
  • Excessive cold conditions: As a sheeta (cold) herb, Patala should be used carefully in individuals with predominant cold symptoms — profound cold intolerance, hypothyroid tendency — or combined with warming anupanas.
