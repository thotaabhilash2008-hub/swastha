Kusha
Darbha — The Sacred Grass of the Vedas
Desmostachya bipinnata  ·  Poa cynosuroides

Fig. 1 — Kusha grass, the primordial seat of sages and sacrifice  ·  Fig. 2 — Desmostachya bipinnata in its natural riparian habitat

❧

Among all grasses that clothe the earth, Kusha alone was chosen by Vishnu as his seat in the churning of the cosmic ocean, by Brahma to seat the gods, and by the sages of every age as the sacred ground of tapasya. Yet beyond its profound ritual sanctity, Kusha is a potent Ayurvedic herb — described by Charaka among the Trnapanchamula, by Sushruta among the Mutravirechaniya, and by Bhavaprakasha as the supreme mutral (diuretic) and trishnashaman (thirst-quenching) grass.

01

Botanical Profile

Kusha (Desmostachya bipinnata) is a tall, robust perennial grass of the Poaceae family, native to South Asia, North Africa, the Middle East, and tropical East Africa. The plant grows in dense tufts reaching 30–180 cm in height, with stiff, sharp-tipped leaves (culms) that are bluish-green and smooth with scabrid margins — the very sharpness that made them ideal for the ritual cutting of darbha rings. It thrives along riverbanks, moist wastelands, canal margins, and irrigated fields up to 800 m altitude.

The rhizome is thick, creeping, and fibrous — the primary medicinal part used in Ayurvedic formulation. Roots are sweet, cooling, and highly mucilaginous. The plant flowers from August to December producing spikes arranged in a bipinnate panicle — the structural feature referenced in its species epithet bipinnata. It is frequently confused with Imperata cylindrica (Kasha) in vernacular usage, but distinguished by its bipinnate spikelets and habitat preference.

02

Sacred & Vedic Significance

No other grass in the entire plant kingdom carries the spiritual weight of Kusha. It is mentioned in the Rigveda, Atharvaveda, Ramayana, Mahabharata, and Manusmriti — an unbroken thread of sacredness spanning four millennia. In the Bhagavad Gita (6.11), Krishna himself prescribes a seat of Kusha grass covered with deerskin for the seated yogi: "shuchau deshe pratishthaapya sthiramasanamaatmanah, natyuchhritam naatineecham chailajinakushottaram".

"Kusho vai pavitram" — Kusha is verily the purifier.
Darbha (Kusha) is the sacred medium between the divine and the human, the bridge between the gross and the subtle. Wherever Kusha is placed, that ground becomes consecrated. Wherever Kusha is held, the hands become fit for sacred action.

Shatapatha Brahmana · Taittiriya Upanishad reference

In Vedic ritual, Kusha is indispensable: it is woven into pavitra rings worn on the fingers during yajna, spread as the seat of the gods (barhis), used to filter Soma juice, strewn at the conclusion of every major rite (paryagni), and placed in the hands and mouths of the deceased to purify the passage of the soul. The Pitri-tarpana (ancestral offerings), Shraddha ceremonies, and all sixteen Samskaras invoke Kusha as a purifier of the ritual space.

The grass is considered by the Puranas to have sprung from the feathers of Garuda as he carried the pot of Amrita — hence its association with immortality and purification. Its sharp tips are said to pierce negative energies; its contact with water is said to sanctify it instantly. The mere act of sitting on Kusha mats during meditation was understood — and is now being validated — as a form of subtle electromagnetic grounding.

03

Phytochemistry

Phytochemical investigation of Kusha has revealed a rich profile of bioactive constituents including flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolic acids, sterols, and significant quantities of silica — the latter likely responsible for the sharp, purifying nature of the leaves recognised in ancient ritual. The rhizome and roots, used primarily in Ayurveda, are mucilaginous, sweet, and loaded with polysaccharides and mineral salts.

04

Ayurvedic Significance

Kusha is classified by Charaka in the Trnapanchamula — the five medicinal roots of grasses — alongside Kasha, Shara, Ikshu, and Itkata, prescribed collectively in decoction for Pitta-aggravated conditions, particularly of the urinary tract and reproductive system. Sushruta lists it in Mutravirechaniya Gana (diuretics) and Sandhaniya Gana (wound-healing). Vagbhata invokes it in kidney stone, urinary burning, and haemorrhagic disorders.

Bhavaprakasha Nighantu (Haritakyadi Varga) states: "Kusha-o madhuro sheetah kashayo laghuhi smritah, mutralah pittashamano raktapittaharo guru" — sweet, cool, astringent, light; diuretic, Pitta-pacifying, and haemostatic. Classical preparations include Trnapanchamula Kwatha, Chaturmula Kwatha, Sarivadyasava, and Chandanasava.

05

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological studies on Desmostachya bipinnata have confirmed diuretic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, wound-healing, haemostatic, and hepatoprotective activities — vindicating the full range of its classical therapeutic claims across Ayurveda, Unani, and traditional African medicine.

Diuretic & Urinary Tract Support
The rhizome decoction is among the most classical urinary remedies in Ayurveda. Diuretic activity confirmed in animal models with significant increase in urine output and sodium excretion. Indicated in dysuria, burning micturition, urethritis, cystitis, and urinary calculi (kidney stones) — the mucilage soothes the epithelium while the potassium salts promote gentle flushing.
Haemostatic & Raktapitta
Kusha is a premier classical herb for Raktapitta — bleeding disorders precipitated by Pitta excess. The astringent-sweet combination arrests haemorrhage from the urinary tract (haematuria), respiratory tract (haemoptysis), and uterine excess (menorrhagia). The kashaya rasa tightens capillaries; the sheeta virya reduces the inflammatory drive behind Pitta-type bleeding.
Antipyretic & Thirst-Quenching
Trnapanchamula kwatha — decoction of the five grass roots led by Kusha — is a time-honoured Ayurvedic cooling tonic prescribed in fevers of Pitta origin, including typhoid, malarial, and inflammatory fevers accompanied by intense thirst. The cooling virya and sweet post-digestive taste replace lost fluids and mineral salts while calming the febrile state.
Hepatoprotective Activity
Ethanolic extracts of the rhizome demonstrated significant hepatoprotection against carbon tetrachloride-induced liver damage in animal models, reducing liver enzyme elevations (AST, ALT, ALP) and restoring hepatic architecture. The triterpenoids cylindrin and arundoin are the principal active agents.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Aqueous and ethanolic extracts significantly inhibit carrageenan-induced paw edema and writhing in standard anti-inflammatory and analgesic assays. Flavone content — luteolin and apigenin — suppresses COX pathway inflammatory mediators. Classical use in gout, joint inflammation, and abdominal pain aligns with these findings.
Wound Healing & Skin Conditions
Both leaf paste and root decoction are used traditionally for wound healing, burns, and skin infections. In vivo wound-healing studies confirm accelerated epithelialisation and collagen synthesis — attributed to the combined action of silica (connective tissue support), phenolic acids (antimicrobial), and mucilaginous polysaccharides (biofilm formation over the wound surface).
Anxiolytic & Nervous System Support
Traditional yogic use of Kusha mats for meditation has prompted modern investigation. Animal studies suggest mild sedative-anxiolytic effects of leaf extracts, with reduced locomotor activity and enhanced pentobarbital sleep time. The flavonoid apigenin is a known GABA-A receptor positive modulator — providing a plausible mechanistic basis for the classical insistence on Kusha as a meditation substrate.
Anti-diabetic & Metabolic
Root extracts in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats showed significant reduction in fasting blood glucose, glycosylated haemoglobin, and improvement in lipid profile. The mechanism appears to involve both insulin secretagogue activity and peripheral glucose uptake enhancement, alongside antioxidant protection of pancreatic beta cells.

How to Use Kusha / Darbha

Kusha's primary medicinal part is its rhizome and roots, though the whole plant — grass, roots, and nodes — is used in different preparations. Internally, the decoction (kwatha) of fresh or dried roots is the most common and classical mode. Externally, the fresh leaf paste and darbha water (kusha-infused water) are widely used both ritually and therapeutically.

Safety & Precautions

Kusha is among the safest herbs in the Ayurvedic materia medica — it contains no known toxic alkaloids or hepatotoxic compounds, and has been used freely for millennia across populations of all ages. Its principal risk is overuse causing Kapha aggravation and mild hyponatremia (with excessive diuretic use). It is, however, a potent coolant — inappropriate in cold, Vata-dominant, or Kapha-dominant constitutions without careful modification.

  • Cold constitutions (Vata/Kapha dominant): The sheeta (cold) virya may aggravate Vata and Kapha in persons with cold, damp constitutions — combine with warming spices like ginger or black pepper when using internally in such individuals.
  • Pregnancy: The root decoction in classical doses is generally considered safe; however, avoid high doses as the diuretic and uterine-stimulating properties of large quantities are unknown. Consult an Ayurvedic practitioner.
  • Electrolyte balance: Prolonged use of high-dose diuretic preparations (Trnapanchamula kwatha) may deplete electrolytes — use with adequate fluid intake and consider periodic breaks.
  • Leaf cuts: The sharp-tipped leaves of Kusha can cause skin cuts and eye injuries during handling — traditional handling involves grasping the base, not the tip. Handle fresh grass carefully.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate the diuretic effect of prescription diuretics — inform your physician if combining with antihypertensive or diuretic medications.
  • Botanical confusion: Ensure correct identification — Kusha (Desmostachya bipinnata) is sometimes confused with Kasha (Saccharum spontaneum) or Ikshu (sugarcane) varieties. Authentic Darbha is identified by its bipinnate spikelets and dense, tufted growth form.
