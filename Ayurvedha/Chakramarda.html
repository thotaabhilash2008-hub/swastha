Chakramarda
Sickle Senna — The Wheel-Crusher of Skin Disease
Senna obtusifolia (L.) H.S.Irwin & Barneby  ·  Cassia tora L. (syn.)

Fig. 1 — Senna obtusifolia bearing its paired obovate leaflets and vivid yellow flowers — a common roadside herb of tropical India  ·  Fig. 2 — The defining feature: long, gracefully curved sickle-shaped seed pods that give the plant its common English name

❧

Its Sanskrit name tells the whole story: Chakra (wheel, disc) and marda (crusher, destroyer) — a plant that destroys ring-shaped skin diseases. Growing wild at every roadside and fallow field across tropical India, this bright-flowered weed has been Ayurveda's foremost remedy for ringworm, scabies, and fungal skin infections for over three thousand years — a classical indication so consistent and so well-supported by modern antifungal research that it stands as one of Ayurveda's most precisely validated ethnopharmacological correspondences.

01

Botanical Profile

Senna obtusifolia (L.) H.S.Irwin & Barneby — more widely known by its synonym Cassia tora L. — is an erect, herbaceous annual or short-lived perennial of the Fabaceae (Leguminosae) family, subfamily Caesalpinioideae. It grows to 60–120 cm, branching from the base, with smooth, slightly glaucous stems. The leaves are paripinnate with 2–3 pairs of obovate leaflets arranged symmetrically; a distinctive clubbed or cylindrical gland sits between the lower pair of leaflets on the petiole — a taxonomic identification marker.

The flowers are bright canary-yellow, five-petalled, and appear singly or in pairs from the leaf axils from August through November. The fruit — the plant's most memorable feature — is a long, slender, gracefully curved pod 10–20 cm in length, resembling a sickle or scythe, containing 20–30 small rhombic-oblong seeds separated by transverse septa. These seeds, called Chakramarda beeja, are the primary medicinal part alongside the leaves. The plant thrives in disturbed soils, roadsides, fallow agricultural land, and waste ground across the Indian plains, foothills, and coastal regions, often growing as a common agricultural weed in rice paddies.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Chakramarda's phytochemical profile is dominated by anthraquinone glycosides — the class of compounds responsible for both its powerful laxative and its antifungal actions. The seeds are particularly rich in chrysophanol, emodin, physcion, and obtusin — anthraquinones that disrupt fungal membrane integrity and inhibit fungal enzyme systems. These same compounds, at higher doses, act as stimulant laxatives by irritating the colonic mucosa and stimulating peristaltic contractions — the same mechanism used by senna (Cassia senna) and cascara.

The seed gum of Senna obtusifolia — a galactomannan polysaccharide — has attracted significant commercial and pharmaceutical interest as a thickening and stabilising agent (sold commercially as Cassia Gum, E499 in Europe). This galactomannan is structurally similar to locust bean gum and has prebiotic and cholesterol-lowering properties distinct from the anthraquinone fraction. The essential oil from leaves and seeds contains predominantly fatty acid esters, while the root yields chrysarobin — a potent topical antifungal and keratolytic agent used in classical Ayurvedic skin preparations.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Chakramarda is classified in all major classical Ayurvedic texts under kushtha (skin disease) management — its name itself encodes this identity. Charaka Samhita's Chikitsasthana lists it in the treatment of dadrU (ringworm/tinea), kushtha (skin diseases including psoriasis and eczema), and kandu (pruritus/itching). Sushruta Samhita includes it in Aragvadhadi Gana — a drug group specifically constituted for skin diseases — and describes topical seed paste as a treatment for charma-kushtha (skin-thickening conditions). Ashtanga Hridaya prescribes it for kilasa (leucoderma) and persistent skin eruptions.

"Chakramarda tikto kashayo ushno laghu, kandu-kushtha-dadrU-krimi-vatakaphanashana" — Dhanvantari Nighantu, Chandanadi Varga — Classical description of Chakramarda's properties

The shloka describes Chakramarda as bitter and astringent in taste, hot in potency, light in quality — a destroyer of itching (kandu), skin diseases (kushtha), ringworm (dadru), parasites (krimi), and Vata-Kapha disorders. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu catalogues it in the Haritakyadi Varga, noting its specific activity in pama (scabies) and vicharchika (weeping eczema). In Siddha medicine it is known as Tagarai and prominently used in Tagarai Ennai (medicated oil) for chronic scalp infections and psoriasis.

04

Key Health Benefits

Dadrughna — The Ayurvedic Antifungal: A Three-Thousand-Year Clinical Correlation
Chakramarda's Sanskrit synonym Dadrughna means literally "destroyer of dadru" — dadru being the classical Ayurvedic term for tinea (ringworm), the fungal skin infection caused by dermatophytes. Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed that chrysophanol, emodin, and physcion from Chakramarda seeds demonstrate potent antifungal activity against the very organisms responsible for dadru — Trichophyton rubrum, T. mentagrophytes, Microsporum canis, and Epidermophyton floccosum — at minimum inhibitory concentrations comparable to pharmaceutical antifungal preparations. The correspondence between an ancient vernacular name and precisely validated molecular antifungal mechanisms against identified target organisms stands as one of the most satisfying validations in the entire Ayurvedic materia medica.
Antifungal — Ringworm & Tinea
The paramount classical and modern indication. Seed paste and leaf juice applied topically to tinea corporis, tinea capitis, tinea pedis, and tinea versicolor. Chrysophanol disrupts ergosterol synthesis in fungal cell membranes; emodin inhibits fungal enzyme systems. Multiple studies confirm activity against T. rubrum, T. mentagrophytes, and M. canis at clinically achievable concentrations. Classical preparation: fresh seed paste with lemon juice or vinegar applied to ringworm lesions for 7–14 days.
Scabies & Ectoparasites
Classical designation as Krimighna (parasite-destroyer) is directly applied to scabies (pama) in Charaka's Chikitsasthana. Seed oil and paste show acaricidal activity against Sarcoptes scabiei in traditional preparations. The combination of anthraquinone toxicity and the sulfur-like irritant quality makes it a classical topical scabicide used across tribal and folk medicine throughout tropical India, Southeast Asia, and East Africa independently.
Eczema & Psoriasis (Topical)
Chrysarobin from the root is the natural precursor of dithranol (anthralin) — a pharmaceutical licensed for psoriasis treatment in Europe and North America. Topical seed preparations reduce the hyperproliferative keratinocyte response in psoriatic plaques. Classical use in vicharchika (weeping eczema) and charma-kushtha (skin thickening conditions) is validated through anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative mechanisms of the anthraquinone and chrysarobin fraction.
Laxative & Bowel Regulation
Sennosides A and B stimulate colonic peristalsis through the same mechanism as pharmaceutical senna preparations — prostaglandin-mediated smooth muscle contraction and reduced water absorption in the colon. Action onset 6–12 hours after ingestion. Classical internal use for vibandha (constipation) and udara roga (abdominal disorders). The dose-response distinction between antifungal topical doses and laxative internal doses is important — internal therapeutic doses are lower than topical concentrations.
Antimicrobial — Bacterial & Viral
Emodin and chrysophanol demonstrate significant antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA strains in some studies), Streptococcus pyogenes, Bacillus subtilis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Emodin inhibits coronavirus replication by blocking the 3CL protease — a finding that gained attention during COVID-19 research. Antiviral activity has also been demonstrated against herpes simplex and hepatitis B virus replication.
Liver & Digestive Protection
Seed extracts and isolated emodin show hepatoprotective activity against CCl₄-induced liver damage in animal models — paradoxically, as high doses of anthraquinones are hepatotoxic. At low therapeutic doses, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant flavonoid fraction (kaempferol, quercetin) dominates, protecting hepatocytes. Cassia tora seed tea has been used in Korean and Chinese traditional medicine for liver complaints and poor digestion for centuries.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Leaf and seed extracts inhibit COX-2 and 5-LOX pathways through emodin-mediated NF-κB suppression. Root decoctions are used in folk medicine across Jharkhand and tribal Odisha for joint pain, rheumatic conditions, and inflammatory fever. Emodin's anti-inflammatory potency has been the subject of multiple preclinical studies validating its potential in inflammatory arthritis and neuroinflammatory conditions.
Cholesterol & Metabolic Support
The galactomannan seed gum (Cassia Gum) is a soluble dietary fibre that reduces LDL cholesterol absorption in the intestine through bile acid sequestration — similar to psyllium husk mechanism. Emodin has also shown anti-diabetic potential through α-glucosidase inhibition. Traditional folk food use of Chakramarda leaves as a cooked vegetable (in parts of West Africa and South Asia) is consistent with its metabolic support properties.

Traditional & Ethnobotanical Uses

Chakramarda occupies a unique position as a plant whose medicinal identity is remarkably consistent across independent traditional medicine systems on three continents — a convergent ethnopharmacological pattern that strongly signals genuine efficacy. The antifungal-skin application has been documented independently in Indian Ayurveda, Siddha medicine, tribal traditions of Jharkhand and Odisha, Chinese traditional medicine, East African folk medicine, and Brazilian folk botany — all arriving at the same plant for the same condition without cross-cultural communication.

How to Use Chakramarda

Chakramarda's primary therapeutic application is topical — seed paste and leaf juice applied to fungal skin infections, scabies, and inflammatory skin conditions. Internal use is secondary and requires careful dosing given the stimulant laxative and potentially hepatotoxic effects of anthraquinones at high doses. Short-course internal use at appropriate doses is safe and classically validated; long-term daily internal use should be supervised.

Safety & Precautions

Chakramarda is safe for short-term topical use and short-course internal use at appropriate doses. The principal concerns relate to the anthraquinone content — the same compounds that make it therapeutically potent are dose-dependently hepatotoxic and can cause electrolyte imbalance with long-term internal use, paralleling the safety profile of pharmaceutical senna preparations.

  • Long-term internal use: Anthraquinone-containing plants including Chakramarda should not be used internally for more than 8–10 consecutive days without medical supervision. Prolonged stimulant laxative use causes laxative dependence, colonic atony (cathartic colon), and potassium depletion. The classical Ayurvedic protocols use short, defined courses — not chronic daily supplementation.
  • Hepatotoxicity at high doses: Emodin and chrysophanol are hepatotoxic at high or sustained doses in animal models. Keep internal doses within classical therapeutic ranges (seed powder 1–3 g; decoction 30–50 ml). Individuals with pre-existing liver disease should avoid internal use or use only under specialist supervision.
  • Pregnancy: Strictly contraindicated internally. Anthraquinone glycosides stimulate intestinal and uterine smooth muscle — risk of uterine contractions and miscarriage. Classical texts contraindicate Chakramarda internally during pregnancy. Topical use on intact skin away from the abdomen is generally considered safe.
  • Breastfeeding: Anthraquinones pass into breast milk and can cause diarrhoea and colic in infants — avoid internal use while breastfeeding.
  • Children (internal): Internal stimulant laxative use in children under 12 is not recommended without paediatric Ayurvedic supervision. Topical use for ringworm in older children is safe and widely practised.
  • Skin irritation (topical): Pure seed paste may cause localised skin irritation, redness, or contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals — particularly around the eyes and on mucous membranes. Dilute with sesame oil for sensitive skin; avoid contact with eyes. Patch test 24 hours before widespread application.
  • Electrolyte monitoring: Patients on diuretics, cardiac glycosides (digoxin), or corticosteroids who use Chakramarda internally face additive potassium-depletion risk — inform treating physician and avoid concurrent use without supervision.
  • Inflammatory bowel disease: Contraindicated internally in Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, irritable bowel syndrome, intestinal obstruction, or acute abdominal conditions — stimulant laxative action will aggravate these conditions.
