Cited by Charaka among the most important Vatahara (Vata-pacifying) herbs and ranked in the celebrated Dashamula-adjacent formulations for musculoskeletal disease, Rasna — "that which delights the tongue" — is Ayurveda's premier orchid medicine: an epiphytic jewel of the forest canopy whose roots and leaves have eased arthritic joints, quieted neural pain, and cleared febrile inflammation across the Indian subcontinent for over three thousand years.

01

Botanical Profile

📋

Nomenclature Note: "Rasna" is one of Ayurveda's most contested botanical identities. Classical texts list it under multiple synonyms — Suvaha, Yuktarasna, Elaparni — and different regional traditions have substituted different species. The primary and most pharmacologically validated species is Vanda tessellata (syn. Vanda roxburghii), used across Eastern and Central India. In parts of North India and Nepal, Pluchea lanceolata (Asteraceae) is substituted as "Rasna." In Kerala and South India, Alpinia galanga (Greater Galangal) has been used. This page focuses on the classical Ayurvedic Rasna — Vanda tessellata — as validated by the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API).

Rasna (Vanda tessellata) is a large epiphytic orchid of the Orchidaceae family — making it the only major orchid in classical Ayurveda's primary materia medica. It grows on the branches and trunks of large deciduous trees in tropical and subtropical forests across India (particularly Eastern India, Deccan plateau, and the Himalayan foothills up to 1,200 m altitude), Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and Southeast Asia.

The plant has no pseudobulbs — instead producing a monopodial (single-stemmed, continuously growing) stem of 30–90 cm with thick, strap-shaped, leathery leaves 15–25 cm long arranged in two opposite rows. Long, silvery-grey aerial roots extend downward from the stem, absorbing moisture from air and rain — a defining orchid adaptation. Flowers are exquisite: 3–5 cm across, tessellated (chequerboard-patterned) in yellow-brown and green with a distinctive violet-purple lip, borne on pendant racemes of 8–15 flowers from June–September. All parts — roots, leaves, stem, and flowers — are recorded as medicinally active, with the roots carrying the greatest pharmacological load.

Phytochemistry & Key Compounds

Phytochemical investigations of Vanda tessellata have revealed a rich profile dominated by phenanthrene derivatives — a class of polycyclic aromatic compounds characteristic of the Orchidaceae family — alongside stilbenoids, flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenoids. The phenanthrenes, including nudol, coelonin, and gigantol, appear to be the principal bioactive constituents responsible for the plant's anti-inflammatory and analgesic actions, and represent compounds rarely found in non-orchid plant families.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Rasna is referenced extensively in all the major classical texts. Charaka places it in Vatahara Mahakashaya — the ten great groups of Vata-pacifying herbs — and in Shothahara (anti-oedema) and Anabhishyandi (non-blocking) groups. Sushruta includes it in Eladi Gana and Viratarvadi Gana. The Ashtaanga Hridayam employs it in numerous Vatavyadhi (Vata disease) formulations. It is a cardinal ingredient of Rasna Saptaka Kwatha, Rasnadi Kwatha, Rasna Erandadi Kwatha, and the celebrated Maharasnadi Kwatha — one of classical Ayurveda's most important polyherbal decoctions for musculoskeletal and neurological disorders.

Classical shloka — "Rasna tikta katu snigdha ushna vatakaphapaha, shothajwaravishagulma shwasahikkashu shasyate" — describes Rasna as bitter, pungent, unctuous, hot in potency, pacifying Vata and Kapha, and specifically indicated in oedema, fever, poisoning, abdominal tumours, dyspnoea, and hiccup.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research on Vanda tessellata has confirmed its potent anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activities through multiple in vitro and in vivo studies. The orchid-specific phenanthrenes represent a structurally novel class of bioactives that have attracted interest from drug discovery researchers for their distinctive mechanisms of action.

Arthritis & Joint Disease
Rasna's foremost classical application — explicitly indicated in Sandhivata (osteoarthritis), Amavata (rheumatoid arthritis), and Vatarakta (gout). Root extracts significantly reduce joint inflammation, cartilage degradation markers, and pain scores in animal arthritis models. The mainstay of Maharasnadi Kwatha — Ayurveda's most prescribed decoction for musculoskeletal disease.
Sciatica & Neuralgic Pain
Classically described as the primary herb for Gridhrasi (sciatica) — the shooting pain radiating from the lower back down the leg caused by sciatic nerve compression. Rasna's analgesic and anti-inflammatory phenanthrenes reduce nerve root irritation, while its Ushna virya counters the cold-type pain characteristic of Vata-origin neuralgias.
Anti-inflammatory (Systemic)
Ethanolic and aqueous root extracts demonstrate significant inhibition of carrageenan-induced paw oedema in rodent models — a validated proxy for acute systemic inflammation. The phenanthrenes (nudol, coelonin) inhibit COX-2 and lipoxygenase pathways. Classical use covers all Shotha (inflammatory oedema) conditions across organ systems.
Fever & Anti-infective
Classically ranked as Jwaraghna (fever-reducing) and listed in the treatment of fevers of all types in the Ashtaanga Hridayam. Root extracts show antipyretic activity comparable to paracetamol in yeast-induced pyrexia models. Antimicrobial activity against both gram-positive and gram-negative pathogens supports its use in infective fevers.
Respiratory Support
Classical indication in Shwasa (dyspnoea/asthma) and Hikka (hiccup) — both considered Vata-Kapha disorders. Rasna's bronchodilatory and anti-inflammatory effects reduce bronchial spasm and mucus hypersecretion. Used in Rasnadi Churna for chronic obstructive pulmonary conditions alongside other Vata-pacifying herbs.
Antioxidant & Neuroprotective
Gigantol — Rasna's principal stilbenoid bibenzyl — demonstrates neuroprotective activity in glutamate-induced neuronal toxicity models and significant DPPH radical-scavenging capacity. Flavonoid components (luteolin, apigenin) provide synergistic antioxidant protection, supporting classical Rasayana (rejuvenative) claims for Rasna in neurological weakness.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Nudol and batatasin III show significant antifungal activity — consistent with the orchid's own use of these compounds as phytoalexins (plant defence molecules against fungal pathogens). The whole-plant extract inhibits a broad range of human pathogens including Aspergillus, Candida, Staphylococcus aureus, and E. coli.
Muscle Relaxant & Spasm Relief
Classical texts prescribe Rasna in Avarana (obstruction-type) Vata conditions with muscle spasm, stiffness, and cramps. Pharmacological studies demonstrate smooth-muscle relaxant activity of root extracts on isolated intestinal and uterine preparations — supporting its use in abdominal colic, uterine cramps, and tension-type muscular pain.

How to Use Rasna

Rasna is primarily used in compound classical formulations rather than as a single herb — reflecting its traditional deployment as part of synergistic multi-herb preparations. Single-herb use of root powder or decoction is practised under physician guidance, particularly for acute joint and neurological presentations. Due to concerns about wild population pressures, ensure sourcing from sustainable cultivated suppliers.

Safety & Precautions

Rasna has a long classical safety record and is not associated with direct systemic toxicity at therapeutic doses. Its principal cautions relate to its Ushna virya (hot potency) — which can aggravate Pitta — and to the importance of correct botanical identity, given the widespread confusion of "Rasna" between three or four different plant species across different regional traditions.

  • Botanical identity verification: Ensure any Rasna preparation is sourced from a reputable Ayurvedic pharmacy that specifies Vanda tessellata as the botanical source — or at minimum, follows the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India specification. Misidentified material from Pluchea lanceolata or Alpinia galanga substitutes have different phytochemical profiles and indications.
  • Pitta aggravation: Ushna virya (hot potency) is contraindicated in hyperacidity, gastric ulcers, bleeding disorders, acute inflammatory Pitta conditions, and individuals with strongly Pitta-dominant constitutions. Combine with ghee, milk, or cooling herbs as anupana if needed.
  • Pregnancy: Internal use during pregnancy should be avoided without physician supervision. Classical texts note its uterine stimulant properties (used in dysmenorrhoea) — these are contraindicated in early pregnancy.
  • Orchid conservation: Vanda tessellata is increasingly threatened in the wild due to habitat loss and collection pressure. Prioritise preparations from cultivated sources or pharmaceutical-grade suppliers. Do not collect wild plants.
  • Drug interactions: Rasna's anti-inflammatory mechanisms may interact additively with NSAIDs and corticosteroids — monitor for enhanced effects and potential gastropathy risk when combined. Inform treating physicians of concurrent use.
  • Diabetic caution: Some preliminary evidence suggests mild hypoglycaemic activity — individuals on anti-diabetic medications should monitor blood glucose when using therapeutic doses.
  • Children: Classical Rasna formulations like Maharasnadi Kwatha are used in Ayurvedic paediatrics, but dose must be carefully scaled by weight (typically ¼ to ⅓ of adult dose) under specialist supervision.
  • Duration of use: Long-term continuous use of single-herb root decoctions beyond 8–12 weeks should be reviewed by a physician. Classical compound formulations are better tolerated for sustained use than single-herb preparations.
