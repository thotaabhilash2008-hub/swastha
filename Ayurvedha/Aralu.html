Known in Sanskrit as Shleshmataka — "that which holds things together with mucilage" — Aralu is a modest deciduous tree whose viscous fruit, astringent bark, and expectorant leaves have been prized by Ayurvedic vaidyas since the time of Charaka and Sushruta. Straddling the worlds of food and medicine, its edible drupes are pickled across rural India while its bark decoction heals fevers, its root treats skin disease, and its mucilaginous fruit coat soothes the most inflamed of respiratory passages.

01

Botanical Profile

Cordia dichotoma G. Forst. is a small to medium deciduous tree of the Boraginaceae family, typically growing 6–15 m tall with a short, often crooked trunk and a spreading, irregularly branched crown. The name dichotoma refers to its distinctly forked — dichotomous — branching pattern, which gives the canopy a characteristic open, airy appearance. It is distributed widely across tropical and subtropical Asia — from Pakistan and India through Myanmar, Thailand, southern China, the Philippines, and northern Australia — and is equally at home in dry deciduous forests, scrub land, and village roadsides.

The bark is brown-grey, longitudinally fissured, and somewhat corky in mature trees; young twigs are pubescent. Leaves are alternate, 5–14 cm long, broadly ovate with a rounded or cordate base, entire or obscurely toothed margins, and a texture that is rough above and softly hairy beneath. Flowers appear in small terminal or axillary cymes before or with the new leaves — small, white to pale cream, 5-petalled, and faintly fragrant. The fruit — botanically a drupe — is the most medically and culinarily significant part: 1–1.5 cm in diameter, globose, greenish-yellow ripening to translucent pale yellow or pinkish-orange, seated in a persistent membranous calyx. The mesocarp is covered in a conspicuously viscid, sticky mucilage — the defining feature that earns it the Sanskrit name Shleshmataka and the English "clammy cherry."

02

Phytochemical Composition

Phytochemical investigations of Cordia dichotoma have identified a structurally diverse array of secondary metabolites distributed across all parts of the plant. The fruit mucilage — clinically the most important fraction — is a complex polysaccharide composed of arabinose, galactose, rhamnose, and glucuronic acid residues, responsible for the plant's acclaimed demulcent and expectorant properties. The bark yields an important suite of terpenoids, phenolics, and alkaloids; the roots contain the bitter allantoin and rosmarinic acid.

Cordifoline and cordifolioside, two novel phenylethanoid glycosides first isolated from C. dichotoma, exhibit potent antioxidant and hepatoprotective activity. The seeds contain a fixed oil (15–20% yield) rich in palmitic, stearic, oleic, and linoleic acids. The presence of allantoin across bark and root ties the plant pharmacologically to wound healing and tissue regeneration — a classical application confirmed by modern research.

Mucilaginous Mesocarp
The sticky, viscid outer flesh is the defining therapeutic element — its complex polysaccharide forms a protective film over inflamed mucous membranes of the throat, bronchi, and GI tract, suppressing cough, easing swallowing, and reducing gastric irritation.
Endocarp (Pyrene / Stone)
The hard inner stone contains the oily seed. Seed oil is used topically for skin inflammation and rheumatic pain. The stone itself was used in traditional bookbinding adhesive — the same mucilage that soothes throats once held manuscripts together.
Persistent Calyx
The dried persistent calyx and outer exocarp carry concentrated tannins and flavonoids. In folk preparations, the whole dried fruit is decocted for use in diarrhoea, dysentery, and febrile conditions — the calyx contributing much of the astringent action.
03

Ayurvedic & Classical Significance

Shleshmataka (Aralu) is catalogued in all major Nighantu texts of classical Ayurveda. Charaka mentions it in the context of respiratory and urinary tract therapy, and lists the fruit among Anulomana (carminative-laxative) preparations. Sushruta employs the bark decoction in Vrana Shodhana (wound-cleansing preparations) and the fruit in Kasa Chikitsa (treatment of cough). The Bhavaprakasha Nighantu classifies Shleshmataka under the Amradi Phala Varga (group of mango-allied fruits) and gives it detailed properties.

"Shleshmatakam guru snigdham kashayam madhuram hitam | Pittasrakashamanascha vrana-shophaharam param ||"
Shleshmataka (Aralu) is heavy, unctuous, astringent-sweet in taste, and wholesome. It is an excellent pacifier of Pitta and blood disorders, and is supreme in reducing wounds and swellings.
— Bhavaprakasha Nighantu, Amradi Phala Varga

Dhanvantari Nighantu places the fruit in treatments for Daha (burning sensations), Trishna (thirst), and Mutraghata (urinary obstruction). Raja Nighantu catalogues its use in Kshaya (wasting diseases, akin to tuberculosis) and as a general Rasayana (rejuvenative) fruit. The bark's role in wound management and fever — mentioned in Ashtanga Hridayam — is substantiated by modern antimicrobial and antipyretic data.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological studies on Cordia dichotoma have validated its classical reputation as an anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, and wound-healing plant across in vitro and animal models. The fruit mucilage has attracted particular scientific interest as a natural polymer with pharmaceutical excipient and therapeutic properties.

Respiratory Demulcent & Antitussive
The viscid fruit mucilage is among Ayurveda's finest natural demulcents for the respiratory tract. It coats and protects inflamed bronchial epithelium, suppresses the cough reflex, thins tenacious mucus, and soothes sore throats. Decoctions of the ripe fruit are used in dry cough, bronchitis, laryngitis, and hoarseness — particularly in Vata-Pitta cough with burning and dryness.
Hepatoprotective & Liver Support
Cordifoline and cordifolioside extracted from the bark and fruit demonstrate significant hepatoprotective activity in CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity models — reducing ALT, AST, and ALP levels while restoring liver architecture. The triterpenoids ursolic and oleanolic acid contribute additional anti-fibrotic and antioxidant liver-protective effects. Traditional use in jaundice (Kamala) is strongly supported.
Wound Healing & Anti-inflammatory
Allantoin in the root and bark promotes tissue regeneration by stimulating fibroblast proliferation; tannins provide antibacterial protection and astringent haemostasis. Bark paste applied to wounds significantly reduces healing time in excision wound models. Ursolic and oleanolic acids inhibit COX-2 and pro-inflammatory cytokines, validating the classical use of bark decoction for inflamed swellings and ulcers.
Antimicrobial & Antipyretic
Bark and leaf extracts show inhibitory activity against S. aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Salmonella typhi, and Candida albicans in disc-diffusion assays. Antipyretic activity of bark decoction has been demonstrated in yeast-induced pyrexia in rats, validating traditional use in febrile illness. Hydroquinone and rosmarinic acid are the primary antimicrobial drivers.
Gastrointestinal Health
The fruit's Picchila (viscid) quality creates a protective mucosal lining throughout the GI tract, reducing gastric acid contact in peptic ulcer disease and colitis. The decoction of dried fruit is used in diarrhoea and dysentery — combining astringent tannins with antimicrobial activity. Mild laxative action of ripe fruit relieves Vata-type constipation without cramping.
Urinary Tract Soothing
The classical indication for Mutraghata (urinary obstruction) and burning micturition is explained by the fruit mucilage's soothing effect on inflamed urethral epithelium — reducing friction, easing passage, and protecting the urothelium from acid urine. Mild diuretic action increases urine output, aiding in flushing the urinary tract in cystitis and urethritis.
Antioxidant Protection
Methanolic extracts of fruit, bark, and leaves demonstrate strong free-radical scavenging activity in DPPH and ABTS assays, with the phenylethanoid glycosides and flavonoids (rutin, quercetin) as primary contributors. Regular consumption of the edible fruit contributes to dietary antioxidant intake comparable to commonly eaten stone fruits.
Skin Disorders & External Use
Root decoction is a classical remedy for Kushtha (skin diseases including psoriasis and eczema) — with arbutin and hydroquinone offering antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory support. The seed oil is applied to chronic skin ulcers, rheumatic joints, and scalp conditions. The mucilaginous fruit pulp is applied as a poultice to minor burns and skin irritation in folk tradition across Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.
05

Culinary & Ethno-food Use

Unlike most medicinal herbs in the Swastha Garden, Aralu enjoys a lively culinary identity alongside its therapeutic one. The fresh unripe and semi-ripe drupes are widely consumed as a vegetable and pickle across rural India — particularly in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, and the Deccan — where they are prepared as Lasura ka achar (Gondi pickle), a preservation tradition dating back centuries. The sticky mucilage, which is somewhat off-putting raw, becomes pleasantly cohesive when pickled with mustard, fenugreek, and asafoetida.

Ripe fresh fruits are eaten raw, dried, or candied in tribal communities across central India. Young leaves are cooked as a pot herb in dry-region cuisines. The gum exuded from the trunk — known as Shleshmataka gum or Indian gum — is soluble in water and was historically used as a bookbinding adhesive in palm-leaf manuscript preparation, and as a sizing agent in textile weaving. Modern food technology has proposed the mucilage as a natural food-grade thickener, stabiliser, and tablet binder in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

06

How to Consume Aralu

Aralu is one of the more food-friendly herbs in the Ayurvedic tradition — the fruit, particularly when ripe, is safely consumed as a food across all constitutions in moderate quantities. Therapeutic internal preparations should be guided by a practitioner, especially for conditions of the liver, urinary tract, and skin. The bark and root preparations are more potent and require supervision.

Safety & Precautions

Aralu fruit consumed as food is extremely safe for most individuals. The plant does not contain known toxic compounds at food quantities and has a long history of safe human consumption. Bark and root preparations at therapeutic doses are generally well-tolerated but warrant the precautions listed below.

  • Kapha-dominant individuals & obesity: The heavy, viscid, and unctuous fruit qualities can aggravate Kapha — individuals with obesity, sluggish digestion, or heavy mucus congestion should limit fruit intake and avoid therapeutic doses without guidance.
  • Pregnancy: The ripe fruit as a food is generally safe. However, therapeutic bark and root decoctions should be avoided in pregnancy — traditional texts note uterine-stimulating properties of bark preparations at higher doses.
  • Excessive intake of unripe fruit: Unripe Aralu fruit is intensely astringent and may cause oral mucosa irritation, gastric constipation, and nausea in sensitive individuals. Ensure fruit is adequately ripe before consumption.
  • Pickle (Achar) and sodium: Commercial and home Gondi pickles are high in salt — those with hypertension, cardiac conditions, or kidney disease should consume pickled Aralu very sparingly.
  • Mucilage as laxative: In large quantities the fruit mucilage acts as a mild laxative — those with pre-existing diarrhoea, IBS-D, or loose stools should avoid high fruit intake.
  • Drug interactions (bark/root): Antipyretic and anti-inflammatory bark preparations may interact additively with NSAIDs and hepatoprotective drugs — inform your physician if using both simultaneously.
  • Allantoin sensitivity: Rare cases of contact sensitivity to allantoin-containing preparations have been reported — discontinue topical bark paste if skin redness or itching develops.
