Revered across millennia as Udumbara — "the tree that bestows abundance" — Ficus racemosa is one of Ayurveda's most celebrated Triphala-adjacent rasayanas, hymned in the Atharva Veda and enshrined in the Charaka Samhita among the ten great fruits. A sacred tree to Hindus, Buddhists, and Jains alike, every part — fruit, bark, leaf, latex, and root — offers a distinct therapeutic gift.

01

Botanical Profile

Udumbara (Ficus racemosa, syn. F. glomerata) is a large, fast-growing deciduous-to-semi-evergreen tree of the Moraceae family reaching 15–30 m in height, with a broad spreading crown and pale grey, often flaky bark. It grows preferentially along riverbanks and moist, low-lying ground across tropical South Asia, Southeast Asia, northern Australia, and parts of China, earning it the common name "Cluster Fig" or "Country Fig."

Its most distinctive feature is cauliflory — the production of its syncarpous, pear-shaped figs (syconia) directly on the trunk and main branches rather than at the tips of twigs. These figs, borne in dense hanging clusters (racemes), ripen from green through yellow to a deep orange-red, each measuring 2–5 cm across. The tree has a mutualistic relationship with a specific species of fig wasp (Ceratosolen fusciceps) for pollination. Leaves are elliptic-oblong, glabrous above, and slightly pubescent beneath.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Extensive phytochemical investigation of Ficus racemosa has revealed a rich matrix of bioactive compounds distributed across its various parts. The bark is particularly rich in triterpenoids and phytosterols; the fruit in sugars, flavonoids, and phenolic acids; the latex in gums and proteolytic enzymes; and the roots in glycosides and tannins. This whole-plant diversity of chemistry accounts for the broad therapeutic applications documented in classical and folk traditions.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Udumbara occupies a singular position in Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia. Charaka lists it among the Phala Varga (therapeutic fruits) and the Pureeshavirajaniya Gana (drugs that restore normal stool colour) as well as Kashaya Skandha (astringent group). Sushruta classifies it among the Nyagrodha Panchaka — the five sacred Ficus species — alongside Ashwattha, Plaksha, Vata, and Parisha, each of supreme therapeutic and ritual importance.

Classical shloka from Ashtanga Hridayam — "Udumbarafalam madhuram kashayam pittaghnam param, raktapittaharam sheetam dahaharakaram tatha" — states: sweet and astringent in taste, supreme in pacifying Pitta, haemostatic in bleeding Pitta disorders, cooling in potency, and alleviant of burning sensations.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has validated an extensive range of classical and ethnobotanical claims for Ficus racemosa — including significant antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and haemostatic activities confirmed in preclinical and early clinical studies.

Antidiabetic & Hypoglycaemic
Among the most robustly documented effects: bark extracts significantly lower fasting blood glucose in streptozotocin-induced diabetic animal models and show promise in human observational studies. Mechanism involves α-glucosidase inhibition, improved insulin sensitivity, and pancreatic beta-cell protection attributed to gluanol acetate, lupeol, and tannin fractions.
Haemostatic & Raktapitta Remedy
The primary classical indication — raktapitta (bleeding Pitta disorders) including menorrhagia, metrorrhagia, epistaxis, haemoptysis, and haematuria. Bark decoctions and fruit juice are used to arrest bleeding via tannin-mediated vasoconstriction, clot formation, and Pitta-cooling action. Clinical studies on dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) show comparable efficacy to standard progestational agents.
Hepatoprotective
Bark extracts demonstrate significant hepatoprotection against paracetamol- and CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity in animal studies, with marked reduction in serum ALT, AST, and bilirubin. Lupeol and racemosic acid are primary bioactive contributors. Traditionally used in jaundice, fatty liver, and liver enlargement.
Anti-diarrhoeal & Dysentery
The astringent (kashaya) bark and unripe fruit are among the most prescribed Ayurvedic remedies for chronic diarrhoea, irritable bowel, and amoebic dysentery. Tannins reduce gut hypermotility, inhibit enteropathogens, and reduce intestinal secretion. Bark decoction with honey or buttermilk is a classical combination for raktatisara (bloody diarrhoea).
Anti-inflammatory & Wound Healing
Bark and leaf extracts show significant anti-inflammatory activity in carrageenan-induced paw-edema models. Leaf paste and latex are traditional wound dressings — antimicrobial, haemostatic, and granulation-promoting. The latex's ficin enzyme has documented proteolytic debridement activity for chronic wounds.
Anthelmintic (Latex)
The white latex of Udumbara has strong anthelmintic properties — traditionally used against intestinal worms including roundworms and threadworms. Ficin, the proteolytic enzyme in latex, is directly toxic to helminth parasites. Used in folk traditions across the subcontinent as a safe, natural deworming agent.
Gynaecological Support
A deeply respected uterine tonic in Ayurveda. Bark decoctions manage menorrhagia, leucorrhoea, and menstrual irregularity. Ripe fruits are prescribed in infertility protocols (Vandhyatva Chikitsa). The Udumbaravaleha — a classical confection — is used for uterine debility and post-partum recovery.
Antioxidant & Rasayana
High flavonoid, tannin, and phenolic acid content confers strong free-radical scavenging activity across bark, fruit, and leaf extracts (DPPH, FRAP, ABTS). The ripe fruit is a nutritive rasayana — rebuilding ojas (vital essence), improving complexion, promoting longevity, and supporting immunity in depleted or recovering individuals.

How to Consume Udumbara

Different parts of Udumbara are prepared in distinct forms according to classical Ayurveda. The fruit is consumed nutritively; the bark primarily as decoction or powder for therapeutic applications; the latex topically. Dosage and duration should be guided by a qualified practitioner, especially for therapeutic use.

Safety & Precautions

Udumbara is generally regarded as safe at therapeutic doses for most adults, with a long and well-established history of dietary and medicinal use. The fruit, when consumed in moderate amounts as food, has no known adverse effects. However, therapeutic preparations of the bark and latex require appropriate caution.

  • Latex (internal): The raw latex of Udumbara should never be consumed internally — it contains high concentrations of ficin and other proteolytic enzymes that can cause serious gastrointestinal irritation, ulceration, and systemic effects.
  • Pregnancy: Bark decoction in high therapeutic doses should be avoided during pregnancy due to potential uterine-stimulating effects; moderate fruit consumption as food is generally considered safe.
  • Kapha-dominant constitutions: The madhura (sweet) rasa and guru (heavy) quality of the ripe fruit can aggravate Kapha — those with obesity, diabetes type-2, mucus disorders, and respiratory congestion should consume in moderation.
  • Hypoglycaemic medications: Bark extracts with documented hypoglycaemic activity may potentiate insulin or oral antidiabetic drugs — blood glucose should be monitored closely when combining with pharmaceutical agents.
  • Unripe figs: Excessive consumption of unripe fruit can cause constipation and digestive discomfort due to high tannin and astringent content.
  • Latex contact allergy: Some individuals may develop contact dermatitis to the milky latex — patch-test before therapeutic topical use; individuals with known latex or fig-tree allergy should avoid entirely.
  • Fig wasps: Commercially unavailable wild figs sometimes contain fig wasp remnants — the proteolytic ficin in the fruit digests these entirely; the presence of insects is harmless but worth noting for those with insect allergies.
  • Interactions with anticoagulants: High-dose bark preparations with haemostatic (clot-promoting) activity may partially oppose anticoagulant medications (warfarin, heparin); consult a physician if on blood-thinning therapy.
