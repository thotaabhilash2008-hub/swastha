Rose — The Queen of Flowers
Rosa damascena Mill.  ·  Rosa centifolia L.  ·  Rosa indica

Fig. 1 — Velvet-petalled Rosa damascena, the primary source of rose attar and rosewater  ·  Fig. 2 — Dried rose petals and rose hips — principal forms used in Ayurvedic, Unani, and culinary traditions

❧

In Sanskrit, Shatapatra — "she of a hundred petals" — the Rose has been woven into the fabric of Indian civilisation as medicine, perfume, ritual offering, and culinary delight for over two thousand years. Beloved equally by Ayurveda, Unani, and Siddha, it is the pre-eminent Hridya dravya (heart tonic), the sovereign Pitta-pacifier, and the fragrant ambassador of beauty in classical pharmacopoeia.

01

Botanical Profile

The medicinal Rose of the Indian subcontinent belongs primarily to three species: Rosa damascena Mill. (Damask Rose — the source of attar and rosewater), Rosa centifolia L. (Cabbage Rose — used in Unani medicine), and Rosa indica (Country Rose — widely distributed across India). All are thorny, deciduous or semi-evergreen shrubs of the Rosaceae family, with pinnately compound leaves, stipulate leaflets, and the characteristic solitary or clustered flowers that have made them universally beloved.

Rosa damascena, the most medicinally significant, is cultivated extensively in the valley of Kannauj (Uttar Pradesh) — India's attar capital — as well as in Rajasthan, Kashmir, and Himachal Pradesh. Its flowers are harvested at dawn before full opening to preserve volatile oil content. The rose hip (fruit), petals, seeds, leaves, and root bark are all employed medicinally, with fresh and dried petals and rosewater forming the backbone of classical preparations.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

The chemistry of Rosa damascena is among the most complex in the plant kingdom — over 400 volatile compounds have been identified in rose essential oil, making it one of the most intricate natural perfumes. The primary constituents are citronellol (18–55%), geraniol (10–22%), nerol, linalool, and phenylethanol (rose alcohol) — the compound most responsible for the characteristic rose fragrance. The petals are rich in polyphenols including quercetin, kaempferol, gallic acid, and ellagic acid. Rose hips are an extraordinarily concentrated source of vitamin C (up to 1,700 mg/100 g dried — one of the highest in the plant kingdom), along with carotenoids, tocopherols, and essential fatty acids.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Rose is prominently placed in the Bhavaprakasha Nighantu (Pushpa Varga — flower group), Dhanvantari Nighantu, Raja Nighantu, and Shaligrama Nighantu. It is the quintessential Hridya dravya — a substance that gladdens and strengthens the heart — and its cooling, sweet, and mildly astringent properties make it the primary Pitta-pacifying flower in classical Ayurveda. Shatapatra is used in a wide range of classical formulations including Gulkand (rose petal jam), Arq-e-Gulab (rosewater), and many compound preparations for the heart, skin, and mind.

Classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha: "Shatapatra madhuram sheetam, Pittaharam hridyam param, Chakshuya varnya cha shramam, Dahashoshartikrut nahi" — sweet, cooling, supremely Pitta-pacifying and heart-nourishing, benefiting the eyes and complexion, relieving fatigue, burning, and wasting. Its fragrance (gandha) alone is considered therapeutically active — a unique recognition that olfactory medicine (aromatherapy) had its roots in classical Ayurveda.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has validated rose's classical reputation across multiple domains — anxiolytic, antidepressant, cardioprotective, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and skin-regenerative effects are all supported by preclinical and clinical evidence. Its aromatherapy applications have the strongest clinical backing of any plant fragrance.

Heart Tonic & Cardioprotective
As the supreme Hridya dravya, rose strengthens cardiac function and soothes palpitations, anxiety-related tachycardia, and emotionally driven heart distress. Quercetin and anthocyanins reduce LDL oxidation, improve endothelial function, and reduce platelet aggregation. Gulkand is a classical daily heart tonic. Rose water reduces stress-induced cortisol spikes implicated in cardiac risk.
Anxiety, Stress & Mood
The most robustly evidenced benefit of rose aromatherapy — multiple RCTs confirm that inhaled rose essential oil significantly reduces anxiety scores, salivary cortisol, blood pressure, and heart rate compared to placebo. Phenylethanol and linalool modulate GABA-A receptors, producing anxiolytic and antidepressant effects. Used in labour anxiety, examination stress, and post-partum depression.
Skin Health & Complexion
Classical Varnya (complexion-enhancing) and Tvachya (skin-nourishing) — rosewater is a classical face toner, reducing redness, tightening pores, and balancing skin pH. Rosehip seed oil (rich in linoleic and α-linolenic acids) clinically demonstrated to reduce post-surgical scarring, melasma, and fine lines. Anti-inflammatory polyphenols soothe eczema, rosacea, and Pitta-type inflammatory dermatoses.
Eye Care & Vision
A premier Chakshuya dravya — rosewater eye drops are a classical Ayurvedic treatment for conjunctivitis, eye fatigue, burning eyes, and mild inflammations. Clinical studies validate rosewater's efficacy in reducing ocular surface inflammation and dryness. The astringent tannins reduce redness; the cooling sheeta virya soothes burning; antimicrobial properties inhibit ocular bacterial growth.
Digestive & Carminative
Gulkand (rose petal preserve with sugar) is one of Ayurveda's most beloved digestive tonics — cooling acid reflux, reducing oral ulcers, relieving constipation from excess Pitta-heat, and improving appetite. Rose petal decoction is used for mouth ulcers, gastritis, and peptic disorders. Mild laxative action from tannins and the cooling effect on the enteric nervous system reduce colonic spasm.
Antimicrobial & Antiviral
Rose essential oil and aqueous extracts show activity against S. aureus, E. coli, Candida albicans, and HSV-1 (herpes simplex). Gallic and ellagic acids are potent antimicrobials with activity against drug-resistant strains. Rosewater is used as a topical antimicrobial rinse for wounds, minor burns, and infected skin conditions in classical and folk traditions.
Menstrual & Reproductive Health
Classical Artava-janana (menstrual normaliser) — rose regulates and initiates healthy menstrual flow, reduces dysmenorrhoea, and soothes PMS-related irritability and heat. Multiple RCTs confirm rose aromatherapy and oral preparations significantly reduce menstrual pain intensity. Used in leucorrhoea, excessive uterine bleeding, and menopausal hot flushes in classical Ayurvedic and Unani practice.
Immune & Antioxidant
Rose hips are one of the most vitamin-C-dense foods known (up to 1,700 mg/100 g dried) — historically used across Asia and Europe to prevent scurvy and infections. Modern studies confirm rosehip powder's anti-inflammatory effects in rheumatoid arthritis through GCSF inhibition. Rich anthocyanin and carotenoid content provide broad-spectrum antioxidant protection against oxidative stress.
05

Rose in Unani & Cultural Traditions

In Unani medicine, the rose — Gul-e-Surkh — is a Mufarreh (exhilarant), Muqawwi-e-Qalb (heart strengthener), and Muqawwi-e-Dimagh (brain tonic). Classical Unani preparations include Arq-e-Gulab (distilled rosewater), Gulkand (rose petal electuary), Rooh Afza (traditional cooler), Majun-e-Baladur, and Qurs-e-Gulab (rose tablets). Ibn Sina (Avicenna) in his Canon of Medicine devotes extensive treatment to rose, naming it a remedy for cardiac palpitations, depression, and nervous debility.

In Siddha medicine, Roja is used for Pitta-related disorders, skin conditions, and as a cooling nervine. In Hindu ritual, the rose is offered to Lakshmi and used in Sufi devotional traditions as the fragrance of the divine. The Mughal attar industry of Kannauj — established by Empress Noor Jahan — still produces rose attar by traditional deg-bhapka hydro-distillation, where steam from simmering rose petals is passed through sandalwood oil to capture the volatile fragrance. One kilogram of rose attar requires 3–5 tonnes of petals harvested at dawn — making genuine rose attar among the most precious botanical substances on earth.

How to Use Rose / Gulab

Rose is one of the safest and most versatile medicinal plants — suitable for internal, external, aromatic, and culinary use across all ages. Its gentle, cooling, and nourishing nature means it can be used regularly without the strict dosing constraints of more potent herbs. Always use food-grade, unsprayed rose petals for internal preparations, and ensure rosewater is 100% pure distillate rather than synthetic fragrance dilution.

Safety & Precautions

Rose is one of the gentlest and safest herbs in the Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia — its mild nature and long culinary history mean it is generally suitable for all ages, constitutions, and life stages. Serious adverse effects are rare and mostly limited to allergic responses or misuse of concentrated essential oil.

  • Rose essential oil — never undiluted on skin: Pure rose attar or essential oil is highly concentrated and must always be diluted to 1–2% in a carrier oil before topical application. Undiluted application can cause contact sensitisation and chemical burns in sensitive individuals.
  • Synthetic rosewater: Most commercially available "rose water" is synthetic fragrance (phenylethanol in water) with no medicinal value. Always verify it is genuine steam-distilled or hydro-distilled rose hydrosol for therapeutic use.
  • Gulkand in diabetes / Kapha excess: Gulkand contains substantial sugar — avoid or restrict in diabetes mellitus, obesity, and heavy Kapha presentations. Sugarless preparations (dried petal powder, rose tea) are preferable in these cases.
  • Pesticide contamination: Commercially grown ornamental roses are among the most heavily pesticide-treated crops globally. Never use florist or garden centre roses for internal preparations — use only certified organic, food-grade, unsprayed roses.
  • Rose hip seeds: The small seeds inside rose hips are covered in tiny irritating hairs that can cause mucous membrane irritation — always strain rose hip preparations thoroughly through a fine mesh before consumption.
  • Pregnancy: Rose petal tea and rosewater at normal culinary amounts are generally considered safe. Therapeutic-dose preparations and concentrated essential oil should be discussed with a practitioner during pregnancy, as high-dose rose oil has historical use as a uterine stimulant.
  • Eye drops: Use only pure, sterile, preservative-free rosewater in eyes. Never apply essential oil near the eyes. Ensure rosewater is freshly opened or freshly prepared; contaminated rosewater can cause eye infections.
  • Drug interactions: Rose at normal food/tea doses has no clinically significant interactions. Concentrated rosehip extract at high doses may theoretically potentiate anticoagulants due to vitamin C and salicylate-like compounds — caution with warfarin at high therapeutic doses.
