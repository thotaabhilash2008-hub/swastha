Entry No. 11
Akhrot
Walnut — The King of Nuts
Juglans regia  ·  Persian Walnut  ·  English Walnut

Fig. 1 — Ripe walnuts in their green outer husk on the Juglans regia tree  ·  Fig. 2 — Shelled walnut kernels showing the characteristic brain-like bilobed form

❧

Revered in Sanskrit as Akshota — "the nut that is eaten with pleasure" — the walnut has graced Ayurvedic texts, Persian royal courts, and Greek medical traditions alike. Pliny the Elder called it Juglans regia, "Jupiter's royal nut," a name that endures. From the snow-touched valleys of Kashmir to the apothecaries of ancient Rome, Akhrot has been prized as a food, a medicine, a dye, and a cosmetic — a tree that gives generously from root to leaf to fruit.

01

Botanical Profile

Juglans regia is a large, long-lived deciduous tree of the Juglandaceae family, native to the mountain ranges stretching from the Balkans through Central Asia to the Himalayas and western China. In India it grows abundantly in Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand, typically at altitudes between 1,200 and 3,300 metres. Mature trees reach 25–35 metres in height with a broad spreading crown and deeply furrowed grey bark.

The compound leaves are pinnate with 5–9 leaflets, aromatic when crushed due to juglone content. Male flowers appear as pendulous catkins; female flowers form small terminal spikes. The fruit is a drupe with a fleshy green outer husk (exocarp) encasing a hard, deeply ridged shell (endocarp) within which sits the wrinkled, bilobed kernel — the edible walnut. The tree is notably allelopathic: juglone secreted from its roots inhibits the growth of many competing plants beneath its canopy.

02

Phytochemistry & Nutritional Composition

The walnut kernel is one of the most nutritionally complete foods in the plant kingdom — delivering a rare convergence of omega-3 fatty acids, tocopherols, polyphenols, melatonin, and a full spectrum of essential minerals. Per 100 g of dried kernel, it provides approximately 654 kcal, 65 g total fat (of which ~13 g is α-linolenic acid, the plant omega-3), 15 g protein, and 7 g dietary fibre.

Beyond the kernel, the green husk is extraordinarily rich in juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone) — a potent antimicrobial, antifungal, and cytotoxic compound. The leaves yield flavonoids, tannins, and essential oil; the bark and root bark contain tannins, juglone, and triterpenes including oleanolic acid. Walnut oil provides an exceptionally favourable omega-6:omega-3 ratio (~4:1) compared to most dietary oils.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Akhrot is described in the Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, and Ashtanga Hridayam. Charaka classifies it as a Balya (strength-promoting) and Brimhana (nourishing/anabolic) dravya. Sushruta uses the bark in wound-healing preparations and the oil in Snehana (oleation therapy). Bhavaprakasha Nighantu groups Akhrot in the Amradi Phala Varga (group of dry fruits) and praises it as a Vata-shamaka and Shukrala (spermatogenic) dravya.

The classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha — "Akshotah snigdho balyah pittalo vatanashanah, shukralo guru vrushyo cha vishodhano rasayanah" — describes Akhrot as unctuous, strength-giving, mildly Pitta-increasing, Vata-pacifying, spermatogenic, heavy, aphrodisiac, purifying, and rasayana (rejuvenative).

04

Key Health Benefits

Among all tree nuts, the walnut is uniquely positioned at the intersection of Ayurvedic rasayana tradition and modern evidence-based nutrition. Its benefits span cardiovascular medicine, neuroscience, oncology, endocrinology, and dermatology — validated by hundreds of peer-reviewed human clinical trials and epidemiological studies.

Cardiovascular Protection
Walnuts are the only nut significantly rich in ALA omega-3, which reduces LDL cholesterol, lowers triglycerides, improves endothelial function, and reduces systemic inflammation. Multiple RCTs and the landmark PREDIMED-Plus trial confirm walnut consumption significantly reduces cardiovascular disease risk.
Brain & Cognitive Health
The doctrine of signatures holds that the brain-shaped kernel nourishes the brain — remarkably, science agrees. Walnuts supply DHA precursors (ALA), melatonin, polyphenols, and folate that slow cognitive decline. Animal studies show reversal of age-related brain inflammation; human studies link walnut intake to better memory and processing speed.
Anti-inflammatory & Antioxidant
Ellagitannins in walnuts are converted by gut microbiota to urolithins — metabolites with potent anti-inflammatory and mitophagy-promoting effects. Regular consumption significantly lowers hs-CRP, IL-6, and other inflammatory markers, making walnuts a functional food against chronic low-grade inflammation.
Blood Sugar & Diabetes Management
Despite high caloric density, walnuts have a low glycemic index and have been shown to improve insulin sensitivity and fasting glucose levels. The PREDIMED study associated regular walnut consumption with a 50% reduction in type 2 diabetes risk; their fibre and polyphenol content modulates post-meal glucose spikes.
Gut Microbiome Support
A 2018 human RCT demonstrated that daily walnut consumption for 8 weeks significantly altered the gut microbiome — increasing Lactobacillus, Ruminococcaceae, and butyrate-producing species while reducing secondary bile acid levels linked to colon cancer risk. Walnuts act as a prebiotic for beneficial bacteria.
Reproductive & Hormonal Health
Classical Ayurveda classifies Akhrot as Shukrala (spermatogenic). Modern studies validate this: a 12-week walnut-supplemented diet in young men significantly improved sperm vitality, motility, and morphology, attributed to ALA, selenium, and antioxidants protecting against sperm lipid peroxidation.
Weight & Satiety Management
Despite high caloric density, regular walnut eaters do not gain weight. fMRI studies show walnut consumption activates the right insula — the brain region governing food cue awareness — increasing satiety and reducing cravings for highly processed foods. Walnut protein and fibre blunt hunger hormones effectively.
Antimicrobial (Husk & Leaves)
Juglone from the green husk and leaf extracts demonstrates potent activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, Herpes simplex virus, and intestinal parasites. Traditionally the husk paste was used for fungal skin infections, ringworm, and as a natural anthelmintic in both Ayurvedic and Unani traditions.
05

How to Consume Akhrot

Akhrot is one of the most versatile medicinal foods — consumed raw, soaked, as oil, in classical ghee preparations, and as part of herbal formulations. Ayurveda recommends soaking overnight to reduce tannin content and improve digestibility. The general therapeutic dose is 2–5 kernels (20–30 g) daily; exceeding this regularly may increase Kapha and Pitta.

Safety & Precautions

Akhrot is a safe, well-tolerated food-medicine when consumed at appropriate quantities. However, its Guru (heavy), Snigdha (unctuous), and Ushna (hot) properties make excess consumption problematic for certain constitutional types and conditions.

  • Allergy: Tree nut allergy to walnut can be severe and life-threatening (anaphylaxis). Individuals with known tree nut allergy must avoid all walnut products including oil. Cross-reactivity with other tree nuts is possible.
  • Kapha-dominant constitutions: Those with obesity, sluggish digestion, hyperlipidemia, or excess mucus conditions should strictly limit to 1–2 kernels daily, preferably with black pepper or ginger to stimulate digestive fire (Agni).
  • Pitta aggravation: Ushna virya may worsen acidity, skin rashes, and inflammatory Pitta conditions if consumed in excess. Those with hyperacidity or peptic ulcers should consume soaked (not raw) kernels in moderation.
  • Husk contact — skin staining: The green husk contains juglone which permanently stains skin brownish-black. Wear gloves when handling fresh walnut hulls. The stain fades over 1–2 weeks but cannot be washed off immediately.
  • Warfarin / blood thinning: High ALA and vitamin E content may theoretically potentiate anticoagulants. Individuals on warfarin or other blood thinners should consume consistent amounts and inform their physician.
  • Pregnancy: Moderate kernel consumption is safe and nutritious. Avoid topical husk preparations and large amounts of leaf/bark decoctions during pregnancy as safety data is limited.
  • Oxalate content: Walnuts contain moderate oxalates. Individuals prone to kidney stones (calcium oxalate type) should limit intake to 1–2 kernels daily and ensure adequate hydration.
  • Digestive sensitivity: Raw walnut tannins may cause nausea or stomach discomfort in sensitive individuals. Always prefer soaked and skin-peeled kernels for better tolerance and enhanced bioavailability.
