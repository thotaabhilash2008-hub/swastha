Its Sanskrit name Tanduliyaka — "resembling rice grains" — hints at the ancient recognition of amaranth's seed as a grain-like superfood, yet this humble roadside weed has been simultaneously one of Ayurveda's most valued leafy medicinal vegetables, a diuretic, haemostatic, anti-diarrhoeal, and cooling agent documented by Charaka, Sushruta, and Vagbhata across three millennia of unbroken clinical tradition.

01

Botanical Profile

Tanduliyaka refers primarily to Amaranthus spinosus L. (spiny amaranth) in Ayurvedic classical texts, though A. tricolor (Chinese spinach / garden amaranth) and A. viridis (slender amaranth) are recognised as functionally related and therapeutically interchangeable in many contexts. The genus Amaranthus comprises about 70 species of annual or short-lived perennial herbs in the family Amaranthaceae — now reclassified to include the former Chenopodiaceae, making amaranth a botanical relative of beet, quinoa, and spinach.

Amaranthus spinosus is an erect, much-branched herb growing to 30–100 cm, identified by the distinctive paired spines at each leaf axil from which it derives both its species name and folk caution. Stems are green to reddish-purple; leaves are ovate to rhombic, glabrous, with long petioles. Flowers are tiny, borne in dense terminal and axillary spikes. Seeds are minute, lens-shaped, shining black or dark red — and in the grain varieties, creamy-white — harvested in enormous quantities from the dense floral spikes. A. tricolor is grown as a leaf vegetable and ornamental, prized for its brilliant red, yellow, and green foliaged forms.

02

Phytochemical Composition

Amaranth's chemical complexity varies significantly by plant part. The leaves are exceptional sources of vitamin K, vitamin A (as β-carotene), vitamin C, folate, calcium, iron, and manganese — nutritional densities that rival or exceed spinach and kale. The seeds are one of the very few plant sources of complete protein, containing all essential amino acids including lysine (typically limiting in cereals), with a protein content of 13–17% and a protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score (PDCAAS) approaching that of milk.

The medicinal roots of A. spinosus are rich in betacyanins (the red-violet betalain pigments), saponins, tannins, and alkaloids including amaranthine. The leaves contain rutin, quercetin, kaempferol glycosides, and oxalic acid (a consideration for those with kidney stones). Squalene — an important triterpene with antioxidant and immunostimulant properties — is notably concentrated in amaranth seed oil at levels (5–8%) far exceeding shark liver oil, the traditional commercial source.
