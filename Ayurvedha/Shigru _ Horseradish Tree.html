Horseradish Tree — Moringa, the Miracle Tree
Moringa oleifera Lam.  ·  Family: Moringaceae

Fig. 1 — Feathery tripinnate leaves and cream-white fragrant flowers of Moringa oleifera  ·  Fig. 2 — The iconic long green drumstick pods (Shigru phala) that name the tree across South India

❧

Named Shigru in Sanskrit — from Shighra, meaning swift — for the extraordinary speed with which its healing properties act upon the body, Moringa oleifera is described in Ayurvedic texts as Shobhanjana: the tree that brings beauty and nourishment. Today called the "Miracle Tree" and "Tree of Life" by the WHO and nutrition scientists worldwide, it is one of the most nutritionally dense plants ever studied — with every part of the tree — leaves, pods, flowers, seeds, roots, bark, and gum — serving documented therapeutic purpose.

01

Botanical Profile

Moringa oleifera Lam. is a fast-growing, drought-resistant deciduous tree of the monogeneric family Moringaceae, native to the sub-Himalayan tracts of northwestern India — particularly the foothills of the Shivaliks — and now naturalised across tropical and subtropical Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Caribbean. It typically grows 5–12 m tall, though it can reach 15 m, with a soft, corky, whitish bark and an open, feathery crown. Its tripinnate compound leaves are pale green, delicate, and finely divided into many small, oval leaflets 1–2 cm long — superficially resembling a fern.

The flowers are cream-white, five-petalled, fragrant, and bilaterally symmetrical — 2.5 cm across — borne in large axillary panicles. They are edible and are used in salads and traditional preparations across South and Southeast Asia. The fruit is the unmistakable drumstick — a pendulous, ribbed, three-sided pod 20–45 cm long, green turning brown when dry, containing 10–20 winged seeds embedded in a soft pithy core. The seeds yield a high-quality, non-drying ben oil (25–40% oil content) used in cooking, cosmetics, and as a lubricant. The root smells and tastes strongly of horseradish, giving the tree one of its most common English names.

02

Nutritional & Phytochemical Composition

Moringa leaves are among the most nutrient-dense foods ever analysed — containing significant quantities of vitamins A, B, C, E, and K; minerals including calcium, iron, potassium, magnesium, and zinc; all nine essential amino acids; and a dense matrix of bioactive phytochemicals. Gram for gram, fresh moringa leaves contain more vitamin C than oranges, more calcium than milk, more iron than spinach, and more potassium than bananas. The dried leaf powder — retaining most micronutrients — has been endorsed by the WHO, UNICEF, and WHO-affiliated NGOs as a food supplement for combating childhood malnutrition in tropical regions.

The defining bioactive compounds of Moringa are its isothiocyanates — particularly moringin (4-alpha-L-rhamnosyloxy benzyl isothiocyanate) — unique glucosinolate breakdown products found almost exclusively in this genus, responsible for much of its anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and antimicrobial activity. The leaves also contain exceptional levels of the antioxidant flavonoids quercetin and kaempferol, zeatin (a cytokinin plant hormone with anti-ageing properties), and the phenolic acid chlorogenic acid.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Shigru is mentioned prominently across all the principal classical texts of Ayurveda. Charaka places Shigru among the Shvasahara dravyas (respiratory-relieving herbs) in the Sutrasthana, and in the Chikitsasthana uses it extensively in the treatment of Shotha (oedema/inflammation), Gulma (abdominal tumours), and Vatarakta (gout). Sushruta includes Shigru in the Vidarigandhadi gana and the Shirovirechana (errhine) preparations. The Ashtanga Hridayam of Vagbhata devotes specific formulations to Shigru in the contexts of splenic enlargement, intestinal worms, and eye disorders.

Bhavaprakasha Nighantu describes Shigru under the Haritakya-adi Varga, calling it Teekshnagandha (sharp-smelling) and Shobhanjana (that which beautifies). Its classical karma includes: Deepana (kindling digestive fire), Pachana (digestive), Krimighna (anti-parasitic), Shothahara (anti-oedematous), Chakshushya (beneficial to eyes), Hridya (cardiac tonic), and Shukrala (semen-augmenting). The root's pungency, compared to horseradish, is classified as a powerful Shirovirechana (nasal purgative) — used to clear sinuses and cranial congestion.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern research on Moringa oleifera has exploded since the early 2000s — with over 1,300 peer-reviewed publications as of 2024. The evidence base is broadening steadily, validating its anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, cardioprotective, and anti-cancer properties, while its nutrition profile has been confirmed by multiple independent laboratory analyses across continents.

Blood Sugar Regulation
Multiple clinical trials confirm significant hypoglycaemic activity of Moringa leaf powder. Chlorogenic acid slows intestinal glucose absorption; isothiocyanates enhance insulin sensitivity. A 2016 RCT found 8g/day of leaf powder reduced fasting blood glucose by 28% over 40 days. Particularly effective as a dietary adjunct in Type 2 diabetes management.
Anti-inflammatory — Whole Body
Moringin (the unique Moringa isothiocyanate) inhibits NF-κB — the master regulator of the inflammatory cascade — more potently than many pharmaceutical anti-inflammatory compounds in cell studies. Quercetin and kaempferol provide additional COX-2 inhibition. Effective across inflammatory conditions: arthritis, bowel inflammation, skin conditions, and metabolic inflammation.
Malnutrition & Micronutrient Support
Endorsed by WHO, UNICEF, and Médecins Sans Frontières as a locally-available, cost-effective intervention for protein-energy malnutrition, iron-deficiency anaemia, and Vitamin A deficiency in tropical regions. A single 25g serving of fresh leaves provides a child's full daily requirement of Vitamins A and C and significant iron and calcium.
Cholesterol & Cardiac Health
Clinical studies — including a landmark Ghanaian RCT — demonstrate significant reductions in total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides with regular Moringa leaf consumption, alongside maintenance or improvement of HDL. Beta-sitosterol and isothiocyanates contribute to this lipid-modulating effect. The Ashtanga Hridayam's classification as Hridya (cardiac tonic) appears well-founded.
Neuroprotection & Cognitive Function
Moringin has shown neuroprotective effects against oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and excitotoxicity in neurodegenerative disease models. Animal studies demonstrate improvement in spatial memory and reduction of amyloid beta deposition. Zeatin's cell-regenerative cytokinin activity may contribute to anti-ageing brain effects. Research in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's prevention is ongoing.
Antimicrobial & Water Purification
Pterygospermin — isolated from roots and seeds — is one of the most potent plant-derived bactericidal compounds known, effective against drug-resistant Staphylococcus, E. coli, Vibrio cholerae, and Salmonella. Crushed Moringa seeds are used as a bioflocculent to clarify turbid drinking water — reducing bacterial load by 90–99% — a WHO-documented application in rural water treatment.
Lactation & Maternal Nutrition
Moringa is the most widely studied galactagogue (milk-enhancing herb) in modern clinical research, with multiple RCTs showing significant increases in breast milk volume (up to 176% in Filipino trials) and improved milk iron, calcium, and protein content. In Ayurveda, Shigru's Shukrala and nutritive properties support postnatal maternal replenishment and infant nutrition simultaneously.
Anti-cancer & Chemoprotective
Niazimicin — isolated from Moringa seeds — was identified as a potent inhibitor of tumour promotion in the Epstein-Barr virus assay, one of the gold-standard cancer chemopreventive screening tests. Isothiocyanates induce Phase II detoxification enzymes, enhance apoptosis in cancer cell lines, and inhibit angiogenesis. Research in breast, colorectal, and liver cancers is most advanced.
05

How to Use Shigru

Every part of Moringa is usable, and it is one of the rare plants where culinary and medicinal use are equally well-developed. The leaves are the most studied part and the most versatile — consumed fresh, cooked, or as dried powder. Drumstick pods are a beloved vegetable across South Indian cuisine (especially sambar). Flowers are edible and mildly medicinal. Seeds yield ben oil, prized in cosmetics. The root should be used with caution due to its higher concentration of spirochin, a potentially toxic alkaloid at high doses.
