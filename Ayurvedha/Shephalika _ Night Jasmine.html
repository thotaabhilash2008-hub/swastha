Shephalika
Night Jasmine — Parijata, the Flower of the Gods
Nyctanthes arbor-tristis L.  ·  Family: Oleaceae

Fig. 1 — Star-white flowers of Parijata with their signature saffron-orange tube, fallen at dawn beneath the tree  ·  Fig. 2 — Nyctanthes arbor-tristis in bloom, adorning the night with fragrance

❧

Named Parijata in Sanskrit — the celestial tree said to have emerged from the Churning of the Cosmic Ocean and brought to earth by Krishna — Shephalika blooms only at night, carpeting the ground with white star-flowers at dawn. Called Arbor Tristis ("the sad tree") by Linnaeus because it sheds its blossoms with the morning light, this poetic plant carries as rich a therapeutic legacy as it does a mythological one — honoured across Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, and folk traditions for its anti-malarial, anti-arthritic, and immunomodulatory properties.

01

Botanical Profile

Nyctanthes arbor-tristis L. is a large, deciduous shrub or small tree of the family Oleaceae (the olive family), growing 10–30 feet tall with a gnarled, flaky bark and quadrangular young branches. Its leaves are opposite, ovate to elliptic, 5–12 cm long, rough and scabrous to the touch — a texture likened to sandpaper in folk memory. The tree is native to the foothills of the Himalayas, the plains of the Indian subcontinent, and Southeast Asia, and is commonly found in gardens, temple courtyards, and forest edges from the plains up to 1,500 m elevation.

Its flowers are among the most distinctive in the botanical world: intensely fragrant, pure white with five to eight flat petals radiating around a vivid saffron-orange corolla tube — nature's own star design. They open after sundown, release their fragrance through the night, and fall at the first touch of dawn. The saffron-orange tube yields a natural dye (nyctanthin) used to colour silk and used as a substitute for saffron in Buddhist robes across Southeast Asia. The fruit is a heart-shaped, flat capsule containing two seeds. Shephalika is the state flower of West Bengal and Kanchanaburi province, Thailand.

Phytochemical Composition

Phytochemical investigations of Nyctanthes arbor-tristis have revealed a rich and diverse matrix of bioactive compounds across its different plant parts. The leaves are the most pharmacologically studied and contain iridoid glycosides, flavonoids, tannins, and essential oils. The flowers yield volatile aromatic compounds responsible for the characteristic nocturnal fragrance, while the orange corolla tube contains the carotenoid glycoside nyctanthin. The seeds contain a fixed oil rich in palmitic, linoleic, and oleic acids, along with a toxic glycoside called arbortristoside.

Ayurvedic Significance

Parijata holds a position of extraordinary reverence in both classical Ayurveda and Hindu cosmology. Mythologically, it is one of the five celestial trees (Panchavriksha) of Indra's paradise — Swarga — obtained during the Samudra Manthan (Churning of the Cosmic Ocean). In the Vishnu Purana, Krishna brings the Parijata tree to earth at Satyabhama's request, establishing a direct divine lineage for its presence in domestic gardens.

In classical texts, Bhavaprakasha Nighantu includes Shephalika under the Pushpa Varga (flower group) and highlights its bitter, pungent taste and hot potency. The Charaka Samhita references Parijata leaves in formulations for Sandhi Vata (joint disorders). Sushruta uses the flowers in aromatic preparations. Dhanvantari Nighantu classifies it as Jwaraghna (fever-reducing) and Krimighna (anti-parasitic). The leaves' specific action against Amavata (rheumatoid arthritis) and Kaphaj Jwara (febrile conditions with Kapha dominance) is well-documented in Ayurvedic clinical literature.

04

Key Health Benefits

Shephalika has been the subject of extensive pharmacological research since the 1980s, with studies from India, Thailand, Bangladesh, and Japan validating its most celebrated traditional applications — particularly its anti-malarial iridoids, its arthritis-relieving leaf extract, and its broad-spectrum immunomodulatory activity.

Anti-malarial & Anti-parasitic
The iridoid glycosides arbortristoside A, B, and C are the plant's signature anti-malarial compounds. In vitro studies demonstrate significant inhibition of Plasmodium falciparum growth. The leaf decoction has been used across folk traditions of India, Bangladesh, and SE Asia as a traditional anti-malarial, validated by multiple pharmacological screenings since Biswas et al. (1990).
Arthritis & Joint Pain Relief
This is Shephalika's most celebrated clinical application in Ayurveda. Leaf extract significantly suppresses carrageenan-induced paw edema, collagen-induced arthritis models, and prostaglandin-mediated inflammation in animal studies. The traditional preparation — leaf decoction taken twice daily — is widely used in rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and sciatica across the Indian subcontinent.
Antipyretic & Febrifuge
Classical texts classify Parijata as Jwaraghna — fever-destroying. Modern studies confirm significant antipyretic activity of leaf and bark extracts comparable to paracetamol in animal fever models. Particularly effective for the intermittent fevers of malarial and Kapha-type origin. A traditional preparation uses 10–15 leaves boiled in water with black pepper and ginger.
Immunomodulation & Antiviral
Leaf extracts of Nyctanthes arbor-tristis have demonstrated significant immunostimulant activity — increasing macrophage phagocytosis, NK cell activity, and antibody titre in experimental models. Arbortristoside C has shown antiviral activity against Herpes simplex virus type 2. The plant is included in traditional anti-dengue and chikungunya supportive formulations across South and SE Asia.
Antifungal & Antimicrobial
Both leaf and flower extracts show broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Salmonella typhi, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and the dermatophytes Trichophyton rubrum and Microsporum canis — supporting traditional topical applications for skin infections, ringworm, and scabies.
Hepatoprotective
Oleanolic acid and the iridoid glycosides in the leaves have demonstrated hepatoprotective activity against carbon tetrachloride and paracetamol-induced hepatotoxicity in animal models — reducing liver enzyme elevation (AST, ALT) and supporting hepatic regeneration. Used in folk medicine for jaundice and liver disorders in Bihar and Bengal.
Anxiolytic & Sedative (Flowers)
The volatile aromatic compounds of Parijata flowers — inhaled through the night as the blooms release their fragrance — have documented anxiolytic and mild sedative effects in animal studies. In Ayurvedic practice, sleeping near a blooming Parijata tree or placing fresh flowers in the bedroom is a traditional remedy for insomnia and anxiety (Chitta Udvega).
Natural Dye & Skin Benefit (Flower Tube)
The vivid saffron-orange corolla tube contains the carotenoid nyctanthin — a natural dye traditionally used to colour silk, Buddhist robes, and as a saffron substitute. Nyctanthin carries antioxidant activity and is studied for potential photoprotective and anti-inflammatory applications in cosmetic formulations targeting oxidative skin stress.

How to Use Shephalika

Different parts of the Parijata tree are used in different preparations. The leaves are the principal medicinal organ, used primarily as decoctions and fresh juice for internal therapeutic purposes. The flowers are fragrant, gentle, and suited to external, aromatic, and mildly therapeutic uses. Seeds contain a toxic glycoside (arbortristoside) and should never be consumed without expert preparation. All internal preparations should be physician-supervised.

Safety & Precautions

Shephalika is a well-tolerated medicinal plant at therapeutic leaf-preparation doses used in traditional practice. However, certain parts of the plant — particularly the seeds — carry documented toxicity, and several populations require caution with internal preparations.

  • Seed toxicity — strictly avoid: Seeds contain arbortristoside, a compound with documented cytotoxic and abortifacient properties. Seeds must never be consumed internally without expert pharmaceutical processing. This is a firm contraindication.
  • Pregnancy: Leaf preparations at medicinal doses are contraindicated in pregnancy due to uterotonic and potential abortifacient activity documented in classical texts and some pharmacological studies. Aromatic flower use in the environment is generally considered safe.
  • Children under 5: Internal leaf preparations should be avoided in very young children due to the bitterness and potency of iridoid glycosides. Topical leaf paste use is generally safe.
  • Autoimmune conditions on immunosuppressants: Shephalika's immunostimulant activity may theoretically counteract immunosuppressive drugs (methotrexate, cyclosporine) used in autoimmune conditions. Consult a physician before therapeutic use in such cases.
  • Anti-malarial drug interactions: While the plant supports anti-malarial action, it should complement — never replace — prescribed anti-malarial treatment. Interaction with quinine derivatives is unstudied.
  • Pitta-dominant individuals (leaves): Although flowers are cooling, the leaves have ushna virya. Individuals with high Pitta, hyperacidity, or bleeding disorders should use leaf preparations cautiously and preferably with cooling adjuvants like coriander or coconut water.
  • Extended use: Long-term continuous use beyond 4–6 weeks of the leaf decoction is not extensively studied. Periodic breaks are recommended in classical practice. Monitor liver function with prolonged use.
  • Fragrance sensitivity: Although the nocturnal fragrance is valued as anxiolytic, individuals with fragrance allergies or asthma may find concentrated Parijata blooms in closed spaces irritating. Ventilate adequately.
