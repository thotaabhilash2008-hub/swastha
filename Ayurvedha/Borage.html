Borage
Starflower — The Herb of Courage
Borago officinalis  ·  Boraginaceae

Fig. 1 — The signature five-pointed sapphire-blue Borage flowers, the very source of its English name "starflower"  ·  Fig. 2 — Mucilaginous hairy leaves and pendulous flower clusters of Borago officinalis in midsummer bloom

❧

"Ego Borago, gaudia semper ago" — "I, Borage, bring courage always" — wrote the medieval herbalists. Roman soldiers infused its leaves in wine before battle; Celtic warriors believed it conferred bravery; Pliny called it euphrosinum, the herb of joy. A Mediterranean traveller now naturalised in India, it carries the highest plant-source concentration of gamma-linolenic acid known to nutrition science — the rare omega-6 that bridges ancient courage and modern dermatology.

01

Botanical Profile

Borage (Borago officinalis L.) is an annual to short-lived biennial herb of the Boraginaceae family — the forget-me-not family — reaching 60–100 cm in height with a sturdy, hollow, succulent stem covered in characteristic short, white, prickly hairs. Leaves are alternate, simple, broadly ovate to oblong-lanceolate, 5–15 cm long, dark green, with a wrinkled surface and prominent veining; both leaves and stem are covered in stiff, bristly hairs that give the plant a distinctively rough, hairy texture.

The signature flowers are stunning — five-pointed, sapphire-blue (occasionally pink or white), star-shaped, with prominent black anthers forming a black cone at the centre — earning the English name "starflower." Each flower hangs pendulously in loose terminal cymes from May to September. The plant has a pleasant cucumber-like aroma and flavour. Native to the Mediterranean (Syria, Palestine, North Africa) and now cultivated worldwide, Borage was introduced to India through colonial-era plant exchange and has since naturalised across temperate India and the Western Ghats. Significantly, Borage is not mentioned in the classical Ayurvedic samhitas of Charaka, Sushruta, or Vagbhata — it is a recent botanical immigrant, integrated into modern Indian and pan-traditional herbal practice over the past few centuries.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

Borage's pharmacological reputation rests almost entirely on one extraordinary compound: gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) — also known as all-cis-6,9,12-octadecatrienoic acid. The seeds yield 28–38% fixed oil, and that oil contains 17–25% GLA — the highest plant-source concentration known to nutrition science (compared to 8–10% in evening primrose oil). GLA is an intermediate metabolite that the body converts into prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) and other anti-inflammatory eicosanoids — making it functionally similar to fish oil's EPA/DHA but plant-derived.

Beyond GLA, Borage contains stearidonic acid (another rare omega-3), oleic acid, palmitic acid, mucilage (giving leaves their characteristic slippery feel), tannins, saponins, the phenolic acids rosmarinic acid and syringic acid, choline, allantoin, and trace amounts of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) — the latter being the basis of significant safety considerations in modern toxicology. Reputable manufacturers now produce PA-free borage seed oil through molecular distillation, which is the only form recommended for therapeutic use.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Borage is not mentioned in the classical Ayurvedic samhitas — it arrived in India during colonial times and has since been integrated into modern Ayurvedic practice using descriptive principles rather than scriptural authority. Modern Ayurvedic interpretation classifies Borage based on its observed properties: rasa (taste) sweet and slightly bitter, guna (quality) light and slightly unctuous (from mucilage), vipaka (post-digestive) sweet, and veerya (potency) cooling.

In modern pan-traditional Indian herbal practice, Borage is recognised as a Pitta-shamaka (Pitta-pacifying) and gentle Vata-anuloma (Vata-balancing) herb — useful in conditions of heat, inflammation, dryness, and nervous burnout. It is sometimes combined with Yashtimadhu (Glycyrrhiza glabra), Tulsi, or Anantmool for skin disorders, respiratory issues, and stress-related conditions. Note: classical European herbalism positions Borage as warming and uplifting, while the Ayurvedic interpretation reads its mucilage and cooling phytochemistry as Pitta-pacifying — the apparent contradiction reflects different traditional frameworks rather than a factual disagreement.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern clinical research on Borage focuses heavily on the seed oil's GLA content. Most documented benefits relate to inflammatory and dermatological conditions, with smaller bodies of evidence emerging for adrenal support, respiratory health, and nervous-system applications. Effects vary widely between individuals — some studies show meaningful improvement, others show minimal effect, suggesting that Borage works best as an adjunct rather than a primary treatment.

Atopic Dermatitis & Eczema
The most studied modern application. A 2008 trial showed measurable symptom-score improvements after 12 weeks of GLA-rich oil supplementation. Some confirmatory studies report up to 30% reduction in eczema severity. Useful in atopic dermatitis, dry skin, and barrier-damaged skin conditions.
Rheumatoid Arthritis & Joint
A 2015 meta-analysis in the International Journal of Rheumatic Diseases concluded that borage oil may serve as a useful adjunct for joint stiffness in rheumatoid arthritis, though effect sizes vary. GLA's anti-inflammatory eicosanoid pathway provides the mechanism — particularly useful as part of multimodal therapy.
Allergic Rhinitis & Histamine
A pilot trial (n=40) showed modest reductions in sneezing frequency and nasal congestion over four weeks of borage seed oil supplementation. Rosmarinic acid's antihistamine properties support traditional use in seasonal allergies, urticaria, and allergic skin conditions.
Adrenal Support & Burnout
Traditional Western herbalism uses Borage for adrenal cortex regeneration in chronic stress, burnout, and steroid withdrawal. Lucy Jones in A Working Herbal Dispensary documents this clinical use. Anecdotal but well-established in Western herbal traditions for nervous-system strain.
Respiratory & Expectorant
Mucilaginous leaves act as a gentle demulcent and expectorant, soothing dry cough, sore throat, and bronchial irritation. Borage tea with honey is a traditional remedy for cold, cough, and chest congestion, particularly for Pitta-driven respiratory symptoms.
Menopausal & Hormonal Support
GLA is a precursor to prostaglandin E1, which has documented role in hormonal balance. Used as adjunct in menopause-related symptoms — hot flushes, mood swings, dryness — and in premenstrual syndrome (PMS), where GLA supplementation has shown meaningful effects in clinical trials.
Cardiovascular & Lipid Support
Emerging research suggests possible benefits on lipid metabolism and arterial health through GLA-derived eicosanoids. Galactagogue properties (lactation support) have also been documented in traditional Western practice. Cardiovascular evidence is preliminary; consult a physician before use as an adjunct to cardiac care.
Mental & Emotional Wellbeing
Pliny's euphrosinum, "the herb of joy" — used since antiquity as a mild mood-lifter and anxiety reliever. Borage tea offers gentle calming action useful for anxious tension, restlessness, and post-stress recovery. The herb's traditional reputation for "courage" predates modern adaptogen frameworks.

How to Consume Borage

Borage crosses food and medicine — fresh leaves and flowers are used in salads, soups, and as edible garnishes (the cucumber-like flavour is much loved in summer drinks), while seed oil supplementation provides therapeutic GLA. Crucially, only PA-free borage seed oil — produced by reputable manufacturers via molecular distillation — should be used internally for therapeutic purposes. Avoid prolonged high-dose consumption of whole leaves due to natural pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA) content.

Safety & Precautions

Borage carries one significant safety consideration that overrides all others: the natural presence of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) in the leaves, stems, and flowers — compounds that are hepatotoxic and potentially carcinogenic with prolonged exposure. Modern reputable borage seed oil is processed to remove PAs via molecular distillation, but raw leaf preparations and unprocessed extracts pose real risk. Source carefully and use thoughtfully.

  • Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs): Borage leaves and other above-ground parts naturally contain PAs, which are hepatotoxic and possibly carcinogenic with chronic high-dose exposure. Only use PA-free certified borage seed oil for therapeutic purposes; avoid prolonged daily consumption of leaf preparations.
  • Pregnancy: Avoid medicinal doses entirely. PAs cross the placenta; potential teratogenic effects; possible uterine-stimulating activity. Even PA-free seed oil should be approached with caution and only under qualified medical supervision.
  • Breastfeeding: PAs pass into breast milk and may cause hepatic injury in infants. Avoid medicinal doses during lactation; small culinary amounts of fresh flowers as garnish are generally acceptable.
  • Liver disease: Strictly avoid in any form of liver disease — hepatitis, cirrhosis, fatty liver, or compromised hepatic function. PAs accumulate selectively in hepatocytes and may worsen liver damage.
  • Bleeding disorders & surgery: Borage seed oil has mild antiplatelet activity. Discontinue at least 2 weeks before scheduled surgery; avoid combining with warfarin, heparin, aspirin, clopidogrel, or other anticoagulants without medical supervision.
  • Seizure disorders: Borage oil may lower seizure threshold and interact with anticonvulsants (phenobarbital, phenytoin). Avoid in epilepsy unless cleared by neurologist.
  • NSAIDs & phenothiazines: Concurrent use with NSAIDs or certain antipsychotics may increase seizure risk. Consult a physician before combining.
  • Children: Avoid internal use in children under 12 due to limited safety data and PA concerns. Topical use of certified PA-free oil for dry skin or eczema is generally considered safe.
