Limonia acidissima  ·  Feronia elephantum  ·  Feronia limonia

❧

Revered in Sanskrit as Kapittha, Dantashekhara (the crown of teeth), and Surabhicchada (the fragrant-leaved), Wood Apple has been honoured across Hindu, Buddhist, and folk traditions for millennia — its stone-hard shell a symbol of protection, its dark aromatic pulp prized in Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita as a premier remedy for digestive ailments, liver disease, and fevers of every kind.

01

Botanical Profile

Kavitha (Limonia acidissima L.) is a slow-growing, deciduous tree of the Rutaceae family — the same family as citrus — native to the dry plains and scrub forests of the Indian subcontinent, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asia. It grows 9–15 m tall with an irregularly spreading crown, armed with sharp axillary spines, and bears pinnate aromatic leaves that release a citrus-like fragrance when crushed.

The fruit — the defining feature of the tree — is a globose berry, 5–9 cm in diameter, with an extremely hard, woody, greyish-brown shell that must be cracked open with a hammer. Within lies a dark brown, sticky, strongly aromatic pulp filled with numerous small seeds and fibrous strands. The pulp is simultaneously astringent, acidic, and sweetly aromatic — a flavour profile unlike any other subtropical fruit. Trees begin fruiting at 15 years; a single mature tree may yield 50–200 fruits per season.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Wood Apple is a phytochemically rich plant with each part — fruit pulp, leaves, bark, gum, and seeds — yielding a distinct and therapeutically active matrix of secondary metabolites. The fruit pulp is notable for its combination of organic acids, coumarins, flavonoids, and tannins. The leaves contain furocoumarins and essential oils, while the gum — a polysaccharide exudate tapped from the bark — finds use as a demulcent and pharmaceutical excipient.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Kapittha holds a distinguished place across all major classical Ayurvedic texts. Charaka includes it in Kakolyadi Gana and extols its use in Aruchi (anorexia), Vomiting, Diarrhea, and Vatarakta. Sushruta places it in Ambasthadi Gana with emphasis on its astringent-hepatoprotective properties. Vagbhata in Ashtanga Hridayam prescribes Kapittha Ghrita for liver disorders and chronic fevers. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu lists it in Amradi Phala Varga — the class of sour fruits.

The classical verse — "Kapittham grahi tiktoshnam dīpanam ruchikārakam, hridyam vātāmayashresthham kaphapittārtivāranam" — describes it as absorbent (grāhi), bitter, hot-potency, digestive-stimulant, heart-pleasing, pre-eminent in Vata disorders, and a reliever of Kapha-Pitta diseases.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has substantiated a wide array of Wood Apple's classical applications, with studies confirming antidiarrheal, hepatoprotective, antimicrobial, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and immunomodulatory activities across leaf, bark, pulp, and gum extracts.

Digestive Health & Antidiarrheal
The tannin-rich pulp's grahi (absorbent) quality is its most celebrated classical indication. It reduces intestinal hypermotility, firms loose stools, and is effective in chronic diarrhea, dysentery, irritable bowel, and colitis. The organic acids simultaneously stimulate bile secretion and digestive enzyme activity.
Liver Protection (Hepatoprotective)
Animal studies demonstrate significant hepatoprotective activity of pulp and leaf extracts against paracetamol-induced liver damage — reducing serum transaminases (SGOT, SGPT) and bilirubin toward normal. Gallic acid, ferulic acid, and marmesin contribute to this liver-shielding effect, corroborating classical use of Kapittha Ghrita in liver disorders.
Antidiabetic & Blood Sugar Regulation
Ethanolic leaf and pulp extracts show dose-dependent reduction of blood glucose in streptozotocin-induced diabetic animal models. The mechanism is postulated to involve alpha-glucosidase inhibition and improved peripheral insulin sensitivity. Tannins and flavonoids appear to be the primary active fractions.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Leaf and bark extracts demonstrate inhibitory activity against E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida albicans. Furocoumarins (psoralen, marmesin) and tannins are the primary antimicrobials. Bark paste has traditional applications in skin infections, ringworm, and parasitic infestations.
Respiratory & Expectorant
The unripe pulp, dried and powdered, is traditionally used with honey for chronic cough, asthma, and bronchitis — its Kapha-reducing ushna-tikta properties help liquefy and expel respiratory mucus. Leaf infusions are used in folk medicine for relief of cold-associated congestion.
Cardiovascular & Cholesterol
Rich in potassium, riboflavin, and beta-sitosterol, Wood Apple pulp supports cardiac function and healthy blood pressure. Animal studies show lipid-lowering effects of the extract, reducing LDL cholesterol and triglycerides. Its heart-tonic (hridya) classification in classical texts reflects this cardioprotective tradition.
Cooling & Anti-fever (Antipyretic)
Kavitha sherbet — pulp mixed with jaggery, black salt, and cold water — is a beloved summer drink across Telangana and Andhra Pradesh precisely for its deeply cooling, electrolyte-replenishing properties. Classical texts recommend it in fever management, excessive thirst (trishna), and burning sensations.
Oral Health & Dental Care
The Sanskrit epithet Dantashekhara — "crown of teeth" — reflects traditional use of Wood Apple in oral hygiene. Bark and leaf decoctions are used as gargles and mouth rinses for gum disease, stomatitis, and toothache; its astringent tannins tighten gum tissue and its antimicrobials suppress oral pathogens.

How to Consume Kavitha

Wood Apple is most commonly consumed as a fresh fruit drink, chutney, or digestive preparation across South India and Telangana. Internal preparations should be adjusted based on individual constitution (prakriti) and health condition, with unripe fruit preparations reserved for digestive-astringent applications and ripe fruit preparations used for general nutrition and cooling purposes.

Safety & Precautions

Wood Apple is broadly safe when consumed as food in customary amounts and has millennia of documented human use. However, therapeutic-dose preparations — particularly leaf and bark extracts — warrant attention to a few contraindications, especially for pregnant women, those with hyperacidity, and individuals on blood-sugar medications.

  • Pregnancy: Unripe fruit and bark preparations are traditionally contraindicated in pregnancy due to potential uterine-stimulant activity. Ripe fruit as food is generally considered safe in moderation.
  • Hyperacidity & Peptic Ulcers: The amla (sour) and ushna (hot) properties of unripe or large quantities of Wood Apple may aggravate Pitta — use cautiously in gastritis, GERD, and acid peptic disease.
  • Diabetes medications: Given documented hypoglycemic activity, concurrent use of therapeutic leaf extracts with oral antidiabetics (metformin, sulfonylureas) or insulin may potentiate glucose lowering — monitor blood sugar carefully.
  • Constipation (dry type): The grahi (absorbent) and ruksha (dry) properties may worsen Vata-type dry constipation — not recommended in this condition without appropriate co-administration of lubricating herbs.
  • Furocoumarins (photosensitivity): Leaf and bark preparations contain psoralen, a photosensitising furocoumarin — topical application followed by sun exposure may cause hyperpigmentation in sensitive individuals.
  • Kidney stones: The high oxalate content in the pulp means individuals with a history of calcium-oxalate kidney stones should moderate consumption and ensure adequate hydration.
  • Overconsumption: Very large quantities of fresh pulp may cause excessive intestinal binding, leading to constipation or abdominal discomfort — moderation (50–100 g pulp/day) is advised.
