Aloe Vera
Ghritkumari
The Silent Healer — The Plant of Immortality
Aloe barbadensis Mill.  ·  Liliaceae / Asphodelaceae  ·  Kumari · Ghrita Kumari · Kanyasara

Fig. 1 — The characteristic succulent rosette of Aloe barbadensis, with serrated leaf margins and waxy cuticle  ·  Fig. 2 — A freshly cut leaf revealing the translucent inner gel (parenchyma) and the yellow latex layer beneath the rind

❧

The ancient Egyptians called it "the plant of immortality" and buried it with pharaohs. Alexander the Great secured the island of Socotra for its supply to treat his wounded soldiers. From the Ebers Papyrus of 1550 BCE through Dioscorides, Pliny, and the Ayurvedic classics — Ghritkumari, "the young girl who is like ghee," has been humanity's most universally beloved healing plant across every civilisation that encountered it.

01

Botanical Profile

Aloe vera (Aloe barbadensis Mill.) is a stemless or very short-stemmed succulent perennial belonging to the Asphodelaceae family (formerly Liliaceae), native to the Arabian Peninsula and naturalised across North Africa, the Mediterranean, the Canary Islands, and the Indian subcontinent. It forms a compact basal rosette of 12–16 thick, fleshy, lanceolate leaves, each reaching 30–50 cm in length, with pale green to grey-green surfaces marked by irregular whitish spots in younger plants. Leaf margins bear sharp, whitish, cartilaginous teeth — the characteristic serrations that give the plant its distinctive silhouette.

The leaf architecture is medically significant: the outermost layer is a tough, waxy green rind (15–20 cell layers thick); immediately beneath it lies a thin layer of yellow latex (the aloin-containing sap); and at the core sits the abundant, water-rich, clear mucilaginous gel — the inner parenchyma — which constitutes the primary therapeutic substance. Aloe produces a single erect flower spike (60–90 cm) bearing pendant tubular yellow to orange flowers. It thrives in arid, well-drained soils and is drought-tolerant to a remarkable degree, making it a near-universal houseplant and a major commercial crop in India, the Americas, and the Caribbean.

The Three Layers of the Aloe Leaf

① Outer Rind (Green skin): Tough, waxy, photosynthetically active; contains proteins, carbohydrates, and a small amount of anthraquinone glycosides. Not used therapeutically.

② Yellow Latex (Aloin layer): The bitter yellow exudate just beneath the rind; rich in anthraquinones (aloin A & B, barbaloin, emodin). Potent laxative and irritant — the basis of traditional purgative use and the source of most safety concerns.

③ Inner Gel (Parenchyma): The clear, mucilaginous, nearly tasteless inner flesh — 98.5% water + acemannan polysaccharides, glycoproteins, vitamins, minerals, enzymes, and amino acids. This is what is meant by "aloe gel" in therapeutic and cosmetic use.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Over 200 biologically active compounds have been identified in Aloe vera, spanning six broad categories: polysaccharides, anthraquinones, vitamins, minerals, enzymes, and miscellaneous organic acids and amino acids. The therapeutic profile of the gel versus the latex are almost entirely distinct — and the safety profiles are dramatically different — making the distinction between these two fractions critical to correct use.

The most therapeutically significant compound is acemannan — a high-molecular-weight acetylated mannan polysaccharide that accounts for the gel's immunomodulatory, wound-healing, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral activity. The anthraquinone fraction (aloin, barbaloin, emodin, aloe-emodin) in the latex is the source of both the potent laxative action and the carcinogenicity concerns that led to FDA reclassification of aloin-containing laxatives in 2002.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Ghritkumari is one of Ayurveda's most extensively described single herbs. Charaka includes it among the Jivaniya (life-giving) group and uses it in treatments for Gulma (abdominal tumours/masses), Arsha (haemorrhoids), and Tvak Vikara (skin diseases). Sushruta describes its use in Visha Chikitsa (toxicology) and wound management. The Ashtanga Hridayam lists it as both a Rasayana (rejuvenative) and a Medhya (intellect-promoting) dravya in the Haritakyadi Varga.

Bhavaprakasha Nighantu places Kumari in the Guduchyadi Varga and describes it as: "Kumari rasayani sheetala tikta vrushya rasayanam, grahi pittakaphaghni cha vishodhana balya param" — classifying it as a Rasayana, cooling, bitter, aphrodisiac, constipating (gel), Pitta-Kapha-pacifying, purifying, and supremely strength-giving. Dhanvantari Nighantu lists Ghritkumari swarasa (fresh gel juice) for liver disorders, eye diseases, and uterine conditions — the last linking its name Kanya (young woman) to its gynaecological applications.

04

Key Health Benefits

No plant in the Swastha Garden has a wider research base than Aloe vera — with hundreds of peer-reviewed human clinical trials validating its applications across dermatology, gastroenterology, immunology, dentistry, oncology, and endocrinology. It is simultaneously one of the world's most rigorously studied and most ancient healing plants.

Burn & Wound Healing
The most extensively documented use of aloe vera — a 2007 systematic review of 4 RCTs found aloe gel healed first and second-degree burns 8.79 days faster than petroleum gauze dressings. Bradykininase reduces pain; acemannan and gibberellins stimulate fibroblast proliferation; mucopolysaccharides maintain a moist wound environment optimal for epithelialisation.
Digestive Health & IBS
Decolourised (anthraquinone-free) aloe gel significantly reduces IBS symptoms in multiple human RCTs — reducing pain, bloating, and stool irregularity. The mucilaginous polysaccharides coat and soothe the gut lining; acemannan modulates gut immunity; and the anti-inflammatory action reduces mucosal inflammation in ulcerative colitis (early-stage human data).
Skin & Dermatological Applications
Aloe gel is one of the most validated dermatological agents: clinically proven for psoriasis (reducing scaling and erythema vs 0.1% triamcinolone in RCTs), seborrhoeic dermatitis, oral lichen planus, acne, and radiation-induced skin reactions. Aloesin inhibits tyrosinase, providing documented skin-brightening effects. It also accelerates surgical and post-laser skin healing.
Blood Sugar & Diabetes
A 2016 meta-analysis of 9 controlled trials found aloe supplementation significantly reduced fasting blood glucose and HbA1c in pre-diabetic and diabetic subjects. Mechanisms include alpha-glucosidase inhibition, improved pancreatic beta-cell function, and acemannan-mediated enhancement of insulin receptor sensitivity. Effective without the gastrointestinal side effects of many hypoglycemic drugs.
Oral Health & Dental Care
Aloe vera gel used as a mouthwash (0.5%) is as effective as 0.2% chlorhexidine (the gold standard) in reducing plaque and gingivitis — without side effects of staining or taste alteration. Effective against Streptococcus mutans, Candida albicans, and periodontal pathogens. Aloe gel toothpaste demonstrates anti-caries and antifungal activity in multiple RCTs.
Liver & Detoxification
Ghritkumari is a primary classical remedy for Yakrit Roga (liver disorders). Animal studies confirm hepatoprotective activity against CCl₄-induced liver damage. Human data from non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) trials shows aloe supplementation significantly reduced liver enzymes (ALT, AST) and hepatic fat index. Its bitter compounds stimulate bile secretion and improve fat digestion.
Immunomodulation & Antiviral
Acemannan is one of the few plant polysaccharides with demonstrated immunomodulatory activity in human trials — stimulating macrophage activation, natural killer cell activity, and cytokine production. It has shown activity against HIV-1 (blocking viral adsorption) and Herpes simplex virus in vitro. Acemannan is used clinically in veterinary medicine for feline leukaemia virus.
Gynaecological & Reproductive Health
Classical Ayurveda uses Ghritkumari swarasa for dysmenorrhea, amenorrhea, leucorrhoea, and uterine debility — the name Kanya (young woman) reflects this affinity. Modern studies suggest aloe phytoestrogens modulate hormonal cycles. The latex fraction (aloin) stimulates uterine contractions — hence its traditional use in menstrual regulation and the strict contraindication in pregnancy.
05

How to Consume Ghritkumari

The single most important preparation distinction with Aloe vera is ensuring that the yellow latex (aloin) is completely removed before any internal use. This is achieved by filleting only the clear inner gel and discarding the outer rind and the yellow layer immediately beneath it. For topical use the entire inner leaf including rind can be split and applied. Commercial products labelled "decolourised" or "purified" have had aloin removed and are preferred for internal therapeutic use.

Safety & Precautions

Topical aloe gel is among the safest of all botanical applications — used by billions worldwide for millennia without serious adverse effects. Internal aloe gel (decolourised, aloin-free) is also well-tolerated. However, the latex fraction carries significant documented risks and requires extreme caution. The distinction between gel and latex is therefore the single most important safety consideration in aloe use.

  • Pregnancy — Strict Contraindication (Latex): Aloin and anthraquinones in the latex are powerful uterine stimulants and have caused miscarriage in animal studies. Internal use of whole-leaf aloe or aloe latex is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy. Topical use of gel on intact skin is generally considered safe.
  • Latex carcinogenicity concern: The US National Toxicology Program (NTP) 2013 study found that non-decolourised (aloin-containing) whole-leaf aloe extract caused intestinal tumours in rats with long-term high-dose exposure. Always use decolourised, purified products for internal use. The FDA reclassified aloin-containing OTC laxatives as "not generally recognised as safe" (non-GRAS) in 2002.
  • Electrolyte depletion with latex: Regular internal use of the latex as a laxative depletes potassium — potentially causing hypokalemia, muscle weakness, and cardiac arrhythmia. Can potentiate cardiac glycosides (digoxin) and antiarrhythmic drugs dangerously.
  • Hypoglycemia risk: Aloe's documented blood-glucose-lowering effect can cause additive hypoglycemia when combined with insulin, metformin, glipizide, or other antidiabetic medications. Monitor blood glucose carefully; dose adjustment may be necessary.
  • Contact dermatitis: A small percentage of people develop allergic contact dermatitis to topical aloe — typically to anthraquinone components. Test a small area first. Cross-reactivity with other Liliaceae plants (garlic, onion, tulip) is possible.
  • Drug interactions: Aloe latex increases intestinal motility and can reduce the absorption of oral medications. Internal gel may interact with diuretics (additive potassium loss), sevoflurane anaesthesia (increased bleeding risk), and diabetes and anticoagulant medications.
  • Children under 12: Internal use of aloe (even gel) is not recommended in young children. Topical gel is safe for children after patch testing.
  • Kidney disease: The nephrotoxic potential of anthraquinones at high doses makes latex use dangerous in those with pre-existing kidney conditions. Even pure gel should be used with caution in advanced renal failure due to electrolyte sensitivity.
