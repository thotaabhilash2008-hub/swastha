Revered across millennia as Rakta Chandana — "blood-red sandalwood" — this endemic tree of the Eastern Ghats holds a singular place in Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia, Unani medicine, Buddhist and Hindu ritual, and classical Indian artisanship. Its wine-red heartwood, yielding the pigment santalin, has been traded across Asia since at least the 9th century CE, lauded by Charaka and Sushruta alike for its Pitta-shamaka, wound-healing, and skin-beautifying virtues.

01

Botanical Profile

Lal Chandan (Pterocarpus santalinus) is a slow-growing, medium-sized deciduous tree of the Fabaceae (Leguminosae) family, endemic to the dry hill forests of the Eastern Ghats — principally the Nallamala, Seshachalam, and Veligonda ranges of Andhra Pradesh. It typically reaches 8–12 m in height with a short, irregular bole and a spreading crown. The bark is dark grey-brown, rough, and deeply furrowed; when cut, the inner layers exude a blood-red resinous sap — the hallmark of the species.

Leaves are alternate, trifoliate, with broadly ovate to suborbicular leaflets bearing finely crenulate margins. Flowers are bright yellow, pea-shaped (papilionaceous), borne in short axillary racemes from April to June — before the leaves fully emerge — presenting a vivid seasonal spectacle. Pods are orbicular, flat, winged samaras, each containing one seed. The heartwood — the therapeutically and commercially valuable part — is deep red to purplish-crimson, extremely dense and hard, non-aromatic (unlike white sandalwood), and naturally resistant to termites and fungi. It is listed as a scheduled species under CITES Appendix II due to decades of over-harvesting and smuggling.

Phytochemistry & Composition

The deep-red colour of Lal Chandan heartwood is due primarily to santalin (santalin A and B) — pterocarpene-based homoisoflavonoids unique to this species. Beyond its pigment, the wood yields a remarkable array of bioactive compounds including pterostilbene, pterolinus A–D, cryptostrobin, savinin, and a spectrum of flavonoids and polyphenols. The roots and bark add further alkaloids, terpenoids, and glycosides to the therapeutic palette.

Ayurvedic Significance

Rakta Chandana is one of the foremost Pitta-pacifying drugs in the Ayurvedic materia medica, mentioned prominently in Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam, and all major Nighantus. It appears in the Chandanadi Gana (Charaka) and is a principal ingredient in classical formulations including Chandanadya Ghrita, Rakta Chandanasava, Eladi Churna, and Usirasava. Sushruta places it among the Sarivadi drugs useful in skin diseases and blood disorders.

Classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha Nighantu — "Raktam chandanam sheetam ca tiktam pittaraktapranut, tvakdosha jwarahara ca vrananam ropakam param" — describes it as cold in potency, bitter in taste, pacifying Pitta and blood disorders, curing skin diseases and fever, and supreme in healing wounds.

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research on Lal Chandan has validated a wide range of its classical applications, with particularly robust data on anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, and skin-healing effects across multiple in vitro and in vivo models.

Skin Health & Complexion
Perhaps the most celebrated classical application. Heartwood paste (lepa) applied topically cools inflamed skin, reduces hyperpigmentation, fades blemishes, and treats acne, prickly heat, and rashes. Santalin and pterostilbene inhibit melanin synthesis and provide UV-protective antioxidant action. Still widely used in Ayurvedic cosmetics, ubtans, and face packs.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Aqueous and ethanolic extracts significantly suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and inhibit COX-2. Animal studies demonstrate reduction in carrageenan-induced paw oedema comparable to standard anti-inflammatory drugs. Savinin and pterostilbene are key active contributors.
Antidiabetic & Metabolic
Santalin A demonstrates significant α-glucosidase inhibitory activity — slowing post-meal glucose absorption. Multiple animal studies show heartwood extracts lower fasting blood glucose and improve insulin sensitivity in diabetic models. Pterostilbene additionally activates PPAR-α pathways implicated in lipid regulation.
Hepatoprotective
Savinin — a key lignan isolated from Lal Chandan — demonstrates dose-dependent protection against CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity, reducing liver enzyme elevations (ALT, AST) and preventing histological damage. Classical formulation Rakta Chandanasava is used in jaundice, hepatitis, and liver congestion.
Antipyretic & Fever Management
Its Sheeta virya (cooling potency) is a classical treatment for fever — fresh heartwood paste is applied to the forehead, temples, and chest in heat fevers. Extracts show significant antipyretic activity in yeast-induced pyrexia in animal models, acting via prostaglandin inhibition.
Wound Healing
Topical application of heartwood paste promotes wound closure through multiple mechanisms: stimulating collagen synthesis, reducing oxidative stress at the wound site, inhibiting bacterial colonisation, and modulating inflammatory resolution. Classical texts term it vrana ropana param — supreme in wound healing.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Pterolinus A–D and angolensin demonstrate significant activity against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Aspergillus niger, and Candida albicans. The wood's natural antimicrobial properties underpin both its traditional wound-care use and its remarkable durability as a timber without chemical treatment.
Urinary & Reproductive Health
Decoction is used in burning urination (mutrakricchra), haematuria (blood in urine), and seminal disorders (shukrameha) in classical Ayurveda. Aqueous extract shows relaxant effects on smooth muscle, and cooling properties soothe urogenital inflammation. Used in formulations for menorrhagia and uterine bleeding.

How to Use Lal Chandan

Lal Chandan is used both internally (decoction, powder, medicated preparations) and extensively as a topical agent. Unlike white sandalwood, its primary modern use is external — in skin care, cooling pastes, and cosmetic formulations. Internal preparations should be taken under qualified supervision. As a protected species, only legally sourced material should be used.

Safety & Precautions

Lal Chandan has an excellent safety profile when used at classical doses — it is considered non-toxic and well tolerated in both internal and external applications for most individuals. Topical use is very safe. Internal use requires attention to constitution, dosage, and duration.

  • Vata constitution / Vata disorders: Its Sheeta (cooling) and Ruksha (drying) properties may aggravate Vata — use cautiously in Vata-dominant individuals, in cold climates, or during winter without appropriate anupana (vehicle) such as warm milk or ghee.
  • Pregnancy: Internal use at therapeutic doses should be supervised. Classical use for uterine bleeding requires physician direction; avoid self-dosing.
  • Kapha disorders: Its cooling and mildly sweet nature may worsen chronic Kapha conditions — obesity, diabetes with Kapha predominance, or chronic mucus disorders — if used in excess internally.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate antidiabetic medications — monitor blood glucose when combining with hypoglycemic drugs. Its hepatoprotective and blood-thinning properties could interact with anticoagulant medications.
  • Skin allergy: Topical application is very well tolerated, but patch test is advisable for highly sensitive skin. Discontinue if contact dermatitis develops.
  • Sourcing & legality: Pterocarpus santalinus is a CITES-listed species and its trade is strictly regulated in India. Always purchase from licensed, certified vendors. Illegally sourced wood should never be used or purchased.
  • Prolonged internal use: No significant toxicity is reported at traditional doses; however, prolonged use beyond 8–12 weeks without supervision is not advisable. Not a substitute for medical treatment in serious conditions.
  • Staining: The deep-red santalin pigment stains fabrics, skin, and surfaces intensely — use protective measures when preparing pastes or decoctions.
