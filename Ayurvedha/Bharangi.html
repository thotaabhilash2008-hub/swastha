Discovered, the texts say, by Sage Bhrigu — and named for the lineage of his daughter, Bhrigubhava. Two of its Sanskrit names tell the entire story: Vatari, "the enemy of Vata," and Kasaghni, "the cough-slayer." Where breath rattles, where mucus settles, where Kapha clings to the lungs and refuses to leave, Bharangi is sent.

01

Botanical Profile

Bharangi (Clerodendrum serratum (L.) Moon, syn. Rotheca serrata) is a perennial woody shrub of the Lamiaceae family (formerly Verbenaceae), reaching 1–3 metres in height with characteristic bluntly quadrangular stems. The leaves are arranged usually in whorls of three at each node — broadly oval to elliptic, 7–20 cm long, with sharply saw-toothed margins (the source of the species name serratum). The plant has small spine-like protrusions that are pulpy and oily.

Bharangi flowers are small, slightly fragrant, pale blue to pinkish-violet with darker blue lower lips, arranged in branched terminal panicles 15–45 cm long. The species name serratum refers to the leaf margins, while the genus name Clerodendrum derives from the Greek kleros (chance) and dendron (tree) — a reference to the variable medicinal nature of the genus. Fruits appear in groups of about 16 small drupes, ripening from green to deep purple-black. Native to the Indian subcontinent, Sri Lanka, and tropical Asia, Bharangi grows wild from sea level up to 1500 m altitude. Importantly, modern Ayurvedic practice sometimes confuses Bharangi with Clerodendrum indicum (Brahmanayastika); the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India authoritatively recognises Clerodendrum serratum as the genuine source.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

Modern phytochemical research on Bharangi roots and aerial parts has identified a remarkable matrix of bioactive compounds — flavonoids (apigenin, luteolin, baicalein, scutellarein, hispidulin), saponins, alkaloids, terpenoids, sterols (β-sitosterol, stigmasterol), phenolics, and the plant-specific markers queretaroic acid, serratagenic acid, and serratumin A. The roots are particularly rich in D-mannitol, oleanolic acid, and the iridoid glycoside verbascoside.

Pharmacologically, the most studied compounds are the flavonoids hispidulin and apigenin (mast cell stabilisers — central to its antiasthmatic action) and queretaroic acid (anti-inflammatory and bronchodilator). Saponins like serratagenin further reduce inflammation in airway tissue. Together, this constellation of compounds explains why classical Ayurvedic texts describe Bharangi as the cornerstone of cough and asthma formulations — a tradition increasingly validated by clinical research on its mast-cell-stabilising, bronchodilatory, and anti-allergic mechanisms.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Bharangi is the foundational anti-respiratory-disease herb of Ayurvedic pharmacology, mentioned widely across Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridaya, and the Bhavaprakasha Nighantu. Charaka places it in Pureesha-Sangrahaniya Mahakashaya (group of herbs that increase bulk of feces), while its broader use is in Shvasa (asthma, dyspnoea), Kasa (cough), and Kapha-vata-shamaka (Kapha-Vata pacification). The classical formula prescribes Bharangi root in combination with Sati (Hedychium spicatum), Pushkaramoola (Inula racemosa), Patha (Cissampelos pareira), Katphala (Myrica nagi), Devadaru (Cedrus deodara), and Karkatshringi (Pistacia integerrima) as a definitive treatment for Kaphaja Jwara (Kapha-type fever).

Its rasa (taste) is tikta (bitter), katu (pungent), and kashaya (astringent); guna is laghu (light) and ruksha (dry); vipaka is katu (pungent); and veerya is ushna (hot). The hot, light, dry qualities make it the ideal Kapha-pacifier — drying excess mucus, opening airway passages, and igniting digestive fire. The Sanskrit name Vatari ("enemy of Vata") indicates its parallel ability to calm Vata-related disorders. Bharangi appears in some of Ayurveda's most beloved respiratory formulations: Bharangi Guda, Pushkaramoolasava, Vasavaleha, Kanakasava, and Ayaskriti.

04

Key Health Benefits

Bharangi's classical reputation as Kasaghni (cough-slayer) has stood the test of both 2,000+ years of clinical use and intensive modern pharmacological investigation. Its respiratory action is the standout, but contemporary research has confirmed activity across hepatic, oncological, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory domains — making it one of the most actively studied Ayurvedic respiratory herbs.

Asthma & Bronchodilator
The flagship classical and modern use. Hispidulin and apigenin in the roots stabilise mast cells, reducing histamine release responsible for bronchoconstriction and allergic airway inflammation. Validated in multiple animal-model studies for antiasthmatic action — supporting traditional use in Shvasa.
Cough & Expectorant (Kasaghni)
Saponins and D-mannitol provide expectorant action — thinning mucus and easing its expulsion. Useful in chronic cough, productive cough, bronchitis, common cold, sinusitis, allergic rhinitis, and the lingering Kaphaja cough that resists ordinary remedies.
Anti-allergic & Antihistamine
Mast cell stabilisation reduces the cascade of histamine, leukotriene, and cytokine release responsible for allergic responses. Useful in seasonal allergies, allergic rhinitis, urticaria (hives), and skin allergies — modern research supports the underlying mechanism.
Anti-inflammatory & Antipyretic
Classical Shothahara (anti-inflammatory) and Jwaraghna (antipyretic). Triterpenoid acids and flavonoids reduce inflammatory mediators in animal-model studies. Used in fever, joint pain, swelling, and inflammatory skin conditions — particularly Kaphaja types of fever.
Digestive & Carminative
Classical Deepana-Pachana (digestive-fire kindler & digestant). Stimulates appetite, relieves bloating and indigestion, and clears Ama (metabolic toxin). Helps facilitate normal direction of doshic movement in the digestive tract — supportive in chronic indigestion.
Hepatoprotective & Antioxidant
Verbascoside, oleanolic acid, and flavonoids together protect hepatocytes from oxidative and toxic damage. Documented hepatoprotective activity supports traditional use in liver disorders and as part of detoxification protocols.
Wound Healing & Skin
Leaf paste applied externally promotes Vrana Pachana (expulsion of pus from boils and wounds) and reduces burning sensation. Also indicated traditionally for Gandamala (cervical lymphadenopathy) — applied topically alongside internal therapy.
Cyst, Fibroid & Gynaecological
Decoction of Bharangi has classical use for cysts, raktagulma (blood clots/uterine masses), and uterine fibroids. The combination of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory action supports its role in chronic gynaecological complaints — under proper Ayurvedic supervision.

How to Consume Bharangi

Bharangi is exclusively medicinal — not consumed as food. The roots are by far the most heavily used part, prepared as decoction, powder, or as a component of polyherbal classical preparations. The Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India recommends 3–6 g of root powder or 10–20 g of kwatha churna (decoction-grade coarse powder) per day for adults. Most therapeutic protocols use Bharangi in combination with respiratory companions like Sati and Pushkaramoola rather than as a standalone single drug.

Safety & Precautions

Bharangi is generally considered safe at API-recommended doses, with limited toxicological data raising no major red flags across centuries of clinical use. However, its hot potency, drying qualities, and Pitta-aggravating tendency call for measured use in specific conditions, and there are notable controversies regarding species authentication that warrant attention from suppliers and consumers alike.

  • Pregnancy: Avoid medicinal doses entirely. The hot potency (ushna virya) and emmenagogue-like properties may affect uterine stability. Modern toxicological data for pregnancy is limited; classical caution applies.
  • Breastfeeding: Insufficient safety data for medicinal use during lactation; consult an Ayurvedic physician before any therapeutic use.
  • High Pitta & Hyperacidity: Ushna virya, tikshna quality, and bitter-pungent rasa may aggravate hyperacidity, gastritis, peptic ulcer, and high-Pitta inflammatory conditions. Avoid during active flare-ups; pair with cooling anupanas if used in moderate Pitta.
  • Vata excess (in dryness): Despite its Vatari name, the dry (ruksha) quality may worsen Vata-driven dryness, joint pain, or constipation if used without ghee or unctuous anupana. Combine with milk or ghee for Vata-prone individuals.
  • Species substitution & controversy: Multiple species (Clerodendrum indicum, Premna species) are sometimes substituted under the name Bharangi. The Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India recognises only Clerodendrum serratum as authentic — source from reputable suppliers to ensure correct material.
  • Maximum dose limit: Stay within API recommendations of 3–6 g root powder or 10–20 g kwatha churna per day. Higher doses may cause digestive discomfort, burning sensation, or excessive heating.
  • Surgery: Discontinue concentrated extracts at least 2 weeks before scheduled surgery due to possible effects on inflammatory pathways, blood pressure, and platelet function.
  • Children: Bharangi is widely used in classical paediatric respiratory formulations, but only at appropriate dose under qualified Ayurvedic supervision. Not recommended as self-medication for young children.
