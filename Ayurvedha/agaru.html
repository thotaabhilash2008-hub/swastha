Known across the ancient world as the "Wood of the Gods" — at times more precious than gold — Agaru is the darkly fragrant resinous heartwood that forms within Aquilaria trees as their natural response to fungal wounding, transmuted by time and injury into one of Ayurveda's most revered aromatic healing agents.

01

Botanical Profile

Agaru is not a wood in the ordinary sense — it is a defence product. The source tree, Aquilaria malaccensis (historically also called A. agallocha), is a medium-to-large evergreen of the Thymelaeaceae family, reaching 15–40 metres in height, with a straight cylindrical trunk, smooth greyish bark, and alternate, glabrous, lanceolate leaves 5–15 cm long with 12–16 pairs of parallel veins. The tree produces small greenish-yellow flowers in umbel-like inflorescences and obovate-cuneate capsular fruits.

In its natural, uninjured state, the heartwood is pale and odourless. Only when the tree is wounded — by storm, insect, or fungal invasion (primarily Phaeoacremonium parasitica) — does it begin to secrete the dark, resin-saturated, fragrant wood known as Agaru, Gaharu, or Oud. This transformation can take decades. The tree is found in Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Tripura, Nagaland, and across Bangladesh, Bhutan, Myanmar, and Southeast Asia up to 1,000 metres altitude. Due to overexploitation, A. malaccensis is now Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List and protected under CITES Appendix II.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

Agaru's distinctive fragrance and pharmacological action derive from one of the most complex natural-product profiles in the plant kingdom — a dense matrix of sesquiterpenes and 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromones that together comprise the hallmark chemistry of genuine agarwood. Over 150 compounds have been isolated across eight different sesquiterpene skeletons, many of which are unique to the genus Aquilaria.

The agarofuran, agarospirane, and eudesmane-type sesquiterpenes drive the wood's signature warm, animalic aroma and account for its anti-inflammatory, sedative, and neuroprotective activity. Chromone derivatives like agarotetrol and 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromones contribute to its antioxidant and immunomodulatory profile.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Agaru occupies a singular place in classical Ayurveda — it is one of the few tikta rasa (bitter) substances possessing ushna virya (hot potency), a rare deviation from the general rule that bitter herbs are cooling. Charaka includes it in the Sheeta Prashamana (cold-relieving), Shvasahara (anti-asthmatic), Shiro Virechanopaga (errhine-adjuvant), and Tikta Skandha groups. Sushruta and Vagbhata place it in Eladi, Salasaradi, and Sleshmashamana (Kapha-pacifying) classifications. Bhavaprakasha catalogues it in Karpooradi Varga among aromatic substances.

Sodhala Nighantu distinguishes three varieties — Agaru, Krishna Agaru, and Kakatunda Agaru. Rajanighantu lists four; Dhanvantari Nighantu mentions Kaleyaka as another variant. Its Sanskrit name Yogajam — "born of union" — captures the essence of its formation: the aromatic heartwood arises only from the union of tree and fungus, a natural alchemy unknown in any other medicinal substance.

04

Key Health Benefits

Agaru's therapeutic profile spans respiratory, neurological, digestive, and dermatological applications — with modern pharmacology increasingly validating its traditional reputation as a Rasayana (rejuvenative) substance. Recent research has also explored its sesquiterpene hydrocarbons for potential immunomodulatory and antiviral activity.

Respiratory Support
Classically prescribed for Kasa (cough), Shwasa (asthma), and Hikka (hiccup) — Ashtanga Hridaya recommends Agaru churna with honey for cough. Sesquiterpenes relax bronchial smooth muscle while the wood's warming, penetrating quality clears Kapha from the respiratory channels. Smoke from Agaru incense traditionally inhaled in acute asthma.
Neuroprotective & Sedative
Agarospirol and related sesquiterpenes demonstrate CNS-depressant and anxiolytic effects in pharmacological studies. Eremophilane sesquiterpenes protect PC12 neurons from corticosterone-induced damage, supporting traditional use as a mental-calming and cognition-supporting substance during meditation and stress.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Eudesmane-type sesquiterpenes inhibit LPS-induced nitric oxide production in macrophages at low micromolar concentrations. Traditionally applied as paste in Shotha (edema), painful joints, and rheumatoid arthritis. Charaka's Agarvadi Taila addresses chills associated with fever and inflammation.
Skin & Wound Healing
Sushruta prescribes Agaru oil for Kushtha (ringworm and skin diseases) and Dushta Vrana (chronic non-healing ulcers). The aromatic wood's fumigation desiccates discharging surfaces while antimicrobial sesquiterpenes prevent infection. Also used in eczematous and fungal skin conditions.
Ear, Eye & Oral Health
Known as Shruti-Netra-Rujahara — reliever of ear and eye pain. Agaru-infused oils are used for ear drops, eye inflammations, and oral-cavity strengthening. Its use as a mouth freshener and oil-pulling adjuvant has persisted for centuries in Ayurvedic and Unani traditions.
Digestive & Carminative
Stimulates digestive fire (agni), relieves intestinal gas, persistent hiccups, and loss of appetite. Sushruta recommends a decoction of Patha and Agaru for Lavanameha (a type of Prameha/diabetes). Modern studies confirm gastroprotective and ulcer-protective activity.
Rasayana & Rejuvenative
Classical texts list Agaru among Rasayana substances — supporting longevity, vitality, and sensory faculties. Its warming, grounding action calms Vata at the pranavaha srotas (respiratory-nervous channels) while gently stimulating Pitta for improved circulation and tissue nourishment.
Spiritual & Aromatic Therapeutics
Agaru incense has been burned for millennia in meditation, temple worship, and funerary rites across Hindu, Buddhist, Islamic, and Taoist traditions — believed to elevate consciousness and purify atmosphere. Modern aromatherapy research validates its anxiolytic and mood-stabilising properties through inhalation.

How to Consume Agaru

Agaru is used both internally (powder, decoction, oil) and externally (paste, topical oil, incense smoke, nasya). Because of its extreme rarity and cost — genuine Oud oil often exceeds the price of 24-carat gold by weight — classical preparations traditionally combine small amounts of Agaru with supporting herbs. Always seek sustainably-sourced, certified agarwood to support conservation efforts.

Safety & Precautions

Agaru is generally well-tolerated at classical doses and has a long record of safe traditional use. However, its ushna virya (hot potency) makes it potentially aggravating for Pitta-dominant individuals, and the rarity-driven market carries significant risk of adulteration. Always source from reputable, CITES-compliant suppliers.

  • Pregnancy: Safety has not been established in pregnancy; avoid internal therapeutic use. External aromatic use (incense) in moderate amounts is generally considered acceptable.
  • Breastfeeding: Insufficient evidence of safety for nursing mothers; avoid concentrated oral preparations and essential oil internally.
  • Pitta-dominant conditions: Its ushna virya can aggravate hyperacidity, heartburn, bleeding disorders, peptic ulcers, and inflammatory Pitta conditions — use cautiously and in reduced doses.
  • Dry skin & high body heat: Those prone to excessive body heat, night sweats, or very dry skin should use Agaru with cooling adjuvants (ghee, milk, rose water).
  • Essential oil handling: Pure Oud oil is highly concentrated; never apply undiluted to skin. Always dilute 1–3% in a neutral carrier oil (sesame, coconut, jojoba) for topical use.
  • Respiratory sensitivity: Incense smoke may trigger reactions in individuals with severe asthma or chemical sensitivities — ventilate well and use briefly at first.
  • Adulteration risk: The market is rife with fake or synthetic Oud. Verify source and prefer steam-distilled oils from regulated plantations. Look for CITES certification.
  • Conservation ethics: Wild A. malaccensis is Critically Endangered. Choose cultivated, inoculated-plantation agarwood and avoid wild-harvested products to support species survival.
  • Drug interactions: Theoretical caution with sedative medications (benzodiazepines, barbiturates) due to CNS-depressant sesquiterpenes — inform your physician before combining.
  • Children & elderly: Reduce internal doses to one-half; prefer external and aromatic applications in these populations.
