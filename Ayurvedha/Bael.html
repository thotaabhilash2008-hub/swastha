Bael
Bilva — Sriphal, The Tree of Lord Shiva
Aegle marmelos  ·  Rutaceae

Fig. 1 — Hard-shelled golden ripe Bael fruit, cracked open to reveal its fragrant orange pulp  ·  Fig. 2 — The sacred trifoliate Bilva leaves, offered to Lord Shiva for over five millennia

❧

No medicinal tree of India sits as deeply in the spiritual imagination as Bilva — the Shiva Druma, the tree under which Lord Shiva is said to dwell. Its trifoliate leaves carry the symbolism of Brahma, Vishnu, Mahesh; of Satva, Rajas, Tamas; of past, present, and future. Yet alongside its sacred role lives a five-thousand-year-old medicinal heritage, named in the Yajurveda and validated by modern research from gut to glycaemia.

01

Botanical Profile

Bael (Aegle marmelos (L.) Corrêa) is a moderately-sized deciduous tree of the Rutaceae family — the citrus family — reaching 8–13 metres in height, with a gnarled, robust trunk, irregular branching, and characteristic sharp thorns up to 4 cm long. The bark is greyish-brown, deeply furrowed, and exudes a clear gum when wounded. Leaves are alternate, trifoliate, with three ovate-lanceolate leaflets — the central one larger — releasing an aromatic citrus-like fragrance when crushed.

The greenish-white, fragrant flowers appear in lateral panicles between April and May. The signature fruit is the Bilva itself — hard, smooth, woody-shelled, 5–15 cm in diameter, ripening from green to golden yellow. Inside lies a fragrant orange pulp, sticky, sweet, and full of small woolly seeds embedded in mucilage. The tree is indigenous to the Eastern Ghats and central India, distributed throughout India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asia. It has been used for over 5,000 years and is one of the ten classical roots of the Dashamoola formulation.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

Bael is a treasure of phytochemicals. The plant's signature bioactive marker is marmelosin — a furocoumarin first isolated from its fruits and named after its scientific epithet. Alongside marmelosin, the plant produces a remarkable array of alkaloids (aegeline, aegelenine, fragrine, skimmianine), coumarins (marmin, psoralen, imperatorin, umbelliferone, scopoletin), terpenes (cineol, β-caryophyllene), tannins, and flavonoids (rutin, marmesin, marmesinin).

The ripe fruit pulp is particularly rich in carotenoids, phenolics, pectins, mucilage, tannins, vitamin C, and the unique sticky polysaccharide that gives Bael Sharbat its characteristic body. The leaves additionally contain aegeline (responsible for hypoglycaemic action), while the root bark yields umbelliferone β-D-galactopyranoside (UFG) — a compound studied for antidiabetic activity in 2014–2018 STZ-diabetic rat research. Different plant parts have been mapped to distinct therapeutic uses based on this chemistry.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Charaka Samhita places Bilva in three classical groupings: Shothahara Mahakashaya (anti-inflammatory group), Arshoghna Mahakashaya (anti-haemorrhoidal group), and Asthapanopaga Mahakashaya (adjuvants in basti / enema therapy). The classical shloka declares: "Bilvam tu kashaya tiktam, deepanam balyam ushnam, samgrahi vata-shleshma-haram, tatra-tikshnam tu pittakrit" — Bilwa is astringent and bitter; kindles digestive fire; strengthens; hot in potency; absorbent; pacifies Vata and Kapha; piercing; increases Pitta.

Different parts have different attributes. Unripe (Bilva Shunthi) fruit: tikshna (piercing), snigdha (unctuous), sangrahi (absorbent), deepana (digestive), katu-tikta-kashaya rasa, ushna virya — best of all for diarrhoea and dysentery. Ripe fruit: heavy, sweet-sour, slightly laxative, but causes diarrhoea, gas, and burning sensation if eaten in excess. Leaf: sangrahi, vatajit, balances tridosha, relieves vomiting and abdominal colic. Root: cornerstone of Dashamoola — anti-inflammatory and pain-relieving. The root, fruit, and leaves are used in some of the most beloved classical formulations: Vilvadi Gulika (insect bites, fever), Bilva Tail (ear diseases), Astapatra Tail (headache).

04

Key Health Benefits

Bilva is one of those rare herbs where every part works — root, bark, leaf, flower, unripe fruit, ripe fruit, seed — and modern research has confirmed activity across digestive, metabolic, hepatic, cardiovascular, antimicrobial, and even radioprotective domains. Few plants traverse so many systems with so much classical and clinical backing.

Digestive & Anti-Diarrhoeal
The flagship classical use. Unripe Bael fruit's tannins and mucilage absorb intestinal toxins, normalise bowel motility, and soothe inflamed gut tissue — making it the single best Ayurvedic fruit for chronic diarrhoea (Atisara), dysentery, IBS, and ulcerative colitis. Belgiri Powder and Kutaja-Bilva formulations are based on this action.
Antidiabetic & Glycaemic
Aegeline and umbelliferone β-D-galactopyranoside (UFG) reduce fasting blood glucose, improve insulin sensitivity, and protect pancreatic β-cells in STZ-induced diabetic rat research. Daily Bilva leaf juice or chewing fresh leaves is a classical folk remedy for diabetes mellitus, supported by modern hypoglycaemic studies.
Anti-inflammatory & Joint Pain
Marmelosin, aegeline, and coumarins inhibit COX-2 and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6). Bilva root is one of the ten Dashamoola roots — Ayurveda's foundational anti-inflammatory pharmacy used for arthritis, sciatica, gout, and post-injury inflammation.
Haemorrhoid Relief (Arshoghna)
Charaka specifically lists Bilva in the Arshoghna Mahakashaya — herbs useful in haemorrhoids. Astringent tannins reduce swelling, control bleeding, and soothe inflamed tissue. Used both internally as fruit pulp and externally as decoction sitz bath.
Cardiovascular & Lipid Health
Documented antihyperlipidemic activity — reduces total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides while supporting HDL. Cardioprotective coumarins improve circulation, support heart muscle function, and reduce oxidative stress. Useful in hypercholesterolaemia and metabolic syndrome.
Hepatoprotective & Detoxifying
Marmelosin and umbelliferone compounds protect hepatocytes from toxic and oxidative damage. Modern reviews document hepatoprotective activity across multiple research models. Useful in liver enlargement, mild hepatic insufficiency, and as adjunct support during chemotherapy.
Antimicrobial & Antiviral
Broad-spectrum activity demonstrated against bacteria, viruses, and fungi. Bael leaf juice with honey is a classical fever and cold remedy. Modern studies document antiviral activity, supporting traditional use in respiratory infections and chronic gastrointestinal infections.
Cooling Sharbat for Summer
In Northern India, ripe Bael fruit is blended into Bael Sharbat — a cooling summer drink with milk, jaggery, and cardamom — that prevents heat stroke, restores hydration, and aids in chronic constipation. The pulp's mucilage and electrolytes make it particularly well-suited to hot climates.

How to Consume Bael

Each part of Bael serves a different therapeutic purpose. The unripe fruit (Bilva Shunthi) is best for digestive disorders; the ripe fruit makes summer's most cherished cooling drink; the leaves treat fever, diabetes, and respiratory issues; the root is Ayurveda's anti-inflammatory cornerstone in Dashamoola. Always pair the appropriate part with the matching condition, and prefer dried unripe fruit (Belgiri) over fresh in classical practice.

Safety & Precautions

Bael in its fruit form is widely consumed as food across India and Southeast Asia, with an excellent safety profile at culinary doses. However, medicinal use of unripe fruit, root, and concentrated extracts calls for thoughtful clinical context — particularly given Bilva's hot potency, Pitta-aggravating quality, and documented hypoglycaemic activity.

  • Pregnancy: Avoid medicinal doses entirely. Bael has documented antifertility activity in research models, and concentrated extracts may affect uterine tone. Small culinary amounts of ripe fruit pulp are generally considered safe.
  • Breastfeeding: Insufficient safety data for medicinal use during lactation; consult an Ayurvedic physician before therapeutic use.
  • Antidiabetic medications: Documented hypoglycaemic activity may potentiate insulin and oral antidiabetics (glimepiride, glipizide, metformin). Monitor blood glucose closely if combining; do not stop prescribed diabetes medication without medical supervision.
  • High Pitta & Hyperacidity: Unripe Bael fruit's ushna virya (hot potency) and tikshna (piercing) qualities may aggravate hyperacidity, gastritis, peptic ulcer, or any active Pitta condition. Use ripe fruit instead, or pair with cooling anupanas.
  • Excess ripe fruit: Consuming ripe Bael fruit in excess can cause diarrhoea, abdominal gas, burning sensation, and aggravation of bowel motility — even though the fruit is famous for treating diarrhoea in moderate doses. Stick to recommended quantities.
  • Surgery: Discontinue concentrated extracts at least 2 weeks before scheduled surgery due to possible effects on blood glucose, blood pressure, and clotting time.
  • Antifertility consideration: Couples actively trying to conceive should be aware of documented antifertility activity in animal models. Avoid concentrated extracts during conception attempts.
  • Tree felling: The Bilva tree carries deep spiritual significance — cutting one is traditionally considered inauspicious. Worth noting for those gathering material from temple gardens or sacred groves; ethical sourcing is preferred.
