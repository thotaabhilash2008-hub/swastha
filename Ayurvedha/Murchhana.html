Saptak — The Seven Swaras of the Octave

The seven primary swaras (Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni) and their vikrit (altered) forms from which Murchhanas are derived  ·  Each Murchhana begins on a different swara, rotating the tonal centre

𝄞

From the Sanskrit root mūrch — "to spread, to expand, to fill space" — Murchhana is the systematic rotation of a parent scale (Grama) by beginning successive octaves on each of its constituent notes, generating a family of derived modal scales. Codified by Bharata Muni in the Nāṭyaśāstra over two millennia ago, Murchhanas form the theoretical bridge between ancient Grama theory and the modern Raga system.

01

What is Murchhana?

In Indian classical musicology, a Murchhana is a specific sequence of seven successive notes (swaras) that fills the span of one octave (saptak), obtained by taking a standard scale and rotating its starting point to a different degree. The term encompasses two interrelated meanings: (1) the melodic ascent through all seven notes of an octave — what we might call a modal scale — and (2) the emotional quality or aesthetic mood that such a scale evokes.

The concept is rooted in the two parent scales (Gramas) recognised by Bharata Muni — the Shadja Grama (Sa-grama) and the Madhyama Grama (Ma-grama) — each of which yields seven Murchhanas by starting successively on each of the seven swaras (Sa, Ri, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni), giving 14 Murchhanas in total. A third Grama, the Gandharva Grama, was mentioned but considered no longer in practical use.

Importantly, each Murchhana preserves the same intervallic pattern of the parent Grama — only the tonal centre (Graha) shifts. This is analogous to Western modal theory, where Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian etc. are rotations of the major scale — though the Indian system preceded and differs from Greek modal theory in both its microtonal (shruti-based) precision and its aesthetic-emotional framework.

02

Historical Sources

The theory of Murchhana is primarily codified in three foundational texts of Indian musicology, spanning nearly fifteen centuries of scholarly tradition.

TextAuthor / PeriodContribution to Murchhana Theory
Nāṭyaśāstra Bharata Muni, c. 200 BCE–200 CE First systematic exposition; defines Grama, Murchhana and their 14 forms; describes shruti intervals underpinning each mode
Dattilam Dattila, c. 100 CE Elaborates on Murchhana in context of Gandharva music; discusses emotional (rasa) associations of each mode
Bṛhaddēśī Matanga Muni, c. 8th–9th c. CE Bridges Murchhana and the emerging Raga concept; first text to use the word "Raga" systematically; shows how Murchhanas generate Jatis (melodic types)
Saṅgītaratnākara Sarngadeva, c. 1210–1247 CE Synthesises all prior theory; detailed treatment of 14 Murchhanas, their swaras, shrutis and practical application in Prabandha compositions
Swaramela Kalanidhi Ramamatya, 1550 CE Attempts reconciliation of ancient Grama–Murchhana theory with contemporary Raga practice; pivotal for Carnatic musicology
03

The 7 Murchhanas of Shadja Grama

The Shadja Grama (Sa-grama) is the primary parent scale, analogous to the natural major scale but defined by 22 shrutis (microtonal divisions of the octave) with specific intervallic ratios. Beginning the octave successively on each of the seven swaras yields seven distinct modal scales, each bearing a proper Sanskrit name and a distinctive emotional character.

SG · I
Uttaramandravā
Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa · Dha · Ni
The root Murchhana beginning on Shadja (Sa). Considered the most natural and balanced mode — the Shadja Grama itself. Evokes stability, groundedness, and the quality of the earth element.
SG · II
Rajani
Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa · Dha · Ni · Sa
Beginning on Rishabha (Ri). The interval shift creates a subtle austerity and introspective mood — associated in later Jati theory with evening and contemplative states.
SG · III
Uttarayatā
Ga · Ma · Pa · Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri
Beginning on Gandhara (Ga). Produces a distinctly poignant and tender quality; this Murchhana's intervallic structure later influenced several Bhairavi-family ragas.
SG · IV
Shuddha Shadjavā
Ma · Pa · Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga
Beginning on Madhyama (Ma). One of the most significant — its intervallic structure closely parallels the Western Lydian mode and is considered the gateway to Madhyama Grama derivations.
SG · V
Matsarikṛtā
Pa · Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma
Beginning on Panchama (Pa). Exudes heroic energy and vigour; its open, ascending feel evokes the quality of Vira Rasa (valour). Structurally comparable to the Western Mixolydian.
SG · VI
Ashvakrāntā
Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa
Beginning on Dhaivata (Dha). Named for the "horse's gait" — its irregular interval pattern creates an undulating, dynamic quality associated with movement and transitions.
SG · VII
Abhirudgatā
Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa · Dha
Beginning on Nishada (Ni). The most remote from the tonal centre — evokes yearning, longing, and the quality of Shringar Rasa (romantic love). Its structure influenced several Yaman-family ragas.
04

The 7 Murchhanas of Madhyama Grama

The Madhyama Grama (Ma-grama) differs from Shadja Grama by a single shruti: Panchama is lowered by one shruti (approximately 22 cents), altering the intervallic character of the entire scale family. This subtle difference generates a family of seven Murchhanas with a distinctly different emotional palette — generally considered more introspective, complex, and rarer in practical use.

MG · I
Sauvīrī
Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa♭ · Dha · Ni
Root Murchhana of the Madhyama Grama. The lowered Panchama immediately introduces a plaintive, melancholic quality absent in Uttaramandravā. Evokes Karuna Rasa (compassion, pathos).
MG · II
Hārinī
Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa♭ · Dha · Ni · Sa
Beginning on Rishabha. The "deer-like" mode — swift, graceful, and elusive. Its intervallic asymmetry creates melodic patterns that feel perpetually in motion, never fully settling.
MG · III
Kalopanatā
Ga · Ma · Pa♭ · Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri
Beginning on Gandhara. Described in Dattilam as suited to themes of discord and conflict resolution; the tension created by the lowered Pa from this starting point is particularly pronounced.
MG · IV
Śuddhamadyā
Ma · Pa♭ · Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga
Beginning on Madhyama. The "pure middle" mode; beginning on the note that defines the Grama itself gives this Murchhana a self-referential quality — introspective, philosophical, withdrawn.
MG · V
Mārgavī
Pa♭ · Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma
Beginning on the lowered Panchama — a tonally ambiguous starting point that creates a searching, wayward quality. Associated with the imagery of a traveller on an unknown path.
MG · VI
Pauravi
Dha · Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa♭
Beginning on Dhaivata. The ending of this Murchhana on the lowered Panchama gives it an unresolved, open quality — evocative of dawn, anticipation, and the space between states.
MG · VII
Hṛṣyakā
Ni · Sa · Ri · Ga · Ma · Pa♭ · Dha
Beginning on Nishada. The "joyful" mode — paradoxically, the final Murchhana of the plaintive Madhyama Grama produces a bright, celebratory character through its specific intervallic arrangement.
05

Murchhana, Jati & the Birth of Ragas

The theoretical pathway from ancient Murchhana to the modern Raga system passes through an intermediate concept: Jati. Matanga Muni's Bṛhaddēśī (c. 8th–9th c. CE) describes 18 Jatis — seven Shuddha (pure) and 11 Vikrita (modified) — each derived from one or more Murchhanas by applying specific rules of omission, emphasis, and ornamentation.

Ragas evolved from Jatis by accumulating additional layers of melodic personality: characteristic phrase patterns (vakra), ascending/descending forms (aroha/avaroha), specific ornamentation (gamaka), time-of-day associations, and emotional mood (rasa). While no modern Raga can be said to be simply a Murchhana, the ancient modes provided the modal DNA from which the Raga system grew.

06

Shruti: The Microtonal Foundation

Murchhanas cannot be properly understood without the concept of Shrutis — the 22 microtonal intervals into which the octave is divided in Indian classical theory. The word shruti means "what is heard" — implying intervals so subtle they are perceived rather than consciously analysed. The 22 shrutis are not equal divisions (unlike Western equal temperament) but occur in groups of 2, 3, or 4 shrutis per swara position.

Ri (Rishabha)रि35–7D (major 2nd)
Ga (Gandhara)ग28–9E (major 3rd)
Ma (Madhyama)म410–13F (perfect 4th)

When a Murchhana rotates the tonal centre, the number of shrutis assigned to each position in the new scale changes — because the note that was Pa (4 shrutis) now becomes Sa (also 4 shrutis), but the note that was Ga (2 shrutis) may now occupy the position of Ri (normally 3 shrutis). This redistribution of shrutis is the precise mechanism by which Murchhanas produce their distinct intervallic characters — it is not a simple transposition but a genuine modal transformation at the microtonal level.

07

Aesthetic Doctrine — Rasa & Mood

Classical theorists were explicit that Murchhanas were not merely abstract scale constructions — each mode carried a specific emotional power (rasa) capable of affecting both the performer and the listener. This doctrine, elaborated in the Nāṭyaśāstra's chapter on music (Natyashastra Adhyaya 28), reflects the broader Indian aesthetic philosophy that sound (nada) is inherently charged with emotional and even spiritual significance.

  • Therapeutic use: Ancient Gandharva Veda (the musicological branch of Atharva Veda) prescribed specific Murchhanas for therapeutic purposes — certain modes to calm the mind, others to stimulate vitality or induce sleep. This proto-music-therapy tradition persisted into medieval Raga-Chikitsa (raga-based healing) practice.
  • Ritual context: Specific Murchhanas were assigned to different times of day, seasons, and ritual occasions in the Sama Veda tradition — an early codification of the time-theory that later became central to Raga classification.
  • Cosmological mapping: Each Murchhana was associated with one of the seven planetary deities (Navagraha), the seven colours of light, and the seven chakras of the subtle body — part of a comprehensive correspondence system bridging music, cosmology, and healing.
  • Nava Rasa correlation: Bharata Muni assigned each Murchhana to one of the nine primary aesthetic emotions (Nava Rasas) — Shringar (love), Vira (heroism), Karuna (compassion), Adbhuta (wonder), Hasya (joy), Bhayanaka (fear), Bibhatsa (disgust), Raudra (fury), and Shanta (peace).
  • Modern relevance: Contemporary music psychology research has found that modal structures analogous to ancient Murchhanas consistently evoke cross-culturally stable emotional responses — lending empirical support to Bharata's 2000-year-old aesthetic doctrine.
