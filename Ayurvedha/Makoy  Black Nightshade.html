Makoy
Black Nightshade — Bearer of the Midnight Berry
Solanum nigrum L.  ·  Solanaceae

Fig. 1 — Lustrous black berries of Makoy ripening in characteristic nodding clusters  ·  Fig. 2 — Delicate white star-shaped flowers with prominent yellow anthers

❧

Known in Sanskrit as Kakamachi — "the crow's berry" — Makoy has occupied a place of deep reverence in Ayurvedic medicine since the time of Charaka and Sushruta, who extolled its unparalleled action on the liver (yakrit), spleen (pliha), and urinary system. A plant at once celebrated and feared, its potent alkaloids demand both respect and careful dosing.

01

Botanical Profile

Makoy (Solanum nigrum L.) is a widely distributed annual herbaceous plant of the Solanaceae family — kin to tomato, potato, and eggplant — found across tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions worldwide. It grows erect, branching freely to 30–90 cm in height, with wavy-toothed ovate leaves and characteristic white star-shaped flowers borne in lateral umbellate cymes of 4–8 blossoms, each with five reflexed petals and a prominent cone of yellow anthers.

The berries — small, globose, 6–8 mm in diameter — ripen from green through orange to lustrous black in drooping clusters, resembling diminutive grapes. The plant thrives in disturbed ground, cultivated fields, roadsides, and waste places from sea level to 2,400 m altitude, flowering year-round in tropical India. All parts contain solanaceous alkaloids, with berries being edible when fully ripe (cooked) in many cultures, and toxic when consumed raw or unripe.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Solanum nigrum is one of the most phytochemically investigated Solanaceous herbs. Its diverse secondary metabolite profile — steroidal glycoalkaloids, saponins, polyphenols, flavonoids, and polysaccharides — underpins a remarkably broad pharmacological spectrum. The principal alkaloids, solanine and solamargine, are steroidal glycoalkaloids concentrated in the unripe berries and green aerial parts; ripe black berries have substantially lower alkaloid content.

The plant's polysaccharides and glycoproteins have attracted particular attention for their immunomodulatory and hepatoprotective activities, with several clinical and experimental studies corroborating the classical Ayurvedic reputation of Kakamachi as the foremost liver tonic of the Indian pharmacopoeia.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Kakamachi is cited in Charaka Samhita's Jwara Chikitsa (fever management) and Sushruta Samhita's Dravyasamgraha. The Ashtanga Hridayam places it foremost among herbs in Panchakola formulations and liver tonics. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu catalogues it in Guduchyadi Varga, praising its bitterness as a purifying force on the blood and liver.

The classical shloka — "Kakamachi tikta laghu ushna pittakaphapaha, shothajwaravranashodhana yakritpliha cha nasyate" — establishes its taste as bitter, quality as light, potency as hot, and therapeutic scope as encompassing fever, inflammation, wound purification, and diseases of the liver and spleen.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern research has positioned Makoy among the most pharmacologically versatile Solanaceous herbs — with validated hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, diuretic, analgesic, and antimicrobial activities backed by a growing body of clinical and experimental evidence.

Hepatoprotective — Liver Tonic
The crown jewel of Makoy's therapeutic profile. Multiple studies confirm that leaf extracts significantly protect against CCl₄- and paracetamol-induced hepatotoxicity, reducing serum AST, ALT, ALP, and bilirubin. The proprietary formulation Liv.52 (Himalaya) prominently features Kakamachi as a core ingredient — one of the most widely prescribed liver tonics globally.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Ethanolic and aqueous leaf extracts produce significant dose-dependent inhibition of carrageenan-induced paw edema in animal models. The saponins and steroidal alkaloids inhibit COX-2 and pro-inflammatory cytokine pathways, providing relief in arthritis, gout, and inflammatory skin conditions.
Anticancer Potential
Solamargine and solanine have demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines including HeLa (cervical), A549 (lung), MCF-7 (breast), and HepG2 (hepatic). Polysaccharide fractions enhance NK cell and macrophage activity. Research remains at in vitro and in vivo stages — not yet clinically validated as therapy.
Diuretic & Kidney Support
Classical indication for dysuria, urinary calculi, and nephritis. Whole plant decoction increases urine output and reduces renal inflammatory markers. Used in combination with Punarnava and Gokshura in classical formulations for urinary tract disorders and fluid retention.
Antipyretic & Anti-infective
A primary classical use — leaf decoction and berry juice used in remittent and intermittent fevers. Extract inhibits growth of Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella, Salmonella typhi, and Candida albicans. Traditional role as a broad-spectrum anti-infective herb in folk traditions across India and Africa.
Skin Diseases & Wound Healing
Leaf paste is a traditional treatment for psoriasis, eczema, scabies, and chronic skin ulcers. Anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial alkaloids reduce infection and accelerate granulation tissue formation. Juice mixed with sesame oil is a classical preparation for soothing inflammatory skin conditions and burns.
Digestive & Gastric Protection
Ripe berry juice and leaf decoction are used traditionally in dyspepsia, colitis, IBS, and dysentery. Gastroprotective activity has been confirmed against ethanol-induced gastric ulcers in animal models, with mechanism attributed to flavonoid-mediated reduction of mucosal oxidative stress.
Nutritive — Leafy Vegetable
Across South India, West Africa, and Southeast Asia, tender leaves of Makoy are cooked as a highly nutritious pot-herb rich in iron, calcium, vitamin C, and protein. Cooking deactivates much of the alkaloid content. Known as Manathakkali keerai in Tamil Nadu, it is a staple remedy for mouth ulcers when consumed as a stir-fry with coconut.

How to Use Makoy

Makoy is used fresh, dried, as juice, decoction, oil infusion, and as a cooked vegetable. The critical distinction across all preparations is between ripe berries (safer, edible when cooked), unripe green berries (alkaloid-rich, potentially toxic), and leaves/roots (safe when prepared correctly at classical doses). All internal medicinal use should be under qualified Ayurvedic guidance.

Safety & Precautions

Makoy's safety profile is nuanced. Ripe berries and cooked leaves have a long history of safe dietary use across many cultures. However, the plant's steroidal glycoalkaloids — particularly concentrated in the unripe berries and raw aerial parts — are genuinely toxic in excess, causing gastrointestinal distress, neurological symptoms, and in severe cases, cardiac arrhythmia. The therapeutic window is respected in classical Ayurveda through careful dosing and processing.

  • Unripe berries: Strictly avoid. Green and orange-stage berries contain the highest solanine concentrations and are a significant poisoning risk, especially for children who may be attracted by their appearance.
  • Pregnancy: Contraindicated for internal medicinal use. The plant has uterine-stimulating alkaloids that may pose risk. Cooked leaves as a routine vegetable in modest quantity may be acceptable — consult practitioner.
  • Children: Medicinal doses are not appropriate for children without specialist supervision. Berry ingestion by children should be treated as a poisoning event — seek emergency care.
  • Overdosage symptoms: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, burning of mouth/throat, dilated pupils, tachycardia, confusion, and drowsiness. Seek immediate medical attention if suspected solanine toxicity.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate anticholinergic drugs, sedatives, and antihypertensive medications. Avoid combining with other hepatotoxic herbs or medications without physician oversight.
  • Pitta conditions: Despite Pitta-pacifying classical action, its Ushna virya may aggravate acute hyperacidity or active peptic ulcer — use cautiously in such individuals.
  • Prolonged use: Extended internal use beyond 4–6 weeks warrants monitoring. Alkaloid accumulation over time is a theoretical concern; periodic breaks are advisable.
  • Confusable species: Do not confuse with Solanum americanum (American black nightshade) or Solanum lycocarpum — only S. nigrum is the classical Kakamachi of Ayurvedic texts. Proper botanical identification is essential.
