Priyangu
Beauty Berry — The Beloved Flowering Creeper
Callicarpa macrophylla Vahl  ·  Callicarpa tomentosa (L.) Murr.

Fig. 1 — Jewel-like violet-purple berries of Callicarpa in dense ornamental clusters  ·  Fig. 2 — Soft, tomentose leaves and pale pink-lilac flowers of Priyangu in bloom

❧

Named from the Sanskrit Priya (beloved, dear) and angu (limb, body) — "that which is pleasing to the body" — Priyangu is one of Ayurveda's most elegant cooling herbs, celebrated in Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita alike for its role in Pitta shamana, its lustrous jewel-like berries a symbol of its gift to the skin, blood, and reproductive tissues.

01

Botanical Profile

Priyangu (Callicarpa macrophylla Vahl, syn. Callicarpa tomentosa) is a large, erect, deciduous shrub or small tree of the Lamiaceae (formerly Verbenaceae) family, growing to 2–4 m in height. Native to subtropical and tropical regions of the Indian subcontinent — particularly the sub-Himalayan tracts, Eastern Himalayas, Assam, Western Ghats, and Sri Lanka — it thrives in moist deciduous forests, forest margins, and riverbanks up to 1,500 m altitude.

The plant is immediately recognisable by its large, broadly ovate leaves (up to 30 cm long), densely covered in white or pale-buff stellate (star-shaped) tomentum on the underside — giving them a soft, velvety feel. The inflorescences are cymose, bearing numerous tiny pale-pink to lilac four-petalled flowers. But it is the fruit that makes Priyangu unmistakable: dense ornamental clusters of small, glossy, deep violet-purple drupes, approximately 2–3 mm in diameter, that persist on the bare branches into winter — earning the genus its common name "Beautyberry." It is these berries and the root bark that constitute the primary medicinal parts.

Phytochemistry Composition

Phytochemical investigation of Callicarpa macrophylla has revealed a rich and structurally diverse secondary metabolome — with diterpenoids, flavonoids, phenylethanoid glycosides, triterpenoids, and iridoids being the dominant classes. Calliterpenone, a potent clerodane diterpenoid first isolated from this species, has attracted significant pharmacological interest for its mosquito-repellent and antifeedant properties. The root bark is particularly dense in bioactive triterpenoids and sterols; the leaves and fruits are rich in flavonoids and polyphenols responsible for the antioxidant and Pitta-cooling actions.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Priyangu occupies a distinguished position in the classical Nighantu and Samhita literature. Charaka classifies it in the Priyangvadi Gana — a group of drugs specifically indicated for Pitta disorders, haemorrhagic conditions, and disorders of the skin and blood (Raktapitta, Kushtha). Sushruta includes it among the Priyangvadi Gana and Nyagrodhadi Gana, recognising its astringent, haemostatic, and wound-healing properties. It is a key ingredient in classical formulations including Chandanasava, Priyangu Ghrita, and Kankayan Vati.

Bhavaprakasha Nighantu catalogues Priyangu in the Haritakyadi Varga with the classical verse: "Priyanguh kashaya tikta sheetala kaphapitanut / Vranahara jwaraghni cha raktapitanivarhini" — describing it as astringent, bitter, cooling, pacifying of Kapha and Pitta, healing wounds, reducing fever, and arresting haemorrhage.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research continues to validate the extensive classical applications of Priyangu — demonstrating significant antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, haemostatic, and insect-repellent activities through rigorous in vitro and in vivo studies.

Haemostatic & Bleeding Disorders
Classical primary indication — Priyangu is Ayurveda's foremost herb for Raktapitta (haemorrhagic conditions including menorrhagia, haemoptysis, epistaxis, rectal bleeding). Tannins and ellagic acid constrict blood vessels and promote clot formation; acteoside stabilises platelet function. A cold decoction of the fruit is the classic preparation.
Skin & Complexion (Varnya)
Priyangu is one of the eight classical Varnya (skin-brightening) dravyas. Its sheeta virya reduces inflammatory hyperpigmentation, redness, and Pitta-origin skin disorders including psoriasis, eczema, and contact dermatitis. Topical preparations of fruit and leaf paste are used for Kushtha (skin diseases), improving skin lustre and reducing blemishes.
Fever & Antipyretic Action
A classical Jwaraghna (anti-pyretic) herb, particularly effective in Pitta-type fevers with burning sensations, thirst, and delirium. Root bark decoction was traditionally used in malarial fevers — supported by modern studies showing anti-malarial activity attributed to calliterpenone and related diterpenoids.
Insect Repellent & Anti-malarial
Among the most pharmacologically validated properties of the genus — callicarpenal and calliterpenone isolated from C. macrophylla show potent repellent activity against Aedes and Anopheles mosquitoes. Leaf extracts have been used in traditional insect-repellent formulations; modern DEET-comparator studies confirm significant efficacy.
Gynaecological & Reproductive
Classical use in dysmenorrhea, menorrhagia, and post-partum care. Acacetin and nepitrin demonstrate mild uterotonic properties; the haemostatic action controls excessive menstrual bleeding. Used in formulations like Chandanasava and Pushyanuga Churna — classical proprietary Ayurvedic formulas for female reproductive health.
Antimicrobial & Wound Healing
Leaf and root bark extracts demonstrate broad-spectrum activity against E. coli, S. aureus, Klebsiella, Salmonella, and Candida albicans. Applied topically as a paste for Vrana (wounds, ulcers) and Dushta Vrana (infected wounds), with astringent tannins promoting tissue contraction and flavonoids reducing wound inflammation.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Ursolic acid, oleanolic acid, and acteoside inhibit COX-2 and NF-κB mediated inflammatory pathways. Ethanolic root bark extract demonstrates significant analgesic activity in hot-plate and acetic-acid-induced writhing models, corroborating classical use in Vedana (pain) and Shotha (swelling).
Antioxidant & Hepatoprotective
The polyphenol-rich fruit extract shows high DPPH and FRAP antioxidant capacity; acteoside (verbascoside) provides documented hepatoprotection against oxidative liver damage. Classical use in Pitta-originated liver inflammation (Pittaja Yakritvikara) and toxic conditions is corroborated by these findings.

Priyangu is an ingredient in numerous classical Ayurvedic formulations recorded in the Samhitas and Nighantu texts. It appears in formulations targeting bleeding disorders, fever, skin diseases, and gynaecological conditions — most often combined with other Pitta-cooling or haemostatic herbs.

How to Consume Priyangu

Priyangu is used internally and externally. The fruit is the most widely used part internally; root bark decoctions are used for fever and infections; leaf pastes and fruit pastes applied topically for skin conditions and wound healing. Always use under the guidance of a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner for therapeutic purposes.

Safety & Precautions

Priyangu has an excellent safety profile when used at classical doses and is well-tolerated in most constitutions. Its primary caution relates to its sheeta virya (cold potency) and kashaya rasa (astringent quality) — which, while therapeutic in Pitta conditions, can aggravate Vata and cause constipation or cold-related disorders if misused.

  • Pregnancy: Use with caution — the uterotonic flavonoids (acacetin, nepitrin) may stimulate uterine contractions. Internal use in pregnancy should only be under direct classical physician supervision.
  • Vata-dominant constitutions: The cold, dry, and astringent qualities can aggravate Vata — leading to constipation, bloating, or coldness. Use with warm anupana (vehicle) like warm water with a pinch of ginger, or combine with ghee.
  • Constipation: The strongly astringent tannin content can cause or worsen constipation, particularly at higher doses. Contraindicated in severe constipation or obstruction.
  • Hypothyroidism & cold constitution: Its sheeta (cold) virya is not recommended for individuals with hypothyroid conditions, Kapha-dominant constitutions with coldness, or those prone to cold extremities.
  • Drug interactions: Potential additive effect with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs (due to haemostatic tannins and acteoside) — consult physician before combining with warfarin, aspirin, or clopidogrel.
  • Long-term internal use: Extended use beyond 8–12 weeks without physician oversight is not recommended. High-dose tannin consumption over long periods may reduce mineral absorption and impair digestion.
  • Children: Fruit consumption in small amounts as food is safe; concentrated therapeutic preparations should only be given to children under specialist Ayurvedic supervision.
  • Topical caution: Leaf and fruit paste occasionally causes mild contact irritation in hypersensitive individuals — perform a patch test before broad topical application.
