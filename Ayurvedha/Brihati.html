"Brihati" simply means big — the larger of the two great nightshade-root sisters of Ayurvedic respiratory medicine. With its smaller twin Kantakari, it forms the legendary Brihati Dwaya, the cornerstone of every Dashamoola formulation. From cough to asthma, from fever to digestive sluggishness, when Kapha clings stubbornly to the lungs, the larger root is sent.

01

Botanical Profile

Brihati (Solanum indicum L.) is a perennial spiny shrub of the Solanaceae family — the nightshade family that also gives us tomato, potato, and chilli — reaching 1–1.5 metres in height. The stems are woody at the base, branching, and densely armed with sharp recurved spines. Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate, sinuately lobed, dark green above and pale below, also bearing scattered prickles along the veins. The plant has a faintly bitter, characteristic Solanaceae aroma when leaves are crushed.

The flowers are stunning — pale purple-blue, star-shaped with five petals fused at the base, and prominent bright-yellow anthers forming a small cone at the centre. The signature fruit is the round, smooth, glossy berry, 1.5–2 cm in diameter, ripening from green to deep purple-black — earning the English name "poison berry" for its toxicity when consumed unprocessed in large amounts. Native to the tropical and subtropical Indian subcontinent up to 1500 m altitude, Brihati is widely distributed across India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, and tropical Asia. Classical Ayurveda recognises Brihati as one of the ten foundational roots of Dashamoola — the cornerstone formulation of Ayurvedic pharmacy. Its smaller pair, Kantakari (Solanum xanthocarpum), forms with it the famous Brihati Dwaya.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

Brihati's pharmacological power rests on its rich profile of steroidal alkaloids — the same compound family that gives the entire nightshade genus its characteristic bioactivity. The flagship compound is solasodine — a steroidal alkaloid responsible for much of the plant's expectorant and respiratory action, and a precursor used industrially in steroid hormone synthesis. Alongside solasodine sit solanidine, α-solanine, solamargine, solasonine, solanocarpone, and other nightshade-family alkaloids.

Beyond alkaloids, Brihati contains diosgenin (another steroidal saponin), β-sitosterol, lanosterol, carpesterol, carotene, vitamin C, and notably high phosphorus content. A 2021 Saxena et al. phytochemistry study using HPTLC fingerprinting confirmed the alkaloid profile, while a 2012 IJPPR study by Jayanthi et al. catalogued the plant's broad pharmacological spectrum. Together, these compounds explain Brihati's documented antibacterial, antispasmodic, expectorant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory pharmacology.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Brihati is one of the foundational herbs of Ayurvedic pharmacology — it is one of the ten roots that compose Dashamoola, the most celebrated polyherbal preparation in the entire materia medica. Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita repeatedly invoke Brihati for Kasa (cough), Shvasa (asthma, dyspnoea), Jwara (fever), Aruchi (anorexia), Hridroga (heart disease), Mutrakrichra (dysuria), and as a general Deepana-Pachana (digestive-fire kindler).

Its rasa (taste) is katu (pungent) and tikta (bitter); guna is laghu (light) and ruksha (dry); vipaka is katu (pungent); and veerya is ushna (hot). The hot, light, dry qualities make Brihati a foundational Vata-Kapha pacifier — but its heat may aggravate Pitta. Brihati features in some of Ayurveda's most beloved formulations: Dashamoola Kwath, Dashmularishta, Nalikerasavam, Ajamamsa Rasayanam, and Manasamitra Vatakam. Together with Kantakari, it forms the famous Brihati Dwaya — frequently invoked in classical respiratory and digestive formulations.

04

Key Health Benefits

As a Dashamoola root, Brihati shares much of its therapeutic territory with the other Dashamoola herbs but is distinguished by its particularly strong respiratory, expectorant, and antibacterial action — driven by solasodine and the broader steroidal alkaloid ensemble. Modern pharmacological research has confirmed its activity across respiratory, digestive, urinary, antimicrobial, and even neuroprotective domains.

Cough, Asthma & Bronchitis
The flagship classical use. Solasodine acts as a powerful expectorant, thinning mucus and easing its expulsion from the airways. Used since antiquity for chronic cough (Kasa), bronchial asthma (Shvasa), bronchitis, and Kaphaja respiratory congestion — a cornerstone of Dashamoola Kwath and Dashmularishta.
Digestive & Appetiser (Deepana)
Classical Deepana-Pachana action. Brihati stimulates digestive fire (Agni), kindles appetite in cases of Aruchi (anorexia), reduces abdominal bloating, and clears Ama (metabolic toxin). Used traditionally as part of pre-meal protocols to restore healthy digestion.
Anti-inflammatory (Dashamoola)
Brihati's role within Dashamoola — Ayurveda's foundational anti-inflammatory pharmacy — addresses joint pain, arthritis, sciatica, gout, and post-injury inflammation. Solanocarpone and steroidal saponins inhibit inflammatory cascades; the polyherbal Dashamoola context amplifies the effect.
Diuretic & Urinary Health
Documented use in Mutrakrichra (dysuria, painful urination) and chronic urinary tract conditions. Acts as a gentle diuretic, supporting urine flow and clearing urinary stagnation. Useful as adjunct in chronic UTIs and renal calculi management under Ayurvedic supervision.
Cardiotonic & Hridroga
Classical reference in Hridroga (heart disease) — supports cardiac function, modulates blood pressure, and enhances circulation. Steroidal alkaloids and phytosterols together support cardiovascular health; phosphorus content contributes to muscle and nerve function.
Antimicrobial & Antibacterial
Documented broad-spectrum activity — α-solanine and related alkaloids inhibit bacterial growth; anti-dermatophytic action useful in fungal skin infections. Anthelmintic activity supports traditional use in worm infestations and chronic gastrointestinal infections.
Antipyretic & Fever Relief
Used classically in Jwara (fever), particularly Vata-Kaphaja fever types. Combined with other Dashamoola roots, Brihati helps reduce body temperature, ease body aches, and support recovery from chronic intermittent fevers — including malaria-pattern presentations.
Anticancer & Neuroprotective
Modern research has documented anticancer activity of solamargine and solasonine across multiple cancer cell lines, and emerging evidence of neuroprotective effects from steroidal alkaloids. Promising preclinical signals — clinical research remains in early stages; not a substitute for primary cancer therapy.

How to Consume Brihati

Brihati is exclusively medicinal — never consumed as food, and the unprocessed berries are mildly toxic in large amounts. Always use Brihati under qualified Ayurvedic supervision; classical practice prefers polyherbal preparations like Dashamoola Kwath rather than standalone single-herb consumption. The roots are by far the most heavily used part, prepared as decoction, powder, or as a component of polyherbal formulations.

Safety & Precautions

Brihati's nightshade-family steroidal alkaloid profile means real toxicological considerations — α-solanine is a known glycoalkaloid that can cause gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms at high doses. Classical Ayurvedic dosing has built in centuries of safety knowledge; modern usage should respect those traditional dosage limits and avoid concentrated single-compound extracts without practitioner supervision.

  • Pregnancy: Avoid entirely. Solanaceae steroidal alkaloids may have teratogenic and uterine-stimulating effects. Brihati is not used during pregnancy in classical Ayurvedic practice.
  • Breastfeeding: Avoid medicinal doses during lactation. Limited safety data; alkaloids may pass into breast milk. Consult a qualified Ayurvedic physician before any therapeutic use.
  • High Pitta & Hyperacidity: Ushna virya (hot potency) and tikshna quality may aggravate hyperacidity, gastritis, peptic ulcer, and Pitta-driven bleeding disorders. Avoid during active flare-ups.
  • Solanine toxicity: Unprocessed berries and high-dose extracts can cause solanine-related symptoms — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, and in severe cases neurological symptoms. Stay strictly within classical dose limits (3–6 g powder or 30–50 ml decoction).
  • Species substitution caution: Solanum anguivi and Solanum ferox are sometimes used in place of authentic Solanum indicum; pharmacology overlaps but is not identical. Source from reputable Ayurvedic suppliers — preferably with API-compliant authentication.
  • MAOI & psychiatric medications: Steroidal alkaloids may interact with monoamine oxidase inhibitors and certain psychiatric medications. Consult a physician before combining.
  • Surgery: Discontinue concentrated extracts at least 2 weeks before scheduled surgery due to possible effects on inflammation, blood pressure, and clotting time.
  • Prefer polyherbal context: Classical practice almost always uses Brihati within Dashamoola formulations rather than as a standalone single herb — the polyherbal context buffers individual alkaloid intensity. Self-medicating with single-herb Brihati is not recommended.
