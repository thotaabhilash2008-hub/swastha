Known across South Asia as Kadi Patta, this small tropical tree is far more than a tadka ingredient — honoured in Ayurveda as Surabhi and Krishnanim, its leaves, bark, roots, and berries have been used for millennia to pacify Kapha and Pitta, strengthen digestion, sharpen vision, and purify the blood, earning it a place in both the kitchen and the classical formulary.

01

Botanical Profile

Murraya koenigii is a small, aromatic, nearly evergreen tree or large shrub of the Rutaceae family (the citrus family), native to the Indian subcontinent and Sri Lanka, now naturalised throughout tropical and subtropical Asia. It typically grows to 4–6 m in height, with a slender trunk, spreading crown, and dark green, glossy, pinnate leaves bearing 11–21 leaflets — each oblique-ovate, acuminate, and intensely aromatic when bruised, owing to a rich suite of volatile carbazole alkaloids and essential oils.

Small white flowers are borne in terminal corymbs and carry a sweet, jasmine-like fragrance. The fruits ripen to a shiny black berry containing a single large seed. The tree is propagated through seeds or root suckers, prefers well-drained, fertile soil, and thrives in warm, humid climates — commonly found in home gardens, forest margins, and village groves across peninsular India.

02

Phytochemical Composition

Murraya koenigii is one of the most phytochemically rich members of the Rutaceae family. Over 200 compounds have been identified across its parts, with carbazole alkaloids — a class unique to and emblematic of this species — being the most pharmacologically significant. The leaves alone contain a complex volatile essential oil (0.8–2.6% yield) dominated by sabinene, β-caryophyllene, α-pinene, and β-phellandrene, which accounts for the herb's distinctive aroma.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Murraya koenigii is described in the classical Ayurvedic texts under the name Surabhi (the fragrant one) and Girinimba (the mountain nimba, owing to its resemblance to neem in leaf form but sweetness in taste). Charaka and Sushruta both reference Nimba kula preparations with affinity for Kapha and Pitta disorders of the digestive and dermal systems. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu classifies it under Aamra Phaladi Varga, attributing to it the properties of deepana (digestive stimulant), pachana (carminative), krimighna (anthelmintic), and raktashodhaka (blood purifier).

The classical shloka describes: "Surabhi tikta katu sheetala laghu, Kapha-pittahara, chakshu, raktashodhana" — bitter and pungent in taste, cooling in effect, light in quality, pacifying Kapha and Pitta, beneficial to the eyes, and purifying to the blood.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has validated Murraya koenigii's remarkable range of therapeutic activities — its antidiabetic, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, antidiarrhoeal, and anti-obesity properties are among the most extensively studied, supported by both in vitro and in vivo evidence.

Antidiabetic & Glycaemic Control
The carbazole alkaloid mahanimbine significantly inhibits α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes, slowing postprandial glucose absorption. Animal studies show leaf extract lowers blood glucose comparably to metformin. Traditionally recommended for Prameha (diabetes and metabolic disorders) — a key use in folk Ayurveda of South India.
Digestive Stimulant & Carminative
The quintessential digestive herb of South Asian cooking — fresh leaves in tadka stimulate digestive enzymes, relieve flatulence, nausea, and indigestion. Classically indicated for ajirna (indigestion), aruchi (anorexia), and grahani (IBS). Leaf decoction is a traditional remedy for diarrhoea and dysentery.
Antioxidant & Anti-ageing
Among the highest antioxidant activities reported in any culinary herb — DPPH radical scavenging attributed to carbazole alkaloids, quercetin, rutin, and vitamin C. Protects cells from oxidative stress, reduces lipid peroxidation, and is associated with anti-ageing effects in traditional dermatological use (Varnya karma).
Hair Growth & Scalp Health
Perhaps its most iconic folk use in modern times — curry leaf oil or paste applied to the scalp is traditionally used to prevent premature greying, reduce hair fall, and stimulate follicles. Research attributes these effects to high iron content, antioxidants preventing follicle oxidative damage, and alkaloids improving scalp microcirculation.
Hepatoprotective Action
Mahanimbine and carbazole-3-carbaldehyde demonstrate significant liver protection — reducing hepatic enzymes (ALT, AST), preventing lipid accumulation, and attenuating paracetamol-induced hepatotoxicity in animal models. Traditionally used in pandu (anaemia) and kamala (jaundice) to support liver function and bile flow.
Anti-obesity & Lipid Regulation
Mahanimbine demonstrated significant reduction in body weight, fat accumulation, cholesterol, and triglycerides in high-fat-diet mouse models. Mechanism involves inhibition of pancreatic lipase and adipogenesis. Supports classical use in sthaulya (obesity) and medoroga (hyperlipidaemia).
Antimicrobial & Anti-infective
Leaf and bark extracts show broad-spectrum activity against E. coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis in vitro. Root bark paste is a traditional application for skin infections, scabies, and fungal conditions — classical Krimighna (anti-parasitic) karma.
Vision & Eye Health (Chaksushya)
Classically listed as Chaksushya — beneficial to the eyes — due to high β-carotene and Vitamin A content in the leaves. Regular consumption is a traditional preventive for night blindness and age-related macular degeneration. Vitamin A concentration rivals that of carrots on a fresh-weight basis.

How to Consume Murraya

Murraya koenigii is uniquely versatile — equally a daily kitchen ingredient and a classical Ayurvedic medicinal. For therapeutic use, fresh leaves are always superior to dried; the volatile alkaloids and essential oils that carry most of the pharmacological activity degrade significantly upon drying. Internal preparations are generally safe for long-term use when consumed as food; concentrated extracts warrant physician guidance.

Safety & Precautions

Murraya koenigii is among the safest Ayurvedic herbs for long-term use when consumed as food — its millennia-long use as a daily culinary ingredient in South Asian cooking is itself a safety record. Therapeutic doses in the form of extracts or concentrated supplements warrant a little more care in specific contexts.

  • Pregnancy: Culinary use (8–15 leaves in cooking) is safe and nutritious. Concentrated leaf juice or root preparations in high doses are traditionally considered uterine stimulants and should be avoided. Consult physician before supplemental use.
  • Hypoglycaemia risk: Individuals on antidiabetic medications (insulin, metformin, sulphonylureas) should monitor blood glucose closely — additive hypoglycaemic effect is well-documented. Dose adjustment of pharmaceutical agents may be necessary.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate anticoagulant medications (warfarin) due to Vitamin K content in large doses. Use with caution alongside hepatic CYP450-metabolised drugs, as carbazole alkaloids may modulate enzyme activity.
  • Vata-dominant constitutions: The cooling virya and bitter taste may occasionally aggravate Vata in highly depleted or constitutionally cold individuals — combine with a warming carrier (ghee, ginger) if this is a concern.
  • Allergy: Rare contact dermatitis reported from topical oil application in Rutaceae-sensitive individuals. Patch test before applying curry leaf oil over large skin areas.
  • Concentrated supplements: Standardised extract capsules at doses above 500 mg should be taken with medical supervision — carbazole alkaloids show cytotoxic activity at high concentrations in cell studies.
  • Kidney stones: High calcium content in very large therapeutic doses is theoretically relevant in calcium-oxalate stone formers — though culinary amounts pose no concern.
