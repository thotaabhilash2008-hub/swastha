Kushtha
Costus — The King of Aromatic Roots
Saussurea costus (Falc.) Lipsch.  ·  Syn. Saussurea lappa · Aucklandia costus

Fig. 1 — Dried root pieces of Kushtha showing characteristic gnarled, aromatic wood  ·  Fig. 2 — Saussurea costus growing in its native sub-alpine Kashmir habitat

❧

Revered as Divyaushadhi — a divine herb — in the Atharva Veda, and eulogised across the Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, and Ashtanga Hridayam, Kushtha is one of Ayurveda's most celebrated aromatic roots. Grown in the fragrant sub-alpine meadows of the Himalayas, its rich, resinous aroma has made it a cornerstone of classical formulations for over 3,000 years — from fumigation of newborns to treatment of respiratory disease, skin disorders, and rheumatism.

01

Botanical Profile

Kushtha (Saussurea costus) is a tall, stout perennial herb of the Asteraceae family, native to sub-alpine meadows of the Himalayan range — primarily Kashmir (2,500–4,000 m), northern Pakistan, and Sikkim. It grows to 1–2 m in height, producing large, irregularly lobed basal leaves up to 1 m long, with a stem that is ribbed and woolly. The inflorescence bears dark violet to purple thistle-like flower heads in terminal clusters, blooming August to October.

The therapeutic material is the root — a large, stout taproot, up to 60 cm long and 8 cm in diameter, with a deeply furrowed dark brown exterior and a pale yellowish interior. When dried, the root yields a characteristically intense, camphoraceous-earthy-sweet aroma — the result of a dense sesquiterpene lactone and essential oil profile — rendering it one of the most prized aromatic botanicals in both Ayurvedic and Unani medicine and one of the oldest traded aromatics in the ancient world.

Conservation status: Saussurea costus is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List and under CITES Appendix I, protecting it from international commercial trade. Wildcrafting is severely restricted; cultivation programmes in Himachal Pradesh and Jammu & Kashmir are the primary legal source.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

The Kushtha root is chemically rich, dominated by sesquiterpene lactones — particularly costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone — which are responsible for its characteristic aroma and the bulk of its anti-inflammatory, antitumour, and bronchodilatory pharmacological activity. The essential oil (0.3–3%) contains a complex array of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and oxygenated derivatives, alongside alkaloids, sterols, polyacetylenes, and resinous compounds.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Kushtha holds one of the most prominent positions in the classical Ayurvedic materia medica. In the Atharva Veda (Kanda 19, Sukta 39), it is invoked as a divine remedy grown in the Himalayas, brought down by an eagle. Charaka assigns it to the Vata-Kapha shamaka dravyas and employs it extensively in Kasa (cough), Shwasa (bronchial asthma), Hridroga (cardiac conditions), Kushtha (skin diseases), Jwara (fever), and Vatarakta (gout).

Sushruta classifies it under Eladi Gana and Arkadi Gana, using it prominently as a component of medicated oils and ghees for skin and neurological disorders. In Ashtanga Hridayam, Vagbhata extols it as an ingredient in Kushtha Chikitsa (paradoxically — the herb "Kushtha" used to treat the disease "kushtha" / leprosy) and respiratory formulations. The classical text declares: "Kushtham katu tikta ushnam, vata kaphaharam param" — Kushtha is pungent, bitter, hot-potency, and supreme among Vata-Kapha alleviating herbs.

A revered classical use is Dhoopana (fumigation) — burning dried Kushtha root to purify the air, protect newborns, and treat respiratory conditions — a practice documented in Kashyapa Samhita and Sushruta Samhita. Its antimicrobial volatile compounds lend modern scientific validity to this ancient practice.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has extensively validated Kushtha's classical applications — with costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone emerging as the dominant bioactive compounds behind its anti-inflammatory, bronchodilatory, antitumour, hepatoprotective, and antimicrobial properties.

Respiratory & Bronchodilatory
Its most celebrated classical application. The alkaloid saussurine directly relaxes bronchial smooth muscle. Root powder decoctions are used in bronchial asthma (Shwasa Roga), chronic cough (Kasa), pleurisy, and rhinitis. Dhoopana (fumigation) with root vapour provides rapid bronchial relief. Multiple studies have confirmed spasmolytic and bronchodilatory activity.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone potently inhibit NF-κB pathway activation — a master regulator of inflammatory gene expression. Validated in models of arthritis, colitis, and neuroinflammation. Classical use in Vatarakta (gouty arthritis), Amavata (rheumatoid arthritis), and Sandhivata (osteoarthritis) is well-supported by contemporary evidence.
Skin Disease (Kushtha Roga)
Paradoxically, the herb Kushtha is the classical treatment for the disease kushtha (skin disorders, including leprosy). Root paste, oils, and fumigation are used for eczema, psoriasis, vitiligo, and fungal skin infections. Antifungal and antimicrobial activity of costol and sesquiterpene lactones is well-documented against dermatophytes and Candida species.
Antitumour & Cytotoxic
Costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone have demonstrated significant cytotoxic activity against multiple cancer cell lines — including breast, colon, cervical, and hepatocellular carcinoma — by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting tumour cell proliferation. Betulinic acid adds anti-HIV and antitumour properties. This remains an active area of pharmaceutical research.
Hepatoprotective
Costunolide has shown significant liver-protective activity in carbon tetrachloride-induced hepatotoxicity models, reducing liver enzymes (ALT, AST) and restoring hepatic histology. Classically used in Yakrit Vikara (liver disorders) and as part of detoxification formulations in Panchakarma preparatory treatments.
Digestive & Carminative
Classified as Deepaniya (digestive stimulant) and Pachana (digestive) in classical texts. Stimulates gastric secretions and bile flow, relieves flatulence, intestinal colic (Shula), anorexia (Aruchi), and worm infestation (Krimighna). Inulin content acts as a prebiotic, supporting gut microflora.
Cardiac Tonic (Hridya)
Listed among Hridya dravyas (cardiac-supporting herbs) in Charaka Samhita. Cardiotonic properties are attributed to its ability to improve circulation, reduce vascular inflammation, and regulate lipid metabolism. Traditionally combined with Arjuna (Terminalia arjuna) in cardiac formulations.
Antimicrobial & Antiparasitic
Broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and parasites. Historically used as a fumigant to purify spaces and protect against infectious disease — a classical epidemiological application recorded in Kashyapa Samhita for neonatal ward fumigation that has genuine scientific basis.

How to Consume Kushtha

Kushtha is used both internally and externally across classical and folk traditions. Due to its CITES-protected status, it should be sourced from legally cultivated, certified suppliers. Internal use must always be supervised by a qualified Ayurvedic physician, as the potent sesquiterpene lactone content requires careful dosing. The root is the primary therapeutic material.

Safety & Precautions

Kushtha is generally safe at classical therapeutic doses under qualified supervision. Its potent sesquiterpene lactones — particularly costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone — can cause adverse effects at excessive doses or with prolonged use. Additionally, its endangered status makes adulteration of commercial products a genuine concern; sourcing from verified, certified suppliers is essential.

  • Pregnancy: Avoid internal use. Kushtha has traditionally been classified as a uterine stimulant (Garbhashaya Sankochaka) and should not be consumed internally during pregnancy; topical use in small amounts is generally considered safe.
  • Contact dermatitis: Sesquiterpene lactones are well-known contact allergens. Patch-test before topical application, especially in sensitive individuals or those with known Asteraceae/Compositae family allergies (ragweed, chrysanthemum, chamomile).
  • Prolonged high-dose use: Extended internal use at high doses may cause hepatotoxicity due to concentrated sesquiterpene lactone exposure. Liver function monitoring is advisable with prolonged therapeutic courses exceeding 4–6 weeks.
  • Children: Internal use in children under 12 requires specialist supervision and significantly reduced dosing. Dhoopana (fumigation) should be done with excellent ventilation to avoid irritation.
  • Drug interactions: May potentiate anticoagulant effects; use cautiously with warfarin, aspirin, or other blood-thinning medications. May also interact with cytochrome P450-metabolised drugs due to sesquiterpene lactone enzyme inhibition.
  • Pitta-aggravating risk: Its ushna virya (hot potency) may aggravate Pitta in susceptible individuals — contraindicated in hyperacidity, peptic ulcer, and active bleeding disorders. Use cautiously in summer season.
  • Sourcing & adulteration: Due to CITES Appendix I protected status and high commercial value, adulteration with Inula racemosa (Pushkarmool) or other roots is common. Always source from certified Ayurvedic pharmacies with verified traceability.
  • Inhalation: Concentrated root EO inhalation should be brief and well-ventilated; prolonged inhalation of undiluted essential oil or heavy fumigation smoke in enclosed spaces may cause mucous membrane irritation.
