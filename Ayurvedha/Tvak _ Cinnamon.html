Tvak
Cinnamon — The Sacred Bark of Antiquity
Cinnamomum verum J.Presl  ·  Cinnamomum cassia (L.) J.Presl  ·  Lauraceae

Fig. 1 — Cinnamomum verum tree with its distinctive glossy, ovate leaves and aromatic bark  ·  Fig. 2 — Dried inner bark hand-rolled into characteristic quills — the true cinnamon of Ayurveda

❧

Among the oldest-known spices in human history, Tvak — "that which is the skin" — is the inner bark of the cinnamon tree, traded across the ancient world long before the Silk Road existed. Mentioned in the Charaka Samhita, the Sushruta Samhita, and the Ashtanga Hridayam, it is one of the principal Tridoshahara spices, simultaneously warming, digestive, cardiac, and carminative — the rare herb that improves nearly every system it touches.

01

Botanical Profile

Cinnamon belongs to the Lauraceae (laurel) family — the same family as bay leaf, avocado, and camphor — and comprises several commercially important species. The tree is an evergreen reaching 10–15 m in wild stands, with thick, leathery, glossy ovate leaves, small yellowish flowers, and a berry-like drupe fruit. The medicinal and culinary bark is harvested from young branches of 2–3 year coppiced shoots: the outer rough bark is scraped away and the thin inner bark is peeled and sun-dried, curling into the familiar quill as it dries.

In Ayurveda, Tvak refers primarily to Cinnamomum verum (true or Ceylon cinnamon, native to Sri Lanka), though C. cassia (Chinese cinnamon) and C. zeylanicum are used interchangeably in many traditions. The two species differ critically in coumarin content — a distinction with important safety implications detailed below.

Cinnamomum verum
True Ceylon Cinnamon · Preferred in Ayurveda
Native to Sri Lanka; also called "soft" or "true" cinnamon. Thin, multi-layered, papery quills with a complex, delicate, mildly sweet flavour profile. Coumarin content is very low (0.017 g/kg) — safe for daily therapeutic use. This is the Tvak of classical Ayurvedic texts.
Cinnamomum cassia
Cassia / Chinese Cinnamon · Common in Commerce
Native to China and widely cultivated across Southeast Asia. Thick, single-layer hard quills with a more intense, spicy-pungent flavour and higher cinnamaldehyde. Coumarin content is significantly higher (0.31–6.97 g/kg) — limits safe daily intake, especially for hepatically vulnerable individuals.

02

Phytochemistry

The essential oil of cinnamon bark (0.5–4% of dry weight) is dominated by trans-cinnamaldehyde — the compound responsible for cinnamon's characteristic aroma and most of its pharmacological activity. Beyond the volatile fraction, the bark yields a complex matrix of polyphenols, proanthocyanidins, flavonoids, diterpenes, and the uniquely named cinnamate esters. The leaves of C. verum are rich in eugenol (70–90% of leaf oil) rather than cinnamaldehyde, giving them a clove-like aroma and distinct antimicrobial profile.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Tvak is classified as one of the Triphala-adjacent spices in Ayurvedic practice and is listed among the Trikatu-complementary aromatics — occupying an exalted position in both Charaka Samhita (under Hridya dravyas — heart-beneficial substances) and Ashtanga Hridayam (in Sheetaprashamana — cold-pacifying medicines). It appears as a key ingredient in Trikatu-based formulations, Chyawanprash, Dashamoola preparations, and dozens of classical churnas, kwathas, and asavas.

Classical verse from Bhavaprakasha — "Tvak cha madhura tikta syad ushnaveerya laghugunam, tridoshaghni vishaghni cha krimighni hridyam param" — describes Tvak as sweet and bitter in taste, hot in potency, light in quality, destroyer of all three doshas, anti-toxic, anti-parasitic, and supremely beneficial to the heart.

04

Key Health Benefits

Cinnamon is among the most scientifically investigated spices for metabolic, cardiovascular, antimicrobial, and neurological health. Its Type-A procyanidins and methylhydroxychalcone polymers (MHCP) exhibit insulin-mimetic activity at the cellular level — making it pharmacologically unique among all culinary spices.

Blood Sugar Regulation
Cinnamon's most clinically validated benefit. MHCP activates insulin receptor tyrosine kinase and inhibits protein tyrosine phosphatase, essentially mimicking insulin signalling. Meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials show significant reductions in fasting blood glucose (–24 mg/dL), HbA1c, and insulin resistance in Type 2 diabetes patients at 1–6 g/day.
Cardiovascular & Lipid Health
Clinical trials demonstrate significant reduction in total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides with 1–3 g cinnamon daily. Cinnamaldehyde inhibits thromboxane A2 synthesis and platelet aggregation, reducing clotting risk. Hridya (cardiotonic) action in classical texts is supported by improved endothelial function and reduced blood pressure in hypertensive models.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Among the most potent antimicrobial spices known. Cinnamaldehyde disrupts bacterial cell membranes and inhibits biofilm formation in Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Salmonella, Helicobacter pylori, and Candida albicans. Cinnamon bark oil is one of very few natural agents with documented activity against drug-resistant Candida species.
Digestive Deepana & Pachana
Classically the foremost Deepana dravya (digestive fire-kindler) among spices. Cinnamaldehyde and camphor stimulate secretion of digestive enzymes and bile, accelerate gastric emptying, and relieve intestinal spasm. Used in flatulence, bloating, nausea, IBS-C (constipation-predominant), and loss of appetite — the classical aruchi.
Neuroprotective & Cognitive
Cinnamaldehyde inhibits tau aggregation and protects against amyloid-β toxicity — two hallmarks of Alzheimer's pathology. Animal studies show improved learning and spatial memory. Cinnamic acid and procyanidins also suppress neuroinflammation. A 2020 review identified cinnamon as a candidate neuroprotective food for cognitive decline prevention.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Cinnamaldehyde inhibits NF-κB and AP-1 transcription factors, suppressing prostaglandin synthesis. Eugenol (abundant in leaf oil) is a potent COX-2 inhibitor comparable to ibuprofen at the molecular level. Tvak paste is classically applied to headache (shirahshula) and arthritic pain.
Respiratory & Warming
Its ushna (hot) virya and katu-tikta rasa make it a principal Kapha-clearing herb for the respiratory tract. Used in cough, bronchitis, sinusitis, and cold. The volatile cinnamaldehyde acts as a mucolytic and bronchodilator. Classical decoctions with honey are used for chronic cough, congestion, and cold-induced asthma.
Women's Health & Dysmenorrhea
A 2015 randomised controlled trial in Iran demonstrated that 420 mg cinnamon powder three times daily significantly reduced primary dysmenorrhea (menstrual pain) and menstrual bleeding compared to placebo. Classical Ayurveda uses Tvak in Artavajanana (menstrual-stimulating) formulations and for PCOS-related cycle irregularities.
