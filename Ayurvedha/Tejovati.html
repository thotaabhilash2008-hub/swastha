Tejovati
Toothache Plant — The Fiery Tingling Shrub
Zanthoxylum armatum DC.  ·  Rutaceae  ·  syn. Z. alatum Roxb.

Fig. 1 — Zanthoxylum armatum fruiting branch showing characteristic paired thorns, compound leaves, and red-brown berry clusters  ·  Fig. 2 — Dried pericarps split open revealing the shiny black seeds that bear the bulk of the essential oil

❧

Named in Sanskrit as Tejovati — "that which possesses brilliance and fierce energy" — this thorny Himalayan shrub is among Ayurveda's most electrifying medicines. Its pericarp produces an immediate, searing numbing sensation unlike any other plant — caused not by capsaicin or menthol, but by hydroxy-α-sanshool, a unique alkylamide that simultaneously activates and then paralyses nerve endings. Revered since the time of Charaka and Sushruta as a premier Deepana, Pachana, and toothache remedy, Tejovati occupies a singular position at the crossing of neuropharmacology, Ayurvedic gastroenterology, and Himalayan folk medicine.

01

Botanical Profile

Zanthoxylum armatum DC. (syn. Z. alatum Roxb.) is an armed, aromatic, deciduous to semi-evergreen shrub or small tree of the family Rutaceae, native to the Himalayan foothills and mountain forests from Afghanistan and Pakistan east through Nepal, Bhutan, and northeast India, extending into China, Japan, and Southeast Asia. It grows typically at altitudes of 1,000–2,700 m, often on rocky slopes, forest margins, and river terraces, reaching 2–6 metres in height.

The plant is immediately identifiable by its winged rachis — the leaf axis is flanked by two narrow leaf-like wings — and its prominent, paired, recurved prickles (thorns) on stems and branches. The leaves are pinnately compound with 3–9 leaflets that are elliptic-lanceolate, pellucid-dotted with oil glands visible against light, and strongly aromatic when crushed. Small yellowish-green flowers appear in March–May; the fruit ripens August–October as red-brown follicles (falsely called berries) that split open to reveal one or two shiny, hard, globose black seeds. The dried pericarp and seeds are the primary medicinal and culinary parts.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Tejovati's phytochemistry is dominated by two extraordinary compound classes: alkylamides (principally hydroxy-α-sanshool and related sanshools), responsible for the unique tingling–numbing sensation, and a rich essential oil containing monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and aromatic compounds. The sanshools act on TRPV1, TRPA1, and KCNK3 ion channels — a unique multi-receptor mechanism that produces simultaneous prickling, numbing, and mild anaesthesia. Beyond sanshools, the plant yields significant amounts of alkaloids (berberine, magnoflorine, liriodenine), lignans, coumarins, and flavonoids.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Tejovati / Tumburu is one of the most prominently mentioned spice-drugs of the classical corpus. Charaka includes it in multiple important Mahakashaya (groups of ten): Shoolaprashamana (pain-relieving), Deepaniya (digestive-stimulating), and Krimighna (antiparasitic) — a rare distinction that few drugs hold across three major categories simultaneously. Sushruta's Pippalyadi Gana and Mustadi Gana both include Tumburu. Vagbhata, Sharangdhara, and Bhavaprakasha all confirm these actions and add its use in Vatarakta (gout) and Arsha (haemorrhoids) formulations.

A famous classical combination — the Tumburu-Panchaka or the broader Shunthi-Pippali-Maricha-Tejovati-Chavya group — forms the backbone of numerous classical digestive and analgesic prescriptions. Bhavaprakasha's shloka describes it: "Tumburuḥ kaṭuko rūkṣas tīkṣṇoṣṇaḥ kaṭuvipākaḥ, vātashleṣmāpahā hṛdyo dīpanaḥ pācano laghūḥ, shūlaghno krimighnaśca..." — pungent, dry, sharp, hot, with pungent post-digestive effect; pacifying Vata and Kapha; heart-tonic; kindles digestive fire; light; relieves colic; destroys worms.

04

Key Health Benefits

Tejovati's pharmacological profile is unusually broad — bridging neuroscience (ion channel pharmacology), gastroenterology, antimicrobial medicine, and analgesic therapy in both classical and contemporary research.

Toothache & Local Anaesthesia
The most celebrated classical use — a single pericarp or seed applied directly to a painful tooth produces immediate, searing relief through sanshool-mediated TRPV1 activation followed by nerve desensitisation. This local anaesthetic action is confirmed by modern electrophysiology. The seed paste is also applied to trigeminal neuralgia points, headache sites, and arthritic joints for topical analgesia.
Digestive Stimulant & Carminative
As one of Charaka's premier Deepaniya drugs, Tejovati powerfully stimulates digestive enzymes, bile secretion, and gastric motility. Used in chronic indigestion (mandagni), flatulence, abdominal colic (shoola), and dyspepsia. The carminative action expels accumulated gas while the pungent alkylamides stimulate pancreatic enzyme release. A cornerstone of Himalayan digestive spice blends.
Antinociceptive & Anti-arthritic
Sanshools act on multiple pain-sensing ion channels — producing analgesia comparable to capsaicin but with a different quality: more tingling and numbing, less burning. In vivo studies confirm significant dose-dependent antinociceptive activity of bark and fruit extracts in acetic-acid writhing and hot plate models. Used in rheumatoid arthritis, sciatica, and myalgia in classical Ayurveda.
Antimicrobial & Antiparasitic
Tejovati is one of Charaka's three Krimighna (antiparasitic) spice-drugs. Essential oil and alkaloid-rich extracts show broad-spectrum activity against E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella typhi, Candida albicans, and Aspergillus niger. Berberine (from bark) has documented efficacy against intestinal parasites and traveller's diarrhoea. Used in Ayurvedic formulations for worm infestations and GI infections.
Respiratory & Expectorant
The pungent volatile oils stimulate bronchial secretion, thin mucus, and reflexively open airways. Used in classical formulations for cough, bronchitis, asthma, and chronic respiratory Kapha conditions. The warming, tikshna quality dispels cold-damp Kapha from the lungs and upper respiratory tract. Often combined with Pippali (long pepper) and Shunthi (dry ginger) for enhanced respiratory effect.
Antioxidant & Hepatoprotective
Flavonoids (quercetin, rutin), lignans (sesamin), and the essential oil deliver significant antioxidant capacity confirmed by DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays. Berberine provides documented hepatoprotective activity — reducing liver enzyme elevation in CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity models. The plant is used in traditional Himalayan medicine for liver complaints and as a general tonic.
Anti-diarrhoeal & GI Infections
Bark preparations containing berberine are used for acute and chronic diarrhoea, dysentery, and cholera in Himalayan folk medicine — an application robustly supported by berberine's clinical evidence base for infectious diarrhoea. Tannins contribute astringency to firm intestinal secretions while the antimicrobial constituents address the pathogenic cause simultaneously.
Cardiotonic & Circulatory
Bhavaprakasha's attribute hridya (heart-tonic) reflects Tejovati's classical use in cardiac debility and poor circulation. Linalool and berberine both show mild vasodilatory and antiarrhythmic properties in vitro. The circulatory-stimulating effect of pungent sanshools may underlie improved peripheral blood flow — applied topically in cold extremities and used in some classical formulations for cardiac Vata conditions.
05

Classical Formulations & Culinary Use

Tejovati appears in classical gastric, analgesic, antiparasitic, and respiratory formulations and continues in active use in both Ayurvedic pharmacy and Himalayan / Northeastern Indian cuisine. It is related to — and sometimes used interchangeably with — Tirphal (Z. rhetsa, South India) and shares culinary overlap with Sichuan Pepper (Z. bungeanum, which it closely resembles in taste and chemistry).

How to Use Tejovati

The dried pericarp (with seeds removed or included) is the primary form used both medicinally and culinarily. The whole pericarp delivers the unique sanshool tingling effect; lightly roasting before use intensifies aroma while mellowing the raw intensity. For internal medicinal use, classical doses are modest — the plant is potent and strongly hot in nature.

Safety & Precautions

Tejovati is a potent herb — its strongly ushna, tikshna, and ruksha qualities demand judicious use. Culinary amounts in food preparations are generally safe for healthy adults. Medicinal doses require supervision, particularly given the strong Pitta-aggravating and uterine-stimulating potential at higher doses. The tingling-numbing sensation is normal and expected — it indicates active sanshool content and is not a sign of harm.

  • Pregnancy: Contraindicated internally at medicinal doses. Tejovati has classical properties as a uterine stimulant — excessive internal use may trigger contractions. Culinary use in small food amounts is generally considered acceptable; medicinal preparations should be avoided throughout pregnancy.
  • Pitta-dominant constitutions: The strongly ushna (hot) and tikshna (sharp) profile makes Tejovati unsuitable at medicinal doses for Pitta-dominant individuals without corrective co-administration of Pitta-cooling drugs (ghee, coriander, fennel). Avoid in active hyperacidity, peptic ulcers, gastritis, and bleeding disorders.
  • Furanocoumarins — photosensitivity: Xanthyletin and xanthotoxin in the plant are furanocoumarins with photosensitising potential. Topical application followed by UV exposure may cause phototoxic dermatitis in sensitive individuals — particularly relevant for topical essential oil use. Avoid sun exposure after applying to skin.
  • Kidney disease: The strongly tikshna and ushna qualities may irritate renal tubules — use with caution in chronic kidney disease; avoid high medicinal doses without professional guidance.
  • Children: Do not use topical seed/pericarp application in children under 5 due to risk of oral mucosal irritation. Medicinal doses not recommended in young children without specialist supervision.
  • Drug interactions: Berberine (from bark) may interact with CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 substrates — potential interactions with cyclosporine, metformin, anticoagulants, and certain antibiotics. Sanshool's TRPV1 modulation may theoretically potentiate capsaicin-based topical analgesics.
  • Species confusion: Z. armatum should not be confused with other Zanthoxylum species of differing alkaloid profiles, or with unrelated thorny plants. Always verify botanical source from reputable Ayurvedic pharmacies.
  • Prolonged high-dose use: Extended high-dose internal use of the bark (berberine-rich) may cause GI irritation. Classical Ayurvedic use specifies short therapeutic courses, not long-term daily supplementation at medicinal doses.
