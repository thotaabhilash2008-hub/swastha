Changeri
Creeping Wood Sorrel — The Sour Clover of the Courtyard
Oxalis corniculata L.  ·  Oxalidaceae

Fig. 1 — Oxalis corniculata creeping along a garden path; its trifoliate clover-like leaves fold downward at night in a behaviour called nyctinasty  ·  Fig. 2 — The characteristic heart-shaped leaflets and delicate five-petalled yellow flowers of Changeri, no larger than a fingernail

❧

It grows between the cracks of every courtyard, beneath every garden bench, along every shaded wall in tropical India — so common and so small it is almost universally overlooked. Yet Changeri has been described in every classical Ayurvedic text from Charaka onward, classified in five Nighantus, and prescribed for conditions ranging from diarrhoea and worms to scurvy and scorpion sting. This is the herb that hides in plain sight — one of Ayurveda's most accessible medicines, growing freely wherever it is needed.

01

Botanical Profile

Oxalis corniculata L. is a small, prostrate or semi-erect annual or perennial herb of the Oxalidaceae family, growing 5–30 cm. It spreads by creeping stems that root at the nodes, forming low mats of trifoliate leaves — each leaflet heart-shaped (obcordate), 5–15 mm wide, sometimes flushed purple-bronze especially in sunny conditions. The plant produces small, bright yellow five-petalled flowers throughout the year in warm climates, and slender cylindrical seed capsules 1–2.5 cm long that eject seeds explosively when ripe — sometimes dispersing them up to a metre from the parent plant. The leaves fold downward at night and in intense heat, a movement known as nyctinasty, giving the plant a liveliness unique among ground-cover herbs.

Though considered a cosmopolitan weed of uncertain geographic origin — possibly tropical America or southern Europe, now naturalised across every temperate and tropical region on earth — Oxalis corniculata has been documented in Indian literature for over two thousand years, suggesting long naturalization or independent origin. It thrives in gardens, lawns, roadsides, rice-paddy bunds, and shaded waste ground from the plains to 3,000 m in the Himalayan foothills. The closely related Oxalis stricta and the ornamental Oxalis acetosella (wood sorrel of European forests) share the sour oxalic acid taste but are less common in Indian traditional medicine.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Changeri's phytochemical identity is dominated by oxalic acid and its salts — potassium oxalate and calcium oxalate — which account for its intense sour taste, its anti-scorbutic and refrigerant properties, and its principal safety concern in high-dose use. The plant is also rich in ascorbic acid (Vitamin C), flavonoids, phenolic acids, and a characteristic profile of isoflavones and phytosterols that contribute to its anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and wound-healing actions.

Significantly, Changeri contains a diverse range of anthraquinones, tartaric acid, and malic acid alongside oxalic acid — giving it a complex polyphenol matrix beyond the simple "sour weed" classification. The leaves are among the richest wild sources of Vitamin C per gram in Indian flora, explaining its classical use as an anti-scorbutic. The root contains tannins and terpenoids with hepatoprotective activity distinct from the aerial parts.

Oxalic Acid — The Therapeutic and Safety Duality
Oxalic acid and potassium oxalate give Changeri its sour taste and its refrigerant, diuretic, and anti-inflammatory properties — but also its principal safety constraint. At culinary quantities (small amounts as potherb or juice), oxalic acid is beneficial: it chelates excess calcium, provides organic acid buffering, and contributes to the antidiarrhoeal astringency. At high or sustained doses, oxalic acid forms insoluble calcium oxalate crystals that can precipitate in renal tubules, potentially contributing to calcium oxalate kidney stones in susceptible individuals. Classical Ayurvedic texts acknowledge this dual nature, using Changeri as a short-course acute remedy rather than a long-term tonic. All internal therapeutic preparations should be of moderate quantity and short duration.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Changeri is one of the relatively few Ayurvedic herbs classified as primarily Amla (sour) in rasa — a distinction it shares with Amalaki (Emblica officinalis) and Chukra (Rumex species), and which places it in a small, therapeutically specialised group. Charaka Samhita classifies Changeri in the Phanita Varga and prescribes it for arsha (haemorrhoids), atisara (diarrhoea), and krimi (intestinal worms). Sushruta Samhita lists it in the Parushakadi Gana — a group of sour, refrigerant drugs used for thirst, fever, and burning sensations. Ashtanga Hridaya includes it among digestive and bowel-regulating preparations.

"Changeri amla kashaya laghu ushna deepani pachani, atisara-arsha-krimi-trishna-jwara-vatakaphanashani" — Bhavaprakasha Nighantu, Guduchyadi Varga — Classical properties of Changeri

The shloka identifies Changeri as sour-astringent, light, hot in potency, stimulating digestion and enhancing digestive fire — and specifically as a destroyer of diarrhoea, haemorrhoids, intestinal worms, thirst, fever, and Vata-Kapha disorders. Dhanvantari Nighantu's Shatapushpadi Varga and Raja Nighantu both describe it as dipana-pachana (digestive stimulant) and shulahara (colic-relieving). In Siddha medicine (Pulichchi Keerai), the leaf juice is a standard prescription for children's digestive complaints, worm infestations, and scurvy — a use so consistently documented that it reflects a pre-modern practical understanding of the plant's high Vitamin C content.

04

Key Health Benefits

The Accessible Anti-Scorbutic — Classical Vitamin C Before Vitamins Were Named
Long before vitamin deficiency diseases were understood biochemically, classical Ayurvedic and Siddha physicians had identified Changeri as a remedy for the cluster of symptoms we now recognise as scurvy — gum bleeding (dantamula shotha), skin haemorrhages (rakta vikara), weakness, and slow wound healing. The prescription of fresh Changeri leaf juice for these conditions — documented in Siddha Gunapadam and multiple folk traditions — reflects empirical recognition of its high Vitamin C content, which modern analysis has confirmed is among the highest of any common wild green in tropical India. In remote Himalayan and tribal communities where access to citrus was seasonal or absent, Changeri growing on doorsteps provided year-round anti-scorbutic protection. It stands as one of the most elegant examples of practical nutritional medicine embedded within ethnobotanical tradition.
Digestive Stimulant & Appetite
Amla rasa (sourness) is a powerful stimulant of gastric acid secretion, salivation, and digestive enzyme release — making Changeri one of Ayurveda's most potent deepana herbs. Classical use for loss of appetite (aruchi), sluggish digestion (mandagni), flatulence, and dyspepsia. The organic acid matrix (oxalic, tartaric, malic) creates an acidic gastric environment conducive to protein digestion and mineral absorption.
Antidiarrhoeal & Intestinal
Tannin-mediated astringency contracts intestinal mucosa and reduces fluid secretion; antimicrobial activity of quercetin and phenolic acids reduces pathogenic load. Classical specific for atisara (diarrhoea) — particularly Kapha-type watery diarrhoea and Vata-type crampy diarrhoea. Fresh plant juice classically given in children's loose motions across South Indian Siddha tradition as Pulichchi Keerai juice.
Antiparasitic & Worm Expulsion
Classical designation as Krimighna (parasite destroyer) encompasses intestinal worms — roundworm, threadworm, and hookworm — for which fresh plant juice is prescribed in classical texts and in tribal practice across Jharkhand, Odisha, and Tamil Nadu. The organic acids create an unfavourable intestinal environment for helminthic survival; flavonoids specifically inhibit worm ATPase enzyme activity.
Scurvy & Vitamin C Deficiency
Among the highest Vitamin C concentrations of any common wild Indian herb. Prescribed in Siddha and folk medicine for bleeding gums, skin purpura, slow wound healing, and constitutional weakness arising from Vitamin C deficiency. In communities without year-round citrus access, Changeri growing wild on doorsteps provides a freely available, potent anti-scorbutic available in every season.
Antipyretic & Refrigerant
Classical designation in the Parushakadi Gana (Sushruta) — a group of refrigerant, thirst-relieving drugs — reflects the cooling sensation of Changeri juice in fever despite its Ushna virya. Organic acids produce a biochemically cooling, hydrating effect at the systemic level distinct from the local hot taste. Used for trishna (burning thirst) and fever in children in folk medicine across the subcontinent.
Antimicrobial & Wound Healing
Extracts show significant activity against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Salmonella typhi. Ursolic and oleanolic acids have broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activity. Fresh leaf paste is applied in tribal medicine to infected wounds, boils, and dermatophyte infections. Vitamin C-mediated collagen synthesis accelerates wound healing and tissue repair.
Haemorrhoids & Rectal Conditions
A classical Charaka indication — arsha nashana (haemorrhoid-destroyer). The astringent-sour combination reduces venous congestion, controls bleeding from haemorrhoidal tissue, and soothes mucosal inflammation. Used as fresh juice internally and as warm leaf poultice externally. Classical formulations combine Changeri with Haritaki and buttermilk for post-partum haemorrhoidal complaints.
Liver & Hepatoprotection
Vitexin, isovitexin, and β-sitosterol from the whole plant protect hepatocytes from CCl₄-induced damage in animal studies. Root extracts show significant reduction in liver enzyme elevation (SGPT, SGOT) and bilirubin in experimentally induced hepatitis models. Traditional use of fresh Changeri juice for jaundice in children — a practice documented independently in Tamil Siddha, tribal Odisha, and rural Maharashtra folk traditions.

Traditional & Ethnobotanical Uses

Changeri's ubiquity as a garden weed across tropical India has made it a first-resort folk remedy in rural and tribal communities — freely available, instantly recognisable, and requiring no preparation beyond squeezing fresh juice. The range of conditions for which it has been independently employed across different regional traditions reflects its genuine pharmacological versatility.

How to Use Changeri

Changeri is primarily used fresh — the whole plant, leaves, or expressed juice. It is also used as a cooked vegetable in small quantities. Given the oxalic acid content, all internal preparations should be moderate in quantity and short in duration. Cooking significantly reduces oxalic acid content (up to 50% reduction by blanching and discarding water), making it safer for regular dietary consumption as a potherb. Fresh juice is more potent medicinally but carries a higher oxalic acid load than cooked preparations.

Safety & Precautions

Changeri is safe at moderate culinary quantities — it has been consumed as a potherb and flavouring across tropical Asia for millennia without reported adverse effects at food-level doses. Therapeutic juice preparations carry a meaningful oxalic acid load and require careful dosing and duration management, particularly in individuals with kidney disease or a history of renal stones.

  • Kidney stones (renal calculi): The most important safety consideration. Oxalic acid combines with calcium in the urine to form calcium oxalate — the most common type of kidney stone. Individuals with a history of calcium oxalate kidney stones, hyperoxaluria, or chronic kidney disease should avoid therapeutic-dose fresh juice preparations and limit dietary consumption. Even culinary use should be discussed with a nephrologist in stone-formers.
  • Chronic kidney disease (CKD): In CKD patients with reduced renal clearance, even normal dietary oxalate loads can accumulate. Changeri juice is contraindicated as a therapeutic preparation in moderate-to-severe CKD.
  • Pitta aggravation: Amla (sour) rasa and Ushna virya will aggravate Pitta with excess or prolonged use — leading to gastric acidity, heartburn, skin redness, or irritability. Limit therapeutic juice to short courses (5–7 days); always dilute fresh juice with water or buttermilk; avoid on empty stomach in Pitta-dominant individuals.
  • Pregnancy: Moderate culinary use as a cooked vegetable is safe. High-dose fresh juice preparations are traditionally classified as emmenagogues in some folk texts and should be avoided in the first trimester. Discuss with Ayurvedic physician before therapeutic use during pregnancy.
  • Dental enamel: Prolonged or frequent contact of oxalic acid with teeth can demineralise enamel. Rinse mouth after consuming fresh juice or sour plant preparations; do not hold juice in the mouth for extended periods.
  • Rheumatic / arthritic conditions: Oxalic acid can precipitate in synovial tissue in susceptible individuals, potentially worsening gouty or rheumatoid conditions. Patients with gout, pseudogout, or hyperuricaemia should use Changeri sparingly and prefer cooked over raw preparations.
  • Children: Fresh juice at 5–10 ml is widely used in children in Siddha and folk practice for worm infestation and digestive complaints — considered safe at these doses. Avoid large quantities of raw plant material in children under 5; cooked preparations are preferred for routine dietary use in young children.
  • Drug interaction (calcium supplements): Concurrent high-dose Changeri juice with calcium supplementation increases the oxalate-calcium crystal formation risk. Separate consumption by at least 2–3 hours; prefer cooked preparations if using calcium supplements regularly.
