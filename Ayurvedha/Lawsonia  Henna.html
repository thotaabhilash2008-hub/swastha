Lawsonia
Henna — The Blesséd Dye Plant of the Orient
Lawsonia inermis L.  ·  Lythraceae

Fig. 1 — Tiny, fragrant white to pale-rose flowers of Lawsonia inermis in terminal panicles  ·  Fig. 2 — Freshly prepared leaf paste yielding its characteristic red-orange dye on skin

❧

Known in Sanskrit as Madayantika and in Arabic as Al-Hinna, Lawsonia inermis is perhaps the world's oldest recorded cosmetic plant — used for over 9,000 years across Egypt, Persia, the Indian subcontinent, and North Africa for body art, hair colouring, and medicinal therapy. Yet behind its celebrated crimson stain lies a sophisticated pharmacopoeia: a plant revered in Unani, Ayurveda, and folk traditions as an anti-inflammatory, antifungal, analgesic, and wound-healing agent of considerable power.

01

Botanical Profile

Lawsonia inermis is a glabrous, much-branched shrub or small tree of the Lythraceae family, typically reaching 1.8–7.5 m in height, with slender, quadrangular branches that may bear terminal spines in older specimens — though the species epithet inermis means "without thorns," referring to the cultivated forms. Native to the semi-arid zones of North Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, it thrives in hot, dry climates with well-drained soils.

The leaves are opposite, subsessile, elliptic to broadly lanceolate (2–3 cm long), glaucous-green, and contain the primary active pigment lawsone (2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone) within vacuoles that rupture upon grinding — releasing the dye. Flowers are borne in large terminal panicles: small (5 mm), four-petalled, white to rose-pink, and remarkably fragrant, used in South Asian perfumery. The fruit is a dry capsule containing numerous angular seeds. Flowering peaks between April and September.

Phytochemistry & Active Constituents

The pharmacological richness of Lawsonia is centred on lawsone (2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone), the signature red-orange naphthoquinone pigment constituting 0.5–1.5% of dry leaf weight. Lawsone is not merely a dye — it is a potent bioactive with antifungal, antimicrobial, antitumour, and UV-blocking properties. The plant further yields a complex array of flavonoids, coumarins, sterols, tannins, alkaloids, and terpenoids across its various parts.

03

Ayurvedic & Unani Significance

In Ayurveda, Lawsonia (Mendika / Madayantika) is described in the Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, and Ashtanga Hridayam. The leaves are classified as Kashaya (astringent), Tikta (bitter), with Sheeta virya (cooling potency) — making it specifically valuable for pacifying aggravated Pitta and conditions of excess heat. It is used in Jwara (fever), Vrana (wounds), Daha (burning sensations), and Shiro Roga (disorders of the head).

In Unani medicine (Ilaj-bil-tadbeer), henna holds an even more prominent place — listed in Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine as a wound-healer, liver tonic, and remedy for alopecia and skin diseases. The Unani system additionally values henna flowers, distilled into an aromatic water (Arq-e-Hinna) used as a nervine tonic and for headaches. The root bark (Ushr-e-Hinna) is used in jaundice, skin eruptions, and urinary disorders.

04

Key Health Benefits

Lawsonia inermis is one of the most pharmacologically validated medicinal plants of the ethnobotanical world. Hundreds of peer-reviewed studies across multiple decades have substantiated its antifungal, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, hepatoprotective, and wound-healing activities — lending scientific credence to millennia of traditional use.

Antifungal & Antimicrobial
Lawsone and hennotannic acid demonstrate potent activity against dermatophytes including Trichophyton, Microsporum, and Candida species. Leaf extract inhibits E. coli, S. aureus, Salmonella, and Klebsiella. Topical application is well-validated for tinea, ringworm, and skin fungal infections.
Wound Healing & Antiseptic
Classical Vrana-ropana (wound healing) action is backed by controlled animal studies showing accelerated wound closure, collagen deposition, and epithelialisation with henna paste application. Hennotannic acid precipitates surface proteins, forming a protective film over raw tissue.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Luteolin, gallic acid, and triterpenoids from leaf extract significantly inhibit prostaglandin synthesis and COX pathways. Application to inflamed joints, burns, and headache (on soles or forehead) is a classical Unani practice validated by animal pain models showing comparable efficacy to standard analgesics.
Hair & Scalp Care
Lawsone binds keratinous protein in hair shafts via Michael addition, imparting colour while simultaneously strengthening the cortex. Henna conditions the scalp, reduces dandruff through antifungal action against Malassezia, and is classically used for premature greying, hair loss, and heat-related scalp inflammation.
Antipyretic & Cooling
Sheeta virya (cold potency) underpins the traditional practice of applying henna paste to palms and soles during high fever to draw out heat — a practice validated by studies showing reduced rectal temperature in animal models. Aqueous leaf extract also demonstrates direct antipyretic activity via hypothalamic pathways.
Hepatoprotective
Laxanthone I & II, ellagic acid, and gallic acid exert significant protection against hepatotoxin-induced liver damage in animal studies. Ethanolic extract reduces elevated liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP) and malondialdehyde in paracetamol- and CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity models.
Antimalarial
Isoplumbagin and lawsone have demonstrated in vitro activity against Plasmodium falciparum. The mechanism involves disruption of the parasite's electron transport chain. Traditional use of henna in malaria-endemic regions of Africa and South Asia as a fever remedy may partly relate to this activity.
Anticancer Potential
Betulinic acid (root bark), lawsone, and ellagic acid show cytotoxic activity against multiple cancer cell lines including HeLa, MCF-7, and HepG2 in vitro. Mechanism involves apoptosis induction and cell-cycle arrest. Research is promising but human clinical trials remain limited.

How to Use Lawsonia / Henna

Henna is primarily used externally; internal use in Ayurveda and Unani is restricted to decoctions and formulations of root bark or leaf — always in supervised, time-limited courses. The iconic mehndi paste combines dried leaf powder with acidic liquids (lemon juice, tea, tamarind) and essential oils to release and fix lawsone onto skin. Internally, the decoction of root bark is used for liver support and urinary complaints.

Safety, Cautions & Black Henna Warning

Natural henna (pure Lawsonia paste) has an excellent safety profile for topical use in most adults. However, several significant safety concerns must be understood — especially regarding adulterated commercial products and internal consumption.

  • ⚠ Black Henna (PPD) — Critical Warning: Commercial "black henna" and many salon products contain para-phenylenediamine (PPD), a potent contact allergen with no relation to Lawsonia. PPD can cause severe allergic contact dermatitis, blistering, scarring, and systemic sensitisation. Always verify natural, PPD-free henna before use.
  • G6PD Deficiency: Lawsone can induce haemolytic anaemia in individuals with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency — a serious and potentially life-threatening complication. Henna should not be applied to neonates or those with known G6PD deficiency.
  • Internal Use — Caution: Internal preparations of root bark should be used only under qualified supervision and for limited durations. High internal doses of lawsone and related naphthoquinones can be nephrotoxic and hepatotoxic.
  • Pregnancy: While topical use is generally considered safe, internal preparations (decoctions) should be avoided in pregnancy. Henna has historically been used as an emmenagogue (to stimulate menstruation) at medicinal doses.
  • Skin Sensitivity Test: Before full application, always perform a 24-hour patch test on the inner forearm — even with natural henna — to rule out individual sensitivity or cross-reactions.
  • Open Wounds: Avoid applying henna paste to open wounds, abraded skin, or infected areas without qualified guidance — the dye penetrates disrupted tissue and may cause staining of underlying structures.
  • Eye Contact: Keep all henna preparations away from eyes. Corneal staining from accidental contact has been reported in rare cases.
  • Drug Interactions: Coumarins in henna may theoretically potentiate anticoagulant drugs (warfarin). Patients on blood thinners should exercise caution with internal henna preparations.
