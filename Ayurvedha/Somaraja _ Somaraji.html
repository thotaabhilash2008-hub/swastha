Somaraja
The King of Soma — Sacred Skin Healer
Psoralea corylifolia  ·  Cullen corylifolium

Fig. 1 — Dark lens-shaped seeds of Somaraja (Bakuchi), primary medicinal part  ·  Fig. 2 — Somaraja in bloom, with characteristic small violet flowers on erect spikes

❧

Known across Sanskrit texts as Somaraja — "King of Soma" — and Bakuchi in vernacular tradition, this small erect herb bears seeds of remarkable potency. Revered since the Atharva Veda as a sovereign remedy for Shvitra (leucoderma / vitiligo), it remains one of Ayurveda's most clinically validated herbs — its psoralen compounds now forming the pharmacological basis of modern PUVA phototherapy for skin disorders worldwide.

01

Botanical Profile

Somaraja (Psoralea corylifolia, syn. Cullen corylifolium) is an erect, branching annual herb of the Fabaceae (Leguminosae) family, native to the Indian subcontinent, southern China, and tropical Asia. The plant grows to 60–120 cm tall. Its leaves are simple, broadly ovate, gland-dotted, with toothed or entire margins. Flowers are small, violet-blue, borne in compact axillary racemes; pods are small, indehiscent, one-seeded, and blackish at maturity.

The seeds — Bakuchi beeja — are the primary medicinal part: small, kidney-shaped, resinous, almost black, with a characteristic aromatic-bitter odour arising from their rich furocoumarin content. The plant thrives in dry, well-drained soils, wastelands, and rocky slopes across peninsular India, particularly in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and the Deccan plateau, flowering from August to October.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

The seeds of Somaraja contain one of the most pharmacologically rich profiles among Ayurvedic herbs. Furocoumarins — primarily psoralen and isopsoralen (bakuchicin) — are the signature compounds, responsible for photosensitising the skin and stimulating melanocyte proliferation. These are the same compounds now purified and used in PUVA phototherapy globally.

Beyond furocoumarins, the seeds yield a fixed oil (approx. 20%), flavonoids (bavachin, isobavachalcone, bakuchiol), meroterpenes (bakuchiol — now widely used in cosmetics as a retinol alternative), monoterpenes, and a range of phenolic compounds with antioxidant, antibacterial, and oestrogenic activity.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Somaraja holds an exceptional position in Ayurveda — one of very few herbs explicitly named for a specific disease (Shvitra — vitiligo/leucoderma) across multiple classical texts. The Atharva Veda contains hymns to Somaraja as a purifier of skin diseases. Charaka classifies it under Kushtaghna (skin disease destroyers) and Krimighna (anti-parasitic) groups. Sushruta places it in Priyangvadi Gana. Vagbhata emphasises it for Shvitra Chikitsa.

Bhavaprakasha Nighantu places Bakuchi in Haritakyadi Varga; Dhanvantari Nighantu in Karaveeradi Varga; Raja Nighantu in Prabhadradi Varga. Classical shloka: "Bakuchi katu tikta cha shvitraghnee krishnaphalaka, deepanee pachani hridya krimivatakaphapraNut" — describing it as pungent-bitter, a destroyer of vitiligo, digestive stimulant, heart tonic, and eliminator of worms, Vata, and Kapha.

04

Key Health Benefits

Somaraja is remarkable for the depth and breadth of modern clinical and pharmacological validation it has received — bridging classical Ayurvedic applications with contemporary dermatology, oncology, and bone medicine. Its seed compounds are among the most studied in the Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia.

Vitiligo & Leucoderma (Shvitra)
Somaraja's signature indication. Psoralen and isopsoralen are photosensitisers that, when combined with UVA light (PUVA therapy), stimulate dormant melanocytes to proliferate and produce melanin — restoring pigmentation. Bakuchi oil applied topically followed by sun exposure is the classical protocol still used widely in integrative dermatology.
Psoriasis & Skin Disorders (Kushtha)
Classically foremost in all Kushtha (skin disease) formulations, Somaraja's furocoumarins reduce keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory cytokines in psoriatic plaques. Modern PUVA treatment for psoriasis is a direct pharmacological derivative of Ayurvedic Bakuchi therapy codified millennia ago.
Anti-ageing & Skin Renewal
Bakuchiol — isolated from Somaraja seeds — has emerged as a leading plant-based retinol alternative. Multiple randomised clinical trials confirm it matches retinol in reducing fine lines, photoageing, and hyperpigmentation while producing significantly fewer side effects. It is now a flagship ingredient in premium clean-beauty skincare globally.
Bone Health & Anti-osteoporotic
Flavonoids bavachin, isobavachin, and neobavaisoflavone demonstrate potent oestrogenic receptor activity, stimulating osteoblast differentiation and inhibiting osteoclast activity in animal and cell studies. Somaraja is a classical ingredient in bone-strengthening formulations and postmenopausal skeletal support.
Antimicrobial & Anti-parasitic (Krimighna)
Isobavachalcone and psoralidin show significant antifungal activity against dermatophytes (Trichophyton), antibacterial action against drug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and antiparasitic effects against intestinal worms — validating classical use in ringworm, scabies, and intestinal helminthiasis.
Digestive Stimulant (Deepana-Pachana)
Classically used to kindle digestive fire (Agni deepana) and promote digestion. The volatile oils and bitters stimulate bile secretion and intestinal motility. Used in loss of appetite, malabsorption, and intestinal dysbiosis — often combined with Haritaki and Pippali in digestive formulations.
Kidney Tonic & Diuretic
Classical texts describe Somaraja as a strengthener of kidney function (Vrishya) and mild diuretic. Bakuchicin supports renal cell protection and the seeds are traditionally used in treating kidney stones, dysuria, and renal insufficiency — often as a decoction combined with Gokshura.
Antitumour & Neuroprotective Potential
Psoralidin and neobavaisoflavone demonstrate apoptotic activity against multiple cancer cell lines (breast, colon, lung) in preclinical studies. Isoflavone fractions show neuroprotective effects in Alzheimer's models by inhibiting amyloid aggregation and acetylcholinesterase — emergent research areas with growing scientific interest.

How to Use Somaraja

Somaraja seeds are potent and require careful dosing. Both internal and external applications are classical. Topical use for vitiligo (Bakuchi oil + controlled sun exposure) is the most documented application and relatively safe under guidance. Internal use must be strictly physician-supervised due to phototoxic and hepatotoxic potential at excess doses.

Safety & Precautions

Somaraja is a potent photosensitising herb. While externally applied Bakuchi oil is relatively safe under controlled conditions, internal use requires strict medical supervision. The furocoumarins can cause severe phototoxic burns if excessive sun exposure occurs after consumption, and high doses have been associated with liver stress.

  • Phototoxicity: Psoralen and isopsoralen make the skin highly sensitive to UV radiation. Uncontrolled sun or UV exposure after internal consumption or topical application can cause severe burns, blistering, and paradoxical hyperpigmentation — exposure must be carefully timed and limited.
  • Pregnancy: Contraindicated internally. Oestrogenic flavonoids and photosensitising compounds make internal use during pregnancy inadvisable; topical use over large skin areas should also be avoided.
  • Hepatotoxicity: High or prolonged internal doses may cause liver stress. Periodic liver function monitoring is advised for patients on sustained internal Bakuchi therapy. Not recommended in pre-existing liver disease.
  • Eye protection: During PUVA-type sun exposure therapy, protective eyewear is essential — furocoumarins can sensitise the ocular lens to UV and increase cataract risk with repeated unprotected exposure.
  • Children: Internal use in children under 12 should only be under qualified Ayurvedic physician supervision. Topical oil use should be patch-tested first.
  • Excess skin burning: If topical Bakuchi oil application causes burning, redness, or blistering, wash immediately with cold water and reduce sun exposure duration. Start with 5–10 minutes and gradually increase.
  • Drug interactions: Avoid combining with other photosensitising drugs (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, thiazide diuretics, St. John's Wort) as additive phototoxicity may result.
  • Pitta excess states: In strongly Pitta-dominant constitutions with active skin inflammation, bleeding disorders, or ulcerative conditions, use cautiously — its ushna (hot) potency may aggravate active Pitta flares.
  • Allergic contact sensitivity: Patch-test before broad topical application. Some individuals develop contact dermatitis to furocoumarin-containing preparations, particularly on facial skin.
