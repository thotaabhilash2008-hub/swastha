Bhanga
Cannabis — The Sacred Herb of Shiva
Cannabis sativa Linn.  ·  Cannabaceae

Fig. 1 — The iconic palmate compound leaves of Cannabis sativa with their finely serrated leaflets  ·  Fig. 2 — Female inflorescence bearing resin-laden trichomes, source of the plant's psychoactive and medicinal compounds

❧

Called Vijaya — "the victorious" — in classical Sanskrit texts, and worshipped as one of the five sacred plants of the Atharva Veda, Bhanga has been woven into the spiritual, medical, and cultural fabric of the Indian subcontinent for over five thousand years. Charaka, Sushruta, and Vagbhata all document its therapeutic applications; Shiva himself is described in the Puranas as drinking Bhanga — and yet the same tradition that venerates it insists with equal force on the supreme necessity of dosage, purification, and physician guidance.

01

Botanical Profile

Bhanga (Cannabis sativa Linn.) is a robust, aromatic annual herb of the Cannabaceae family, native to Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent but now naturalised across temperate and tropical regions worldwide. The plant is dioecious — bearing male and female flowers on separate plants — with the female plant being therapeutically primary due to its resin-rich inflorescences (flowers). It grows erect to 1–5 metres depending on variety and cultivation conditions, with a hollow-jointed stem and deeply lobed, palmate compound leaves bearing 5–11 lanceolate leaflets with serrated margins.

Cannabis is one of the oldest cultivated plants known to humanity — archaeological evidence from China dates cultivation to at least 4,000 BCE for fibre, and Indian textual references to its medicinal and ritual use appear in the Atharvaveda (c. 1500–2000 BCE). The plant produces trichome-bearing resinous glands primarily on female floral bracts, which concentrate the plant's rich portfolio of cannabinoids, terpenes, and flavonoids.

02

The Three Classical Forms

Ayurvedic tradition — and Indian ethnobotanical practice — recognises three distinct preparations from Cannabis sativa, each with different potency, therapeutic application, and associated risk. Understanding these distinctions is essential to interpreting classical texts accurately.

Bhanga
भाङ्ग  ·  Leaves & Stems
The dried leaves and small stems of the plant — the mildest preparation. Used to make the ritual drink mixed with milk, water, spices, and sweeteners. The standard form referenced in most classical Ayurvedic texts. Moderate THC content from leaf trichomes. Used in digestive disorders, anxiety, and pain management.
Ganja
गाँजा  ·  Dried Female Flowers
The dried, unfertilised female inflorescence — substantially more potent than bhanga due to concentration of resinous trichomes. Used in classical and folk medicine for pain, neurological disorders, and respiratory conditions. Corresponds to modern medical cannabis flower. Requires careful dosing and supervision.
Charas
चरस  ·  Hand-Rubbed Resin
The fresh resin manually extracted from live female plants — the most potent preparation, equivalent to hashish. Highest concentration of cannabinoids (particularly THC). Used sparingly in classical medicine for intractable pain, terminal illness, and advanced neurological conditions. Traditionally associated with Shaivite ascetics (sadhus).

Phytochemistry & Composition

Cannabis sativa is one of the most phytochemically complex medicinal plants known — producing over 500 distinct compounds, of which more than 150 are plant-specific cannabinoids found nowhere else in nature. This chemical complexity underlies both its extraordinary therapeutic versatility and the nuance required in its clinical application.

04

Ayurvedic Significance

Bhanga occupies a unique position in Ayurvedic literature — simultaneously revered as Vijaya (the victorious herb) and acknowledged as Madakari (intoxicating). Charaka describes its effects in Sutrasthana in the context of intoxicants; Sushruta prescribes it as an anaesthetic adjunct and digestive agent; Vagbhata in the Ashtanga Hridayam lists it among Deepana (digestive stimulant) drugs. The Dhanvantari Nighantu gives it the title Trailokya Vijaya — conqueror of the three worlds.

The classical verse from Rajnighantu reads: "Vijaya vatahara proktaa, deepani pachanee laghu, madakari kashayaa cha, pittalam kaphavatanut" — Vijaya (Cannabis) is said to pacify Vata, stimulate digestive fire, be light, intoxicating, astringent, slightly Pitta-aggravating, and a pacifier of Kapha and Vata disorders.

Importantly, classical Ayurveda also includes Cannabis among the Madya Dravyas (intoxicating substances) and the texts repeatedly warn against recreational overuse — distinguishing sharply between aushadha prayoga (medicinal use) and vyasana (addictive abuse). This distinction is the foundation of its Ayurvedic status.

05

Key Health Benefits

Cannabis is the subject of more pharmacological research in the past two decades than perhaps any other plant on earth. Modern science has confirmed, refined, and in many cases mechanistically explained virtually every classical Ayurvedic therapeutic application — through the discovery of the endocannabinoid system (ECS), a pervasive regulatory network present in all vertebrates that Cannabis compounds directly modulate.

Pain Relief & Analgesic
THC and CBD act on CB1 and CB2 receptors of the endocannabinoid system to modulate pain signalling at multiple levels — peripheral, spinal, and supraspinal. Clinically validated for neuropathic pain, cancer pain, fibromyalgia, and arthritis. The terpene β-caryophyllene independently activates CB2 receptors. Classical Ayurveda lists it as a premier Vedanasthapana (pain-relieving) drug.
Neurological & Anticonvulsant
CBD's anticonvulsant activity is now FDA-validated through Epidiolex — a pharmaceutical CBD preparation for Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut syndromes. Beyond seizures, cannabinoids demonstrate neuroprotective effects in Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, and multiple sclerosis models. Classical Ayurveda recorded its use in Apasmara (epilepsy) and Vata-origin neurological disorders.
Digestive Stimulant & Anti-emetic
Among the most anciently documented applications — its Deepana (digestive stimulant) and Grahi (binding) actions are mentioned in Sushruta and Charaka. THC's appetite-stimulating and anti-nausea properties are now standard supportive care in oncology. Useful in anorexia, cachexia, chemotherapy-induced nausea, and chronic digestive weakness.
Anxiety, Stress & Sleep
CBD demonstrates significant anxiolytic effects via 5-HT1A serotonin receptor modulation — without THC's psychoactivity. CBD and the terpene linalool have demonstrated anxiolytic effects comparable to diazepam in animal models. Low-dose THC is sedative and hypnotic; CBN promotes sleep. Classical Ayurveda used Bhanga preparations for Nidranasha (insomnia) and Chittodvega (anxiety).
Anti-inflammatory
Cannabis-unique flavonoids (Cannflavin A and B) inhibit prostaglandin E2 production more potently than aspirin on a per-weight basis. CBD inhibits NF-κB signalling and COX enzymes. β-Caryophyllene directly activates CB2 receptors on immune cells. Potent anti-inflammatory activity supports use in arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune conditions.
Oncology Support
Beyond antiemetic and analgesic support, cannabinoids have demonstrated direct antitumour activity in multiple preclinical models — inducing apoptosis, inhibiting angiogenesis, and reducing tumour invasion in glioblastoma, breast, colorectal, and lung cancer cell lines. Human clinical evidence remains emerging but promising, particularly for glioblastoma (GBM) in combination with standard therapy.
Glaucoma & Intraocular Pressure
One of the earliest scientifically documented applications (1970s): THC reduces intraocular pressure (IOP) by 25–30% — offering glaucoma relief. CBG independently reduces IOP through different mechanisms. However, the short duration of action (3–4 hours) and systemic effects limit its use as a standalone glaucoma treatment; research into targeted ocular delivery systems is ongoing.
Skin & Dermatological Use
Topical CBD and hemp seed oil show significant benefit in inflammatory skin conditions — eczema, psoriasis, acne, and pruritis — through CB1/CB2 receptor modulation in keratinocytes and mast cells. Hemp seed oil's optimal 3:1 omega-6 to omega-3 ratio is deeply nourishing for the skin barrier. Classical Ayurveda used bhanga paste for skin disorders, itching, and parasitic infestations.

The Endocannabinoid System — Ancient Wisdom, Modern Science

The discovery of the Endocannabinoid System (ECS) in the 1990s by Raphael Mechoulam and colleagues represents perhaps the most significant bridge between traditional Cannabis medicine and modern pharmacology. The ECS comprises CB1 receptors (concentrated in the brain and CNS), CB2 receptors (concentrated in immune tissues and periphery), and endogenous ligands — primarily anandamide (named after the Sanskrit Ananda, meaning bliss) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG).

The ECS regulates a remarkable breadth of physiological functions — pain perception, mood, memory, appetite, sleep, immune function, inflammation, reproductive health, and neuroprotection. Cannabis phytocannabinoids modulate this system directly: THC mimics anandamide by binding CB1 and CB2 receptors; CBD modulates receptor activity without directly binding — acting instead on TRPV1, 5-HT1A, GPR55, and adenosine pathways. This mechanistic understanding finally explains why a single plant could address such a seemingly disparate range of conditions in classical medicine.

Safety, Risks & Precautions

Bhanga's classical texts are remarkably candid about its risks alongside its virtues — a nuance often lost in modern polarised debate. Ayurveda classifies it as a Madya (intoxicant) that must be used with the same precision as any potent medicine: appropriate patient, appropriate preparation, appropriate dose, appropriate duration, and appropriate supervision.

  • Psychosis risk: High-dose or frequent use of high-THC preparations significantly increases risk of psychosis, particularly in individuals with personal or family history of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or psychotic illness. Classical texts warn of Unmada (madness) as a consequence of Bhanga excess.
  • Adolescents and developing brains: Use before age 25 is strongly contraindicated. Adolescent Cannabis use is associated with lasting cognitive impairment, reduced IQ, and substantially elevated risk of psychiatric disorders due to endocannabinoid system's role in neurodevelopment.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Strictly contraindicated. THC crosses the placenta and blood-brain barrier of the fetus; associated with low birth weight, preterm birth, and neurodevelopmental impairment. Transfers through breast milk.
  • Cannabinoid Hyperemesis Syndrome (CHS): Paradoxical condition of severe cyclical vomiting in heavy, long-term users — opposite to Cannabis's anti-emetic action. Mechanism not fully understood but involves CB1 receptor downregulation in the gut.
  • Dependence and withdrawal: Cannabis Use Disorder affects approximately 9% of users, rising to 17% in daily users and 25–50% in those who begin in adolescence. Withdrawal syndrome — anxiety, irritability, insomnia, appetite loss — is real, though not life-threatening.
  • Respiratory: Smoked Cannabis shares many of the carcinogenic risks of tobacco combustion products. Vaporisation reduces but does not eliminate respiratory risks; oral preparations are preferable for medicinal use.
  • Cardiovascular: THC causes dose-dependent tachycardia (elevated heart rate) and transient blood pressure changes. Contraindicated in serious cardiovascular disease, history of cardiac arrhythmia, and recent myocardial infarction.
  • Drug interactions: CBD is a potent inhibitor of CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4 liver enzymes — meaning it can substantially alter blood levels of blood thinners (warfarin), antiepileptics (clobazam), immunosuppressants (tacrolimus), and many other medications. Mandatory disclosure to physicians.
  • Legal compliance: In India, Ganja and Charas remain Schedule I substances under the NDPS Act. Bhang leaves are regulated at the state level with licensing. Always confirm applicable laws before therapeutic or ritual use.
