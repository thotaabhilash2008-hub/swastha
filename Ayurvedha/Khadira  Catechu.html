Khadira
Catechu — The Heartwood of Ancient Healing
Acacia catechu (L.f.) Willd.  ·  Senegalia catechu

❧

Known since Vedic antiquity as Khadira — "the firm, the hard" — this thorned acacia of the dry Himalayan foothills has yielded Katha (cutch) and Kattha to generations of healers, dyers, tanners, and betel-chewers. Charaka placed it among the most important trees of classical medicine; Sushruta ranked its heartwood extract as supreme in treating kushtha (skin disorders), prameha (metabolic dysfunction), and diseases of the blood and oral cavity.

01

Botanical Profile

Khadira (Acacia catechu) is a medium-sized, deciduous thorny tree of the Fabaceae (Leguminosae) family, native to the Indian subcontinent and Myanmar, now naturalised across tropical Asia and Africa. It typically grows to 9–12 m in height with a rough, dark-grey to brown bark that peels off in long strips, revealing a reddish-brown layer beneath. The sapwood is white; the heartwood is dark reddish-brown — the source of the prized Catechu (Katha / Kattha / Cutch) extract.

Leaves are bipinnate with 8–30 pairs of pinnae, each bearing 30–50 pairs of linear leaflets. Twin recurved stipular spines at the base of each leaf are characteristic. Flowers are pale yellow or cream, arranged in cylindrical spike-like racemes 5–10 cm long, appearing July–September. Pods are thin, flat, strap-shaped, 5–8 cm long, dehiscent, brown when ripe, containing 3–10 seeds. The tree thrives in dry deciduous forests, riverine woodlands, and foothills up to 1,500 m altitude — particularly the Himalayan Shivalik ranges, Rajasthan, and Central India.

Phytochemistry & Composition

The heartwood of Khadira — and especially its aqueous extract Katha — is among the richest natural sources of condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) in the plant kingdom. Catechin and epicatechin, the defining compounds, constitute up to 35% of dry heartwood weight. These polyphenols are potent antioxidants that form the biochemical basis for virtually all of Khadira's classical and pharmacologically validated actions.

Beyond catechins, the heartwood yields a complex matrix of flavonoids, terpenoids, phytosterols, and phenolic glycosides. The bark contains a different tannin profile dominated by gallotannins, while the gum, leaves, and seeds each offer distinct secondary metabolite profiles. Two distinct commercial products — pale catechu (Kattha, used in betel leaf) and dark/black catechu (Cutch, used for dyeing and tanning) — arise from different processing methods of the same heartwood.

Ayurvedic Significance

Khadira holds an extraordinary position in classical Ayurveda — it is one of the very few substances simultaneously cited across all eight branches (Ashtanga Ayurveda). Charaka lists it among the Kushthaghna (anti-dermatosis), Kandughna (anti-pruritic), Krimighna (anthelmintic), Trishnanigrahana (thirst-quenching), and Lekhaniya (scraping/reducing) mahakashayas — placing it in five of his ten fundamental dashemani groupings. Sushruta classifies it in Nyagrodhadi and Salasaradi Gana, prizing the heartwood for skin, blood, and oral conditions.

The classical shloka — "Khadiro raktatvakpushpo raktasaro vranarogahrit, kaphaharo mehaghno cha kushtha kriminivarhanam" — celebrates Khadira's red bark, red heartwood, and red flowers, and praises its capacity to heal wounds, clear Kapha, combat prameha, and eradicate skin diseases and parasites. The Ashtanga Hridayam devotes special attention to Khadira in the context of Kushtha treatment, recommending its decoction, ghrita, and tailam preparations extensively.

Khadirarishta — a classical fermented preparation combining Khadira heartwood with Daru Haridra, Haritaki, and other herbs — remains one of the most widely prescribed Ayurvedic formulas for chronic skin conditions. Khadira Vati, tablets of Katha extract, are universally used for oral hygiene, ulcers, and throat conditions.

Key Health Benefits

Khadira is one of the most extensively pharmacologically researched Ayurvedic botanicals. Its catechin-rich heartwood extract has been validated across dermatological, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, and wound-healing domains — with a particularly strong evidence base for oral and skin applications.

Skin Disorders & Blood Purification
Classical texts call Khadira kushtha-kriminivarhanam — the supreme destroyer of skin diseases and parasites. Catechins and procyanidins exert potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory action on inflammatory skin conditions including eczema, psoriasis, urticaria, and acne. Khadirarishta remains a gold-standard Ayurvedic formula for chronic skin conditions.
Oral Health & Dentistry
Khadira Vati is the classical oral hygiene preparation — Katha extract is chewed or held in the mouth to treat aphthous ulcers, stomatitis, gingivitis, dental caries, and halitosis. Its astringent action tightens gum tissue; its antimicrobial catechins inhibit Streptococcus mutans, the primary caries-causing bacterium. One of Charaka's foremost Dantadhavana (teeth-cleaning) herbs.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Catechin and procyanidin extracts significantly inhibit COX-1, COX-2, and lipoxygenase pathways — validated in carrageenan-induced paw edema and cotton pellet granuloma models. Traditional application of bark decoction as a fomentation for arthritic joints is supported by in vivo analgesic studies comparable to aspirin at equivalent doses.
Anti-diabetic & Metabolic
A major classical indication — Khadira mehaghno cha. Catechin significantly enhances glucose uptake in peripheral tissues via GLUT-4 translocation, inhibits α-glucosidase and α-amylase (reducing post-prandial glucose spikes), and improves pancreatic beta-cell function in streptozotocin-induced diabetic models. Khadira churna is a classical formula for prameha (urinary disorders including glycosuria).
Antimicrobial & Anthelmintic
Broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity documented against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Candida albicans, and Aspergillus species. Anti-parasitic action against intestinal worms is well-documented in folk practice and partially validated against Ascaris in animal studies.
Wound Healing
Katha extract in topical preparations accelerates all phases of wound healing — haemostasis (via astringent tannins), anti-inflammatory phase (via catechin-mediated prostaglandin suppression), proliferative phase (via collagen cross-linking), and remodelling (via antimicrobial protection). Classical Ayurveda uses bark paste and Katha directly on ulcers, burns, and abscesses.
Hepatoprotective
Catechin and taxifolin in Khadira extract protect hepatocytes against CCl₄-induced and paracetamol-induced liver damage in animal studies, reducing AST, ALT, and bilirubin levels significantly. The mechanism involves free-radical scavenging, mitochondrial stabilisation, and upregulation of hepatic antioxidant enzymes — supporting the classical use for liver disorders and jaundice (kamala).
Respiratory & Anti-tussive
Classical texts recommend Khadira decoction in chronic cough, bronchitis, and asthma due to its Kapha-reducing, drying, and antimicrobial properties. Khirsal (acacetin glucoside) demonstrates smooth muscle relaxant activity on bronchial tissue in isolated organ studies, offering pharmacological support for bronchospasm relief in traditional preparations.

How to Consume Khadira

Khadira is used extensively in both internal and external preparations. Its heartwood extract Katha is the most widely available form and is safe for routine oral use in appropriate amounts. Internal decoctions and classical formulations (Khadirarishta, Khadira Ghrita) should be taken under practitioner guidance. The bark and heartwood chips are available in Ayurvedic pharmacies and herbal markets across India.

Safety & Precautions

Khadira has an excellent safety profile and has been consumed as part of daily betel-leaf preparations (tambula) across South and Southeast Asia for millennia. At therapeutic Ayurvedic doses, the heartwood extract is generally well-tolerated. The primary concerns relate to excessive or prolonged use, specific physiological states, and potential for drug interactions via its strong antioxidant chemistry.

  • Pregnancy: Avoid internal use in pregnancy. Classical texts flag Khadira's Kapha-Vata reducing action and its potential to affect uterine tissue; though direct evidence is limited, caution is advised given the high tannin content.
  • Constipation: The strong astringent (kashaya) quality of Khadira can exacerbate constipation and dry bowel conditions. Use with caution in Vata-type constitutions prone to dryness; combine with Triphala or unctuous foods.
  • Dry constitutions (Vata prakruti): Excessive or prolonged internal use in Ruksha (dry) constitutions may cause increased dryness — monitor for dry mouth, dry skin, or constipation and reduce dose if these arise.
  • Anticoagulant medications: Catechins have demonstrated mild antiplatelet and antithrombotic activity in pharmacological studies. Concurrent use with warfarin, aspirin, or antiplatelet drugs should be supervised to avoid additive bleeding risk.
  • Antidiabetic medications: Khadira lowers blood glucose. When combined with hypoglycaemic drugs (metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin), blood sugar should be monitored to prevent hypoglycaemia.
  • Excessive betel nut chewing: Katha is traditionally used with betel nut (Areca catechu) in paan. The combination of betel nut, tobacco, and Katha in paan products is associated with oral submucous fibrosis and carcinoma risk — this risk relates to the overall paan habit, not Khadira per se, but context is important.
  • Iron absorption: High tannin content can chelate dietary iron and reduce its absorption. Avoid consuming Khadira preparations immediately before or after iron-rich meals or iron supplements.
  • Renal considerations: Large doses of tannin-rich extracts over long periods have theoretical oxalate-loading implications. Individuals with a history of oxalate kidney stones should use with caution and adequate hydration.
