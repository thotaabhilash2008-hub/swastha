Plaksha
Indian Wavy-leaf Fig — The Sacred River Fig
Ficus lacor Buch.-Ham.  ·  Moraceae

Fig. 1 — The broad, shade-giving canopy of Ficus lacor, a sacred riverbank and temple tree of the subcontinent  ·  Fig. 2 — Characteristic large, wavy-margined leaves and small globose figs ripening on branchlets

❧

One of the five sacred fig trees of ancient India — alongside Ashvattha, Audumbara, Nyagrodha, and Madhuka — Plaksha has been venerated since the Vedic age as the tree under which the sacred river Sarasvati springs eternal. Its bark, latex, leaves, and fruit form a classical Ayurvedic quartet for stanching bleeding, healing wounds, cooling inflammation, and strengthening the reproductive and urinary systems across three millennia of continuous unbroken practice.

01

Botanical Profile

Ficus lacor (syn. Ficus infectoria auct. non Roxb.; Ficus tsiela Roxb.) is a large, deciduous to semi-evergreen fig tree of the Moraceae family, growing to 20–30 metres with a wide-spreading crown and a trunk that, in maturity, develops prominent buttresses and occasionally aerial prop roots — lending it the appearance of its more famous cousins the banyan and the peepal. It is native to the sub-Himalayan tracts, foothills, and riverbanks from Pakistan and Nepal through northern and central India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, and into Southeast Asia, typically found at altitudes up to 1,500 metres.

The leaves are large — 10–20 cm long — ovate to elliptic-oblong with a markedly wavy or undulate margin and a long, slender petiole; their surface is smooth above and slightly hairy below. Young leaves emerge a distinctive coppery-red before maturing to deep green. The figs (syconia) are small, 8–12 mm across, in pairs, turning yellowish to purple-red at maturity, and are eagerly consumed by birds, bats, and monkeys, making Plaksha an ecological keystone species. The bark is pale grey to whitish, smooth when young, exuding a milky latex when cut. It flowers and fruits throughout the year with peaks in spring and autumn.

02

Phytochemistry

The phytochemistry of Ficus lacor closely mirrors the rich secondary metabolite profile common to the medically important Ficus genus — dominated by polyphenols, triterpenoids, sterols, and flavonoids — with the bark and latex carrying the highest concentrations of bioactive compounds. Tannins (hydrolysable and condensed) are the dominant class and underpin the plant's classical haemostatic and wound-healing reputation. Latex contains a complex proteolytic enzyme system analogous to ficin in other Ficus species.

Several bioactives recently isolated from F. lacor bark — including β-sitosterol glucoside, lupeol, gluanol acetate, and various triterpenoids — have confirmed anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and antibacterial activities in experimental models, lending modern credibility to classical Ayurvedic formulations using Plaksha bark in conditions ranging from diabetes and leucorrhoea to haemorrhage and skin infections.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Plaksha is one of the Pañcavriksha — the five auspicious fig trees of Ayurveda — and is mentioned in the Atharvaveda, Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam, and all major Nighantus. Charaka includes Plaksha in the Nyagrodhadi Gana — a classical ten-herb group dominated by fig trees used for their astringent, healing, and Pittahara properties. Sushruta places it in Rodhradi and Nyagrodhadi groups. It is also listed in Kashaya Skandha (astringent group) across multiple texts.

Classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha: "Plakshah kashayatiktoshno raktapittakaphapranut, vranahara shothahara prameha yonidoshanut" — Plaksha is astringent, bitter, slightly warm, clears Raktapitta (bleeding disorders), Kapha, wounds, swellings, Prameha (urinary disorders including diabetes), and diseases of the female reproductive tract. Its bark decoction is a cornerstone of classical formulations for leucorrhoea, menorrhagia, haemorrhage, and diabetic conditions.

04

Key Health Benefits

Plaksha's pharmacological profile — anchored in its tannin-triterpenoid-flavonoid matrix — gives it a coherent set of clinically relevant actions, particularly in haemostasis, wound healing, reproductive health, antimicrobial activity, and metabolic support, corroborated by both classical authority and emerging experimental data.

Haemostasis & Bleeding Disorders
Plaksha is Ayurveda's foremost Raktasthambhana (blood-stanching) fig bark. The high tannin and phlobaphene content contracts blood vessels, promotes clot formation, and arrests haemorrhage — classically indicated in menorrhagia, haematemesis, haematuria, bleeding piles, and traumatic wounds. Bark decoction applied locally or consumed as kwatha is a classical first response to abnormal bleeding.
Wound Healing & Skin Infections
The latex of Plaksha possesses proteolytic ficin activity that debrides necrotic tissue while tannins provide antimicrobial and astringent cover, and flavonoids reduce local inflammation — a complete wound-healing trilogy. Bark paste is applied to chronic ulcers, infected wounds, and skin infections; the leaf decoction is used as a wound wash in tribal medicine from the Himalayan foothills to Kerala.
Gynaecological Health
One of Plaksha's most established classical applications — bark decoction as a vaginal douche and internal kwatha for leucorrhoea, menorrhagia, and general yonidosha (diseases of the female reproductive tract). Its astringent-haemostatic action tones uterine and vaginal tissues, reduces muco-purulent discharge, and controls excessive uterine bleeding. Featured in classical formulations like Pushyanuga Churna.
Antidiabetic & Prameha
Classified in Ayurveda under Pramehaghna (anti-diabetic) herbs — bark and aerial root extracts show significant hypoglycaemic activity in streptozotocin-induced diabetic animal models. β-Sitosterol glucoside and lupeol contribute to improved insulin sensitivity and alpha-glucosidase inhibition. Plaksha bark is an ingredient in several classical Prameha formulations alongside Jambu, Haridra, and Shilajit.
Antimicrobial & Anti-inflammatory
Bark and leaf extracts demonstrate broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans in vitro. Lupeol, gluanol acetate, and quercetin collectively inhibit COX-2-mediated inflammation — confirming the classical Shothahara (anti-oedema) and Kushtaghna (skin disease-resolving) karma of the bark.
Diarrhoea & Dysentery
As a Grahi herb (absorptive, stool-binding), Plaksha bark decoction is used in acute and chronic diarrhoea, dysentery, and irritable bowel syndrome with loose motions. Tannins bind intestinal mucosa, reduce secretory fluid loss, and inhibit enteropathogens simultaneously. A classical combination with Kutaja (Holarrhena antidysenterica) is used in amoebic dysentery.
Urinary Tract & Burning Dysuria
Plaksha's Pramehaghna and Pittahara properties extend to the urinary tract — bark decoction soothes burning micturition, reduces urinary tract inflammation, and is used in mild urinary infections. Featured in classical formulations for mutrakriccha (painful urination) alongside Gokshura and Chandana, providing a cooling, astringent, and antimicrobial triad.
Oral Health & Dental Care
The astringent bark decoction is a traditional gargle for bleeding gums, mouth ulcers, aphthae, and gingivitis. Chewing tender twigs of Plaksha as a natural toothbrush (danta kashtha) is prescribed in Charaka Samhita — the tannins tighten gum tissue, the antimicrobial compounds suppress periodontal pathogens, and the gentle abrasion cleans enamel surfaces.
05

How to Consume Plaksha

Plaksha is primarily a bark medicine — the dried bark of mature trees is the most used and best-documented part. Latex is used topically; leaves and fruits have secondary applications. As with all astringent-tannin-rich herbs, prolonged high-dose internal use should be supervised; standard therapeutic courses of 4–8 weeks are typical.

Safety & Precautions

Plaksha bark has an excellent safety record in classical Ayurveda at traditional doses. Its primary risks relate to its strongly astringent, drying nature and the latex's potential for local irritation. Sustained high-dose use of tannin-rich preparations requires monitoring.

  • Constipation & Vata aggravation: Plaksha's Grahi (absorbent) and Ruksha (dry) properties can cause or worsen constipation, intestinal dryness, and Vata-related symptoms with prolonged use. Ensure adequate hydration and avoid in those with chronic constipation or Vata-predominant constitution without unctuous anupana.
  • Pregnancy: High-dose internal use of astringent bark decoctions is generally avoided in pregnancy due to uterine-toning activity. Moderate culinary consumption of ripe fruits is considered safe. Consult a practitioner for any therapeutic use during pregnancy.
  • Latex sensitisation: Plaksha latex contains calcium oxalate crystals and proteolytic enzymes that may cause contact dermatitis, mucosal irritation, or latex-type allergic reactions in sensitised individuals. Always dilute before topical use; avoid contact with eyes, open wounds on sensitive skin, and mucous membranes.
  • Tannin overload with prolonged use: Sustained high tannin intake may impair iron and mineral absorption — relevant for individuals with anaemia or nutrient-deficiency conditions. Separate Plaksha preparations by at least two hours from iron supplements or iron-rich meals.
  • Interaction with antidiabetic medications: Due to its documented hypoglycaemic activity, concurrent use with insulin, metformin, or sulphonylureas requires monitoring of blood glucose to avoid hypoglycaemia.
  • Botanical identity: Plaksha (F. lacor) must be carefully distinguished from Ashvattha (F. religiosa) and Udumbara (F. racemosa), which share Sanskrit Ficus nomenclature but have distinct therapeutic profiles. Incorrect substitution is common in the market; verify identity with a reputable supplier.
  • Children: Internal use of bark decoctions in young children should be under practitioner supervision due to the concentrated tannin content and its potential to irritate developing GI mucosa at high doses.
