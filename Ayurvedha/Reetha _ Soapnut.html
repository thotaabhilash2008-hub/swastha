Reetha
Soapnut — Nature's Ancient Cleanser
Sapindus mukorossi  ·  Sapindus trifoliatus

Fig. 1 — Ripe amber-brown drupes of Reetha hanging in clusters  ·  Fig. 2 — Sun-dried Reetha shells ready for therapeutic and cosmetic use

❧

Known in Sanskrit as Aritha — "that which cleanses" — Reetha has served the Indian subcontinent for over three millennia as a sacred soap, medicine, and pesticide. Its pericarp, extraordinarily rich in saponins, lathers in water like no other plant, earning its place in classical Ayurveda as a Keshya (hair tonic), Shiro Virechana (nasal errhine), and remedy in the Phala Varga of the Ashtanga Hridayam.

01

Botanical Profile

Reetha (Sapindus mukorossi Gaertn.) is a large, deciduous tree of the Sapindaceae family, growing to 15–25 m in height, native to the sub-Himalayan tract, Nepal, Bhutan, and China. A closely related species, S. trifoliatus, is native to peninsular India. The tree is long-lived (up to 90 years), with a dense spreading crown, pinnate leaves up to 45 cm long, and panicles of small white flowers in spring. Its most remarkable feature is its drupe — a translucent, amber to dark-brown, fleshy pericarp enclosing a single hard, jet-black seed. This pericarp, the "soapnut shell," foams vigorously in water due to extraordinary saponin content (7–10%), and has been harvested for millennia as a natural surfactant for washing hair, silk, jewellery, and skin.

02

Phytochemistry Composition

The pericarp of Reetha is among the richest natural sources of triterpenoid saponins in the plant kingdom. Saponins (7–10% dry weight) — predominantly mukoroside, sapindoside A–G, and hederagenin glycosides — are responsible for its lathering, emulsifying, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities. Beyond saponins, the pericarp contains polyphenols, flavonoids, fatty acids, and minerals. The seeds contain a fixed oil rich in erucic and gadoleic acids; the leaves contain quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Reetha is classified in the Phala Varga (fruit group) of the Ashtanga Hridayam and appears in the Dhanvantari Nighantu, Bhavaprakasha Nighantu, and Raja Nighantu. Vagbhata includes it in Shiro Virechana dravyas — substances used to induce sneezing and purge excess Kapha from the head. It is a primary ingredient in classical hair formulations (Keshya dravyas) and is employed in Nasya (nasal therapy) for sinusitis and epilepsy.

Classical shloka describes Aritha as "katu tikta kashaya ushna, kaphavataghna, shiro virechana, krimighna, vishaghna" — pungent, bitter, and astringent in taste; hot in potency; pacifying Kapha and Vata; purging the head; destroys parasites and neutralises poisons. Its foam (phena) is the primary active form in classical application.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern research has confirmed many of Reetha's classical applications — its saponins exhibit documented surfactant, antimicrobial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, larvicidal, and hepatoprotective activities. It remains one of the most extensively used plants in Ayurvedic cosmetology and is gaining global recognition as a sustainable, biodegradable alternative to synthetic detergents.

Hair Care & Scalp Health
Reetha's most celebrated application — its gentle saponin foam cleanses hair and scalp without stripping natural oils, unlike sulphate shampoos. Clinical studies confirm efficacy in reducing dandruff, scalp eczema, lice infestations, and seborrheic dermatitis. Combined with Amla and Shikakai, it forms the classic Ayurvedic hair wash trinity.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Ethanolic extracts of the pericarp show broad-spectrum activity against S. aureus, E. coli, Candida albicans, Aspergillus, and dermatophytes responsible for ringworm and tinea. The saponin-surfactant action disrupts microbial cell membranes, offering both topical and internal antimicrobial potential.
Respiratory & Expectorant
A key Kapha-clearing herb — classically used in bronchitis, asthma, and excess mucus. The saponins stimulate mucosal secretion and thin bronchial mucus, aiding expectoration. Nasya (nasal instillation) of Reetha decoction is a classical treatment for sinusitis, nasal polyps, and allergic rhinitis.
Antiparasitic & Larvicidal
Among the strongest natural antiparasitic agents — extracts are highly toxic to Aedes aegypti and Culex quinquefasciatus mosquito larvae (used in WHO-recognised vector control research). Topically, it eliminates head lice, scabies mites, and skin parasites. Classical use as Krimighna (destroyer of parasites) is well-validated.
Neurological & Anti-epileptic
A classical remedy for apasmara (epilepsy) — Nasya with Reetha powder in cow's ghee is referenced in Charaka Samhita. Animal studies on saponin fractions demonstrate anticonvulsant activity, possibly through GABA-modulating effects. Also used in hysteria, migraine with Kapha involvement, and fainting.
Skin & Wound Care
Pericarp paste or wash is applied to eczema, psoriasis, scabies, and skin fungal infections. Its mild saponin foam acts as a gentle antiseptic wash for wounds and ulcers. The anti-inflammatory triterpenoids reduce redness and swelling; astringent tannins tighten and tone the skin.
Dental & Oral Hygiene
Classical use as a tooth powder and gum cleanser — still practised in traditional communities. Saponins reduce oral bacterial load, while astringent tannins tighten gum tissue. Reetha-based tooth powders demonstrate activity against Streptococcus mutans, the primary caries-causing organism.
Hepatoprotective & Antioxidant
Hederagenin and oleanolic acid from the pericarp demonstrate hepatoprotective activity in CCl₄-induced liver injury models, reducing ALT and AST enzyme elevations. Significant DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activity of phenolic fractions supports its use in protecting cells from oxidative stress.
05

Traditional & Folk Applications

Beyond formal Ayurvedic medicine, Reetha pervades the folk traditions of the Indian subcontinent. In hill communities of Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh, the whole soapnut is used as a natural laundry wash, jewellery cleaner, and leather conditioner. In Nepal, it is a traditional offering in religious ceremonies. Fishermen use pounded soapnut shells to stun fish in ponds — an ancient ichthyotoxic practice. The wood is used for cricket bats and light construction; the seeds as beads, marbles, and buttons.

In Unani medicine, Reetha (called Aritha) is used as an Muqawwi Dimagh (brain tonic) and for Sara (epilepsy). In Siddha, it appears as Puvam in external applications for skin diseases and hair loss. Its seeds contain a non-edible oil used in biopesticide and biofuel research, and the pericarp saponins are now investigated for their potential as natural adjuvants in vaccine formulations.

How to Use Reetha

Reetha is used primarily as an external application — hair wash, skin rinse, dental powder — and secondarily as an internal Ayurvedic preparation under supervision. Internal preparations require careful dosing due to the haemolytic potential of saponins at high concentrations. The pericarp is the primary medicinal part; seeds are generally discarded for therapeutic preparations.

Safety & Precautions

Reetha is safe for topical use at normal concentrations and has a long record of external application without adverse effects. However, internal use requires caution — saponins are mildly haemolytic at high doses and can irritate the gastrointestinal mucosa. The key is to respect classical dose limits and never self-medicate for serious conditions.

  • Pregnancy: Avoid internal consumption. Some sources indicate uterotonic effects at higher doses. Topical hair and skin use at normal concentrations is generally considered safe.
  • Breastfeeding: Internal use not recommended. Limit to external applications. Saponins can potentially transfer into breast milk.
  • Eye contact: Strictly avoid — the saponin-rich water is a strong mucous membrane irritant. If contact occurs, flush immediately with copious clean water.
  • Haemolytic potential: High-dose internal use can lyse red blood cells. Individuals with haemolytic anaemia or G6PD deficiency must avoid internal preparations.
  • Open wounds: Avoid applying soapnut decoction directly to deep or bleeding wounds — saponins irritate exposed tissue. Use only on intact or superficially broken skin.
  • Children under 5: Do not use Reetha decoction as a baby shampoo or near infants' eyes or mouths. Use diluted, mild preparations only under supervision for older children.
  • Pitta-dominant constitutions: Internal use may aggravate Pitta — contraindicated in peptic ulcer, hyperacidity, and inflammatory bowel conditions.
  • Drug interactions: May theoretically interact with blood-thinning medications (warfarin, aspirin) and drugs with narrow therapeutic indices due to surfactant effects on mucosal absorption.
  • Allergic contact dermatitis: Though rare, some individuals develop sensitisation to saponins on repeated topical exposure — discontinue if redness or itching develops.
