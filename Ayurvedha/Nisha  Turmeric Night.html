Nishapushpa
Turmeric Flower — The Blossom of the Night Goddess
Curcuma longa L.  ·  Zingiberaceae

Fig. 1 — The elegant cone-shaped inflorescence of Curcuma longa, crowned with pale pink and white bracts  ·  Fig. 2 — Close view of the turmeric flower emerging from its golden-rhizomed plant

❧

Though the world knows Haridra for its golden rhizome, it is the flower — Nishapushpa, "bloom of the night one" — that carries its own quiet pharmacopoeia. Borne on a cone-shaped spike of pale bracts, this ethereal blossom is prized in folk medicine across South and Southeast Asia as an anti-inflammatory, digestive, and wound-healing agent, its culinary and ornamental grace matched by a rich heritage of therapeutic use.

01

Botanical Profile

Curcuma longa is a robust, rhizomatous perennial herb of the Zingiberaceae family — kin to ginger, cardamom, and galangal — native to the Indian subcontinent and cultivated throughout tropical Asia. The plant grows 60–100 cm tall from a cluster of aromatic, deep-orange rhizomes, with large, oblong-lanceolate leaves emerging on long sheaths. The flower spike — the Nishapushpa — rises directly from the rhizome on a separate peduncle or from the centre of the leafy shoot, reaching 15–20 cm in length.

The inflorescence is a dense cylindrical spike of spirally arranged bracts: the lower fertile bracts are pale green, the upper sterile coma bracts are vivid pink to pale lilac, and the actual flowers are small, yellow, tubular, and three-petalled — emerging one or two at a time from within the fertile bracts. The flower is ephemeral, lasting only a day; the entire spike, however, persists for weeks, making it highly ornamental. It blooms in the monsoon season — July to September — coinciding with peak rhizome growth.

Phytochemistry of the Flower

While the rhizome is globally studied for curcuminoids (curcumin, demethoxycurcumin, bisdemethoxycurcumin), the flower of Curcuma longa presents a distinct and complementary phytochemical profile. Flower extracts are notably rich in flavonoids, phenolic acids, and essential oil volatiles — including ar-turmerone, α-turmerone, and zingiberene — while curcuminoid content, though present, is lower than in the rhizome. This makes the flower pharmacologically valuable in its own right, particularly for antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory applications.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Haridra is one of the most extensively described plants in classical Ayurveda, appearing in Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam, and virtually every Nighantu text. The plant's name Nisha ("night") refers to its deep golden rhizome resembling the skin colour attributed to the goddess Durga (Nisha as an epithet), while Nishapushpa — the flower — is specifically referenced in regional ethnobotanical traditions and in Bhavaprakasha as the flowering part of the Haridra plant used in ritual, cosmetic, and therapeutic preparations.

Classically, Haridra (including its flower) is categorised under Lekhaniya (scraping/reducing), Kushtaghna (skin disease–curing), and Krimighna (anti-parasitic) drugs by Charaka. In Sushruta, it appears in Priyangvadi Gana (complexion-improving drugs) and Mustadi Gana. The flower specifically appears in folk traditions of Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and Kerala as an ingredient in ritual offerings, bridal preparations, and wound-healing poultices.

04

Key Health Benefits

The flower of Curcuma longa shares the broad therapeutic footprint of the rhizome while offering unique properties from its distinct phytochemical composition. Folk traditions of South India particularly value the fresh flower in skin care, wound healing, and menstrual health — uses corroborated by emerging pharmacological research.

Anti-inflammatory
Curcuminoids in the flower inhibit COX-2, LOX, and NF-κB inflammatory pathways. Flower extract poultices are applied to inflamed joints, sprains, and post-partum abdominal inflammation in traditional South Indian practice. Comparable to ibuprofen in some animal models.
Wound Healing & Skin Care
Fresh flower paste is applied to cuts, burns, and infected wounds in folk medicine across Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Curcumin accelerates re-epithelialisation and collagen deposition. Combined with its antimicrobial activity, the flower is a powerful topical healing agent.
Digestive Health
The flower's volatile oils — zingiberene, cineole — stimulate bile flow, improve digestion, relieve flatulence, and soothe intestinal inflammation. In Kerala cuisine, turmeric flowers are eaten as a vegetable precisely for their digestive benefits alongside their delicate flavour.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Flower extracts demonstrate significant inhibition of E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, and Aspergillus sp. Essential oil constituents — ar-turmerone and 1,8-cineole — are primarily responsible for antifungal activity, making the flower useful in skin and oral infections.
Antioxidant Activity
DPPH and FRAP assay studies show high free-radical scavenging activity in turmeric flower extracts — in some studies exceeding that of the rhizome per unit weight, due to elevated flavonoid and phenolic acid concentrations in the bracts and petals.
Menstrual & Uterine Health
Classical texts describe Haridra as artavajanana (emmenagogue) — promoting healthy menstrual flow. The flower is used in folk preparations for dysmenorrhea (painful periods) and menstrual irregularity across South Indian tribal traditions, often combined with jaggery.
Skin Brightening & Cosmetic
Turmeric flowers are a traditional ingredient in bridal ubtan (body scrub) preparations in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. The flower's mild curcuminoid content and volatile oils impart a subtle luminosity to the skin, and its anti-inflammatory properties reduce redness and pigmentation.
Respiratory Support
1,8-Cineole and ar-turmerone in the flower act as expectorants and bronchodilators. Decoction of the flower is used in folk traditions for cough, bronchitis, and sinusitis — complementing the rhizome's well-known application of turmeric milk for respiratory ailments.
05

Culinary Uses of the Flower

Unlike the rhizome, which is universally used, the turmeric flower is a regional culinary delicacy — most prominently in Kerala, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, and parts of Northeast India and Southeast Asia. Its flavour is delicate, mildly bitter, faintly floral, and earthy — quite unlike the assertive pungency of the root. It is typically used fresh or briefly cooked, as prolonged heat diminishes both flavour and nutritional value.

How to Use Nishapushpa

The turmeric flower is primarily used fresh during its July–September blooming season. It can be preserved by sun-drying or refrigerating for up to a week. Internal therapeutic preparations should be taken in moderation; topical use is generally safe for all.

Safety & Precautions

The turmeric flower is generally very safe — far more so than many medicinal herbs. Its culinary use across South and Southeast Asia for centuries attests to excellent safety at food-grade quantities. Therapeutic internal use at higher doses warrants some attention, particularly regarding its emmenagogic (uterine-stimulating) properties.

  • Pregnancy: Culinary amounts (as a vegetable in cooking) are safe. Concentrated extracts, decoctions, or medicinal doses should be avoided during pregnancy due to emmenagogic and uterine-stimulating properties.
  • Gallbladder conditions: Curcuminoids strongly stimulate bile production — those with gallstones or bile duct obstruction should use therapeutic doses cautiously.
  • Blood-thinning medications: Curcumin has mild antiplatelet activity. Avoid high therapeutic doses if on warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel, or other anticoagulants without medical supervision.
  • Topical staining: Fresh flower juice and paste will stain skin and fabric yellow — this is temporary on skin (2–3 days) but near-permanent on fabric. Use accordingly.
  • Iron absorption: Curcumin can chelate iron. Individuals with iron-deficiency anaemia should avoid consuming turmeric flower preparations simultaneously with iron-rich foods or supplements.
  • Allergic dermatitis: Rare contact allergy to Curcuma species (usually from curcumin or essential oils) can cause contact dermatitis — patch-test topical preparations if skin sensitivity is a concern.
  • Drug interactions: Curcumin may affect the metabolism of some drugs via CYP450 enzymes. Consult a physician if using multiple medications alongside concentrated flower extracts.
