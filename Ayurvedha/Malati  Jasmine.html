Jasmine — The Queen of Night Blossoms
Jasminum grandiflorum  ·  Jasminum officinale  ·  Jasminum sambac

Fig. 1 — Star-white blossoms of Jasminum grandiflorum unfurling at dusk  ·  Fig. 2 — Malati garlands, a devotional and therapeutic tradition across the Indian subcontinent

❧

Celebrated in Sanskrit as Malati, Mallika, and Sumanasa — "of beautiful fragrance" — Jasmine has perfumed India's temples, bridal chambers, and apothecaries for over 3,000 years. Cherished in Charaka Samhita as a wound-healer and mind-soother, and in Sushruta Samhita for its action on the eyes and skin, this luminous vine occupies a singular place at the crossroads of medicine, devotion, poetry, and perfumery.

01

Botanical Profile

Jasmine belongs to the genus Jasminum of the olive family Oleaceae, encompassing over 200 species native to tropical and warm-temperate regions of Eurasia and Africa. The three species of foremost Ayurvedic significance are J. grandiflorum (Spanish Jasmine / Jati), J. sambac (Arabian Jasmine / Mallika), and J. officinale (Common Jasmine / Malati). All three are twining or scrambling shrubs with opposite, pinnately compound or simple leaves and clusters of intensely fragrant, star-shaped white or pale-pink flowers.

Flowers bloom predominantly at night or at dusk — releasing their highest concentration of volatile oils in the cooler hours — which accounts for both their distinctive fragrance profile and their Sanskrit epithets like Nishapushpa ("night flower") and Chandrapushpa ("moon flower"). The flower has 5–9 petals, a slender calyx tube, and two stamens enclosed within the corolla. Fruits are small black berries rarely observed in cultivation, as most jasmine is vegetatively propagated.

02

Phytochemistry & Aroma

Jasmine's pharmacological richness lies primarily in its volatile oil — obtained by solvent extraction or enfleurage — and in the glycosides, flavonoids, and phenolic acids concentrated in its flowers, leaves, and roots. Jasmine absolute from J. grandiflorum is one of the most complex floral oils known, containing over 100 identified constituents, with benzyl acetate, linalool, indole, methyl jasmonate, and phytol among the most abundant.

Methyl jasmonate — the compound responsible for jasmine's characteristic warm, narcotic depth — was subsequently identified as a universal plant stress-signalling hormone, now used extensively in pharmacological and agricultural research. The roots contain iridoid glycosides (jasminin) and alkaloids with antimicrobial and antioxidant significance. Leaves are rich in flavonoids (rutin, quercetin) and hydroxycinnamic acids.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Malati and Mallika are referenced extensively across the classical corpus. Charaka mentions jasmine flowers in formulations for Kushtha (skin disorders), Vrana (wound healing), and oral hygiene. Sushruta prescribes the leaf juice for eye diseases and the root for dental disorders and fever. Vagbhata endorses the flower as Medhya (intellect-promoting) and Hridya (cardiac tonic).

Classical verse — "Malati tikta kashaya katu sheetala vishapaham / Vranahara twakaprasada netrahita cha keshyam" — describes Malati as bitter-astringent-pungent in taste, cooling in potency, poison-neutralising, wound-healing, skin-brightening, beneficial to the eyes, and promoter of hair growth.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacology has validated a wide spectrum of jasmine's classical indications, with studies in aromatherapy, dermatology, neuropsychopharmacology, and antimicrobial science confirming the efficacy of both the essential oil and plant extracts across multiple organ systems.

Anxiolytic & Mood Elevating
Inhalation of jasmine essential oil has been shown in controlled human trials to elevate mood, reduce anxiety, and increase alertness — working through olfactory stimulation of the limbic system. Linalool and benzyl acetate modulate GABA-A receptor activity, producing mild sedation without drowsiness.
Skin Care & Wound Healing
Jasmine's classical designation as Twakaprasada (skin-brightening) and Vranahara (wound-healer) is supported by demonstrated antimicrobial activity against skin pathogens and anti-inflammatory effects that reduce wound-site redness and accelerate tissue repair. The oil is used in dermatological preparations for dry, sensitive skin.
Uterine Tonic & Labour Support
Jasmine oil applied as abdominal massage during labour has been clinically shown to reduce pain, shorten labour duration, and lower anxiety — its antispasmodic and oxytocin-like properties documented in obstetric aromatherapy research. Classically used as Garbhashaya Shodana (uterine purifier) post-partum.
Oral Health & Dental Care
Root powder and decoction are classical remedies for toothache, mouth ulcers, and gum infections. Modern studies confirm antimicrobial activity of root extracts against Streptococcus mutans, Candida albicans, and periodontal pathogens, validating its use in traditional tooth powders.
Eye Care (Netra Hita)
Fresh leaf juice diluted and used as eyedrops — a practice recorded in Sushruta — is supported by anti-inflammatory and mild antimicrobial properties. Jasmine water has been used to relieve conjunctival redness, tired eyes, and mild infections across South Asian folk traditions.
Hair Growth & Scalp Health
Jasmine's classical Keshya (hair-promoting) property is expressed in jasmine-infused oils for the scalp — documented antimicrobial effects combat dandruff and scalp infections, while the cooling Sheeta virya soothes scalp inflammation and may support hair follicle health through improved microcirculation.
Antimicrobial & Antipyretic
Methanolic and aqueous extracts of jasmine leaves and roots exhibit broad-spectrum activity against bacteria and fungi. Decoction of the leaves is a classical remedy for fever (jwara) and is used to treat dengue and typhoid-associated fevers in folk medicine across southern India.
Cardiac & Nervous System Tonic
Vagbhata's designation of jasmine as Hridya (heart-pleasing) reflects both its psychological calming effect on cardiac anxiety and mild antioxidant cardioprotective properties. Regular aromatherapy with jasmine oil has been shown to reduce heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and galvanic skin response in stressed subjects.

How to Use Malati

Jasmine is used across an extraordinary range of therapeutic forms — from fresh flower garlands worn in the hair for psychological wellness, to decoctions, infused oils, powders, and steam inhalations. Topical and aromatic uses are generally safe for all; internal preparations of the root and leaf should be used under practitioner guidance.

Safety & Precautions

Jasmine used aromatically and topically in appropriate dilutions is among the safest of all botanical medicines with an exceptional record across millennia of use. Internal use of plant parts is generally well-tolerated at classical doses; the concentrated essential oil is never taken internally. Certain cautions apply.

  • Pregnancy (first two trimesters): Jasmine oil has uterotonic properties — avoid therapeutic massage with jasmine essential oil during the first and second trimester. Aromatic use is generally considered safe in moderation.
  • Essential oil — never ingest: Jasmine absolute and essential oil are highly concentrated and should never be taken orally. Aromatic and topical use (properly diluted in carrier oil) are the appropriate routes.
  • Skin sensitivity: Always dilute jasmine essential oil to 1–2% in a carrier oil before topical application. Patch test first — rare contact dermatitis reactions have been reported, particularly with synthetic jasmine fragrance compounds.
  • Kapha imbalance: Those with significant Kapha aggravation (excess mucus, congestion, lethargy) should use jasmine sparingly internally, as its Guru guna and sweet vipaka may increase Kapha.
  • Children: Jasmine tea and topical use is generally safe; concentrated essential oil should not be used near the face of infants under 2 years.
  • Sedative medications: Jasmine's linalool has mild GABA-A activity — avoid combining high-dose jasmine aromatherapy with strong sedatives, anxiolytics, or anaesthesia without medical advice.
  • Fragrance sensitivity / headaches: Jasmine's intense fragrance can trigger headaches or nausea in fragrance-sensitive individuals — use in well-ventilated spaces and limit diffuser sessions to 30 minutes.
  • Allergic individuals: Those with known Oleaceae allergies (olive pollen, privet) may cross-react with jasmine. Consult an allergist before therapeutic use if suspected.
