Tankana
Borax — The Purifying Salt of the Himalayas
Sodium Tetraborate Decahydrate  ·  Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O

Fig. 1 — Raw Tankana crystals as mined from Himalayan saline deposits  ·  Fig. 2 — Shodhita Tankana after classical purification (calcination with lemon juice or cow's milk)

❧

Known as Tankana Kshara in classical Ayurveda — a mineral salt of ancient repute — Borax was prized by Charaka, Sushruta, and Vagbhata alike as a powerful mucolytic, digestive stimulant, and uterine tonic. Sourced from the saline lake-beds of Tibet and the Himalayas, this white crystalline salt has served Ayurvedic, Unani, and Siddha medicine for millennia, occupying a unique position as both Kshara (alkali mineral) and a critical adjunct in hundreds of classical formulations.

01

Mineral Profile

Tankana is sodium tetraborate decahydrate — a naturally occurring boron mineral found in the dried beds of saline lakes, particularly in Tibet (Tso Kar, Pangong), Central Asia, and parts of California (Death Valley). In its natural state it forms colourless to white monoclinic prismatic crystals that lose water (effloresce) on exposure to dry air, forming a chalky white powder. The name Tankana derives from the Sanskrit root referring to its place of origin — the Tankana region of Tibet — and it is classified in Ayurveda as a Kshara (alkaline mineral) under the sub-category Khanija Kshara (mineral alkali).

Classical texts distinguish two forms: Shodhita Tankana (purified, calcined borax — also called Suhaga) and Ashodhita Tankana (raw borax). The Shodhita form — produced by roasting raw crystals until they swell, lose their water of crystallisation, and become an opaque white powder — is the therapeutically preferred form for internal use and is considered safer and more bioavailable.

02

Chemistry & Composition

Tankana's therapeutic profile is rooted in its boron chemistry. As a borate salt it is both a Lewis acid and a source of reactive borate ions in solution — capable of complexing with polyols, inhibiting certain enzymes, and modulating inflammatory pathways. Upon calcination (Shodhana), the decahydrate loses all 10 molecules of water of crystallisation to form anhydrous sodium tetraborate (Na₂B₄O₇), which is more concentrated and thermally stable.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Tankana is referenced across the foundational classical texts: Charaka Samhita (Kalpasthana), Sushruta Samhita (Kalpa Sthana), Ashtanga Hridayam, Sharangdhara Samhita, Bhavaprakasha Nighantu, and Rasa Ratna Samuchchaya. It is particularly prominent in Rasa Shastra (Ayurvedic alchemy/pharmaceutical science) where it serves as a Samskara Dravya — a processing agent used to purify and prepare mercury, metals, and other minerals by acting as a flux.

Classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha: "Tankanaṃ kṣārasaṃjñaṃ ca śleṣmaghnaṃ kāsanāśanam, dīpanaṃ lekhanaṃ tīkṣṇaṃ yonidoṣāpaham param" — describing Tankana as a Kshara substance that destroys Kapha and cough, is a digestive stimulant (deepana), scraping (lekhana), sharp (tikshna), and supreme in curing disorders of the female reproductive tract (yoni dosha).

04

Key Health Benefits

Tankana's classical indications span respiratory medicine, gynaecology, digestive disorders, dental care, and Rasa Shastra pharmaceutical processing. Modern research has confirmed several of these applications, particularly its anti-fungal, mucolytic, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties.

Mucolytic & Respiratory
Tankana's primary classical application — thinning and expelling tenacious Kapha (mucus) from the respiratory tract. Used in cough (kasa), bronchitis, asthma, whooping cough, and chronic respiratory congestion. Its alkalinity saponifies mucus glycoproteins, making them easier to expectorate. Combined with honey or ginger juice for enhanced effect.
Gynaecological & Uterine
Described as yonidoshapaha param — supreme in uterine disorders. Used internally for dysmenorrhoea, amenorrhoea, and leucorrhoea, and externally as a vaginal douche for infections. Modern research confirms boric acid / borax efficacy against recurrent vaginal candidiasis (supported by clinical trials) and bacterial vaginosis.
Digestive Stimulant (Deepana)
Classified as Deepana (kindles digestive fire) and Pachana (digests Ama). Small doses stimulate gastric secretions and improve appetite in anorexia (aruchi) and indigestion (ajirna). Used in preparations for flatulence, colic (shoola), and abdominal distension due to Kapha.
Dental & Oral Health
A well-documented traditional application: Tankana powder mixed with mustard oil is a classical tooth-cleaning agent for pyorrhoea (periodontitis), toothache, and bad breath. Its alkalinity neutralises oral acid; antimicrobial borates suppress pathogenic oral bacteria. Used in classical Dantamanjana (tooth powder) formulations.
Anti-fungal & Antimicrobial
Borate ions disrupt fungal membrane integrity and inhibit spore germination — the basis for topical and vaginal antifungal applications. Studies confirm activity against Candida albicans, Aspergillus, and dermatophytes. Also active against several gram-positive bacteria and has been used in classical preparations for skin infections and wounds.
Rasa Shastra Processing
Uniquely among Ayurvedic minerals, Tankana's most important role may be as a pharmaceutical adjunct — serving as a flux to reduce the melting point of metals and purify mercury, gold, silver, and other minerals in classical Shodhana and Marana (calcination) processes. Indispensable in the preparation of Rasayana and Rasa preparations.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Boron modulates inflammatory cytokines and has been studied for arthritis and joint pain. Traditional Ayurvedic use includes topical application for joint swelling and muscle pain. Emerging research on dietary boron supplementation shows promise for reducing inflammatory markers in osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.
Fever & Throat Disorders
Used in jwara (fever), tonsillitis, and sore throat both internally (small dose) and as a gargle. Combined with rock salt and warm water as a classical gargle preparation for pharyngitis and laryngitis. Also used as errhine (nasal cleansing agent) to clear upper respiratory mucus in chronic sinusitis.

How to Use Tankana

Tankana is used both internally and externally. Only Shodhita (calcined/purified) Tankana should be used for internal purposes. Raw borax is not suitable for internal consumption due to higher sodium and borate ion concentrations. Internal doses are small — classical texts typically specify 125–500 mg (¼ to ½ Ratti equivalent). Always use under supervision of a qualified Vaidya for internal applications.

Safety & Precautions

Shodhita Tankana at classical doses (125–500 mg) is considered safe and well-tolerated in Ayurvedic practice. However, borax (raw sodium tetraborate) is regulated in many countries — the EU has classified it as a reproductive toxin (Category 1B) at high doses. Internal use of raw borax or high doses of borax solutions is dangerous. Classical Ayurvedic use employs tiny doses of the calcined form, which is fundamentally different from the large-dose exposures studied in toxicological literature.

  • Pregnancy: Strictly contraindicated internally. Tankana is a classical garbhashaya sankochaka (uterine contractor) and has historically been used as an abortifacient. Even topical vaginal use should be avoided in pregnancy.
  • Infants and young children: Do not use internally in children under 5. Borate ions are more slowly excreted in infants; even small doses can cause toxicity. Use under strict specialist supervision only.
  • Kidney disease: Borate is primarily renally excreted. Impaired kidney function significantly reduces excretion capacity — avoid internal use in renal insufficiency or chronic kidney disease.
  • Raw borax vs. Shodhita: Never use raw, uncalcined borax internally. Only classically purified Shodhita Tankana (Phula Tankana) should be consumed, and only at classical doses under supervision.
  • Dose discipline: Internal dose must not exceed 500 mg per day. Excess borate accumulates and can cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, dermatitis, and in severe toxicity — renal and neurological effects.
  • Pitta constitution: Its ushna (hot), tikshna (sharp) qualities aggravate Pitta — avoid in hyperacidity, gastritis, peptic ulcer, or inflammatory Pitta conditions without corrective co-medications.
  • Prolonged use: Not recommended for continuous internal use beyond the prescribed therapeutic period. Periodic breaks and liver/kidney function monitoring are advisable.
  • Drug interactions: No well-documented classical or modern drug interactions, but caution with nephrotoxic drugs, hormonal medications (boron influences oestrogen metabolism), and anti-coagulants.
  • Topical safety: Generally safe topically at concentrations used in Ayurvedic practice. Avoid application to large areas of broken or inflamed skin where absorption is increased. Keep away from eyes.
