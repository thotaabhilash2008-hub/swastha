Maida Lakdi — The Smooth-Barked Spice Tree
Litsea glutinosa  ·  Litsea polyantha Juss.

Fig. 1 — Litsea glutinosa tree habit with characteristic umbellate yellow flower clusters  ·  Fig. 2 — Large elliptic leaves and dark globose drupes of Maida Lakdi

❧

Known in Hindi as Maida Lakdi — "flour wood" — for its pale, smooth bark and starchy inner wood, Litsea glutinosa is a versatile medicinal tree of the Lauraceae family revered across Ayurveda, Siddha, and Unani systems and prized in folk traditions from the Himalayas to Sri Lanka as a demulcent, astringent, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing tree whose bark, leaves, fruit, and seed oil each carry distinct therapeutic virtue.

01

Botanical Profile

Litsea glutinosa (Lour.) C.B. Rob. — synonymous with Litsea polyantha Juss. and Sebifera glutinosa Lour. — is a medium to large evergreen or semi-deciduous tree of the Lauraceae (laurel) family, growing 10–20 m tall with a spreading crown, smooth greyish-brown bark, and a starchy, mucilaginous inner wood that gives the plant its common Hindi name Maida Lakdi. Native to tropical and subtropical Asia — India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Myanmar, China, and Southeast Asia — it thrives in moist deciduous forests, forest margins, river banks, and disturbed habitats up to 1,500 m altitude.

The leaves are alternate, large, elliptic to oblong-lanceolate (10–25 cm), glabrous above and silky-pubescent beneath, with a characteristic aromatic fragrance when crushed. Flowers are small, yellow, and borne in dense umbellate clusters in the leaf axils — characteristic of the Lauraceae. The fruit is a fleshy, globose drupe (1–1.5 cm), dark purple to black at maturity, seated in a shallow cup-like receptacle. The seed contains a rich fixed oil used both medicinally and in soap-making.

02

Phytochemistry

Phytochemical investigations of Litsea glutinosa have revealed a rich array of alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, tannins, mucilaginous polysaccharides, and fixed oils distributed across different plant parts. The bark is exceptionally mucilage-rich — a characteristic that has made it a classical demulcent and binding agent in Unani medicine (Gond Maida). The leaves and roots contain the most pharmacologically potent alkaloid profile, including the cytotoxic and antitumour actinodaphnine and boldine derivatives.

03

Ayurvedic & Classical Significance

In Ayurveda, Litsea glutinosa is classified under Medaskanda Varga — the group of mucilaginous, unctuous drugs — and is mentioned in the Ashtanga Hridayam and Chakradatta. Its Sanskrit name Medaskandha directly references its fatty, mucilage-rich bark. In Unani medicine, the dried bark powder is known as Maida Lakdi and its gum as Gond Maida, both widely used as demulcents and astringents in formulations for diarrhea, dysentery, and leucorrhea.

Classical shloka: "Maedaskandho guru snigdho madhuro balyastaranah, pittasravaharo vrushyo balya vata-kaphapaham" — describing Medaskanda as heavy, unctuous, sweet in taste, a nourisher and strengthener, a coolant for Pitta disorders, aphrodisiac, and pacifier of Vata and Kapha.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has validated many of the classical applications of Litsea glutinosa through studies on bark, leaf, root, and seed extracts — confirming potent anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, antidiarrheal, wound-healing, and hepatoprotective activities.

Antidiarrheal & GI Astringent
The bark's mucilage and tannins coat and soothe the intestinal mucosa while its astringency checks excessive secretion. Classical Unani preparations use Gond Maida (bark gum) and bark powder for diarrhea, dysentery, and irritable bowel — corroborated by studies showing significant inhibition of castor-oil-induced diarrhea in animal models.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Methanolic leaf and bark extracts demonstrate marked anti-inflammatory activity — inhibiting carrageenan-induced paw edema and acetic acid-induced writhing in mice, comparable to standard NSAID controls. The alkaloids actinodaphnine and litsealactone are the primary active agents, supporting traditional use in rheumatism and joint pain.
Wound Healing & Skin Repair
Leaf paste and bark poultice are classical wound remedies across tribal medicine systems of India. Studies confirm that extracts significantly accelerate epithelialisation and collagen synthesis in excision wound models. The mucilage forms a protective film that keeps wounds moist, while tannins prevent microbial colonisation.
Hepatoprotective
Boldine — a major bark alkaloid — is one of the most potent natural hepatoprotective agents known, reducing liver enzyme levels and oxidative stress markers in CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity models. Supports classical use of Maida Lakdi bark in jaundice and liver disorders across folk traditions of Bengal and Odisha.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Leaf, bark, and root extracts exhibit broad-spectrum activity against S. aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella, Candida albicans, and dermatophytic fungi. The butenolide lactones litseakolide A and B are particularly active against drug-resistant fungal strains — supporting traditional application of bark paste to skin infections and ringworm.
Leucorrhea & Gynaecological Support
One of the most prominent classical and folk uses — Gond Maida and bark decoctions are prescribed for leucorrhea (white discharge) in both Ayurveda and Unani systems, acting through astringent reduction of excess vaginal secretions and antimicrobial control of opportunistic pathogens responsible for abnormal discharge.
Antioxidant & Anti-ageing
High phenolic and flavonoid content (quercetin, kaempferol, gallic acid, boldine) confers strong free-radical scavenging capacity demonstrated across DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays. Bark and leaf extracts show significant protective activity against lipid peroxidation — relevant to both systemic and topical anti-ageing applications.
Antitumour Potential
Actinodaphnine and betulinic acid from bark and leaf extracts demonstrate cytotoxic activity against multiple human cancer cell lines (HeLa, MCF-7, A549) in vitro. While human clinical data is preliminary, the traditional use of Maida Lakdi in tumours and growths in Unani medicine has a phytochemical basis warranting further investigation.

Traditional & Ethnobotanical Uses

Across more than 30 tribal and folk communities of India, Litsea glutinosa is among the most versatile ethnomedicinal trees. The bark mucilage has been used as a binding agent in clay pottery, a natural glue for bamboo crafts, a hair fixative, and an oral demulcent — reflecting a sophisticated indigenous understanding of its physical and chemical properties that predates modern phytochemistry by centuries.

How to Use Maida Lakdi

Maida Lakdi is used internally and externally; the bark is the most commonly used part. Internal preparations should be taken under the guidance of a qualified Ayurvedic or Unani practitioner. The mucilaginous bark gum (Gond Maida) is widely sold in Indian herbal markets and is perhaps the most accessible form for domestic use.

Safety & Precautions

Maida Lakdi is generally considered safe when used at classical doses and durations. Unlike Adhapushpi, it does not contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids. However, its alkaloid profile warrants caution in certain populations, and the heavy, unctuous nature of the bark gum may not suit all digestive constitutions.

  • Pregnancy: Consult a qualified practitioner before use. Though traditionally used for uterine conditions, internal use in pregnancy should be supervised.
  • Heavy constitution / Kapha excess: Its guru (heavy) and snigdha (unctuous) nature may aggravate Kapha in constitutionally heavy individuals — avoid prolonged use if prone to mucus, obesity, or lethargy.
  • Digestive weakness: High doses of bark gum may cause bloating or constipation in individuals with weak digestive fire (mandagni) — use with digestives like dry ginger if needed.
  • Alkaloid sensitivity: Individuals sensitive to Lauraceae alkaloids (same family as cinnamon, camphor) should start with small doses and monitor for any allergic response.
  • Drug interactions: Being an astringent, bark preparations may reduce absorption of concurrently taken oral medications — maintain a gap of at least 2 hours.
  • Hepatic conditions: While boldine is hepatoprotective at therapeutic doses, it may have different effects at high doses — use under supervision in existing liver disease.
  • Topical: Fresh bark and leaf paste is generally safe topically. Those with sensitive skin should patch-test, as the aromatic compounds in Lauraceae can occasionally cause contact dermatitis.
  • Children: Small doses of bark decoction or Gond Maida in milk have traditional use in paediatric diarrhea, but should be given only under qualified supervision.
