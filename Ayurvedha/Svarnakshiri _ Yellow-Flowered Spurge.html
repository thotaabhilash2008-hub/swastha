Svarnakshiri
Yellow-Flowered Spurge — The Golden Milk Herb
Euphorbia hirta L.  ·  Euphorbiaceae

Fig. 1 — Euphorbia hirta in its characteristic prostrate habitat — a wayside herb of extraordinary therapeutic reach  ·  Fig. 2 — The milky latex (Kshira) that gives Svarnakshiri its name, exuding from a broken stem

❧

Its Sanskrit name declares its nature: Svarna — gold, and Kshiri — milky, yielding latex. This small, hairy, prostrate weed of roadsides and wastelands carries within it one of the most pharmacologically rich profiles of any common herb of the Indian plains. Revered in classical Ayurveda for respiratory, urogenital, and galactagogue applications, Svarnakshiri has earned modern scientific attention for its antiasthmatic, antimicrobial, antidiarrhoeal, and anxiolytic properties — a herb whose modesty of appearance belies immense medicinal depth.

01

Botanical Profile

Euphorbia hirta L. (syn. Chamaesyce hirta) is a small, annual, hairy herb of the vast family Euphorbiaceae — cousins to the rubber tree, castor, and poinsettia. It grows prostrate to semi-erect, rarely exceeding 40 cm, with reddish-brown stems densely covered in stiff yellow hairs. Leaves are opposite, ovate to lanceolate, finely serrate, often with a red-purple blotch in the centre — a characteristic that aids field identification. Stems and leaves exude a white milky latex when broken.

The flowers are tiny — borne in dense axillary clusters of characteristic Euphorbiaceae cyathia (cup-shaped pseudoflowers comprising a single female flower surrounded by male flowers within a cup of bracts). The fruit is a three-lobed capsule containing small seeds. The plant is pantropical — native to the Americas but now thoroughly naturalised across India, Southeast Asia, Africa, and Australia, found thriving in disturbed soils, roadsides, paddy field margins, and gardens throughout Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu.

A closely related species, Euphorbia antiquorum L. (Sehunda / Tridhara), is the taller, triangular-stemmed succulent Euphorbia sometimes conflated with Svarnakshiri in folk practice — but the two are pharmacologically distinct. Classical texts primarily reference E. hirta and related small-leafed milky Euphorbias under the Kshirini and Dugdhika complex.

02

Phytochemistry

Phytochemical investigation of E. hirta has revealed an extraordinarily complex secondary metabolite profile spanning terpenoids, flavonoids, tannins, alkaloids, and the characteristic Euphorbiaceae diterpene esters. Over 130 compounds have been identified across different plant parts, with the whole-plant methanolic extract showing the broadest spectrum of activity. The latex fraction — most visually striking — contains the most irritant compounds but also potent antiviral and wound-healing agents.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Svarnakshiri and its close relatives in the Kshirini complex are cited across multiple classical Nighantu texts. Dhanvantari Nighantu includes it in the Guduchyadi Varga; Raja Nighantu in the Parpataadi Varga; Kaiyadeva Nighantu in the Aushadhi Varga. It is recognised in the Brihat Trayi (three great classical compilations) under the Kshiravriksha group — plants that yield latex resembling milk and carry associated galactagogue, wound-healing, and Pitta-modulating properties.

Classical description: "Svarnakshiri tikta kashaya cha, kaphavatahara shwasanashani, krimighni cha shodhanee, vranahara prashasyate" — "Svarnakshiri, bitter and astringent, pacifies Kapha and Vata, destroys worms, purifies the body, relieves asthma, and is praised for healing wounds." The latex (Kshira) is separately categorised — used topically in classical practice for warts, ringworm, and opacities of the cornea.

04

Key Health Benefits

Antiasthmatic & Bronchodilatory
The plant's most celebrated and best-validated application — hence its common name "Asthma Weed." Quercitrin and myricitrin relax bronchial smooth muscle via inhibition of phosphodiesterase and histamine release. Whole-plant decoction significantly reduces bronchospasm frequency in clinical folk use; animal studies confirm bronchodilatory activity comparable to theophylline at extract doses.
Antidiarrhoeal & Gut Antimicrobial
Among the most consistent findings across in vitro and in vivo studies. Tannins (euphorbins, geraniin) are strongly astringent and antimicrobial against E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, and Vibrio cholerae — the primary pathogens of infectious diarrhoea. Quercitrin reduces intestinal motility and secretion. Classical whole-plant juice or decoction remains a frontline folk remedy for acute diarrhoea across tropical India.
Galactagogue (Milk Promoter)
One of the most distinctive and historically prized applications — Svarnakshiri as a promoter of breast milk secretion in lactating mothers. Choline and phytosterol constituents are proposed to act on prolactin signalling. Classical Ayurvedic texts classify it among Stanya Janana (milk-promoting) drugs; the folk practice of feeding a whole-plant decoction to nursing mothers persists across rural Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh.
Anxiolytic & Sedative
Animal studies using methanolic and aqueous extracts demonstrate significant anxiolytic effects in the elevated plus maze, open field, and forced swim tests — comparable to diazepam at higher doses. Myricetin and myricitrin modulate GABA-A receptor activity. Classical Vata-pacifying indication for nervine restlessness, insomnia, and anxiety-related palpitations aligns with this documented pharmacology.
Antimicrobial & Antiviral
Euphorbins A–D are among the most potent plant-derived inhibitors of HIV reverse transcriptase identified in natural product screening. Broad-spectrum antibacterial activity is confirmed against Staphylococcus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, and oral pathogens. Antifungal activity against dermatophytes makes the leaf paste a rational folk remedy for ringworm and tinea infections.
Wound Healing & Dermatological
Lupeol, β-amyrin, and tannins together accelerate wound contraction and re-epithelialisation in excision wound models. The latex — applied with extreme caution only to intact callus, warts, or verrucae — acts as a keratolytic and vesicant. Classical Ayurvedic use of the plant paste for skin infections, eczema, and fungal conditions is supported by antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory evidence.
Antiparasitic & Anthelmintic
Classical krimighna (worm-destroying) classification is pharmacologically validated — whole-plant extracts show activity against intestinal helminths including Ascaris and Strongyloides in animal models. The plant is used across Unani, Siddha, and folk traditions of South India for intestinal worm infestations in children, typically as a fresh-plant juice with jaggery as palatability aid.
Diuretic & Urogenital Support
Significant diuretic activity documented in animal models with increased sodium and potassium excretion. Classical use in dysuria (burning urination), urethritis, and gonorrhoea-related urethral discharge is supported by the combination of diuretic, antimicrobial, and astringent tannin activity. Root decoction is the classical preparation of choice for urogenital conditions.
05

The Latex — A Special Note

The white milky latex of Svarnakshiri — its most visually distinctive feature and the source of its Sanskrit name — deserves separate consideration. In classical Ayurveda, the latex (Kshira) is regarded as a potent topical agent: applied to warts (charmakeela), corns, ringworm, and — with extreme caution — to opacities of the cornea. It was never recommended for internal use.

⚠ LATEX CAUTION
The latex of Euphorbia hirta and all Euphorbia species contains diterpene esters (euphorbol, ingenol derivatives) that are potent skin and mucous membrane irritants. Direct contact causes inflammation, blistering, and chemical conjunctivitis if it reaches the eyes — corneal damage has been documented. Never apply latex near eyes, on broken or inflamed skin, or allow contact with mucous membranes. Wash hands thoroughly after any contact with the broken plant. Internal consumption of the latex is contraindicated. Topical therapeutic use of latex should only be performed under the guidance of a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner.
06

How to Consume Svarnakshiri

Internal preparations use the whole plant excluding the latex — the fresh juice, decoction, or powder of aerial parts. Given the irritant potential of raw plant compounds at high doses, classical prescriptions are conservative in dose and typically combined with soothing anupanas (honey, ghee, jaggery). All internal therapeutic use should be under the guidance of a qualified practitioner.

07

Safety & Precautions

Svarnakshiri requires more careful handling than most herbs in this garden — primarily owing to its latex, which is a potent irritant. The whole plant at classical internal doses is generally well-tolerated, but several important contraindications apply.

  • Latex — eye contact: The single most critical safety concern. Euphorbia latex in or near the eyes causes severe chemical conjunctivitis, corneal ulceration, and — in documented cases — permanent vision loss. If contact occurs, irrigate immediately with large volumes of water and seek emergency medical care.
  • Latex — skin irritation: The latex is a potent vesicant. On sensitive skin it causes blistering, erythema, and contact dermatitis. Never apply to mucous membranes, broken skin, facial skin, or in skin folds. Wash hands immediately after plant contact.
  • Pregnancy: Strictly contraindicated internally. Classical texts list uterotonic and abortifacient properties; diterpene esters at higher exposures can stimulate uterine contractions. Topical leaf paste on extremities is generally considered low-risk but should be avoided as a precaution.
  • Infants under 1 year: Internal preparations not recommended without specific physician direction; tannin load may cause constipation or GI irritation in very young infants.
  • Pitta-excess constitutions: Ushna virya (hot potency) combined with irritant diterpenes may aggravate hyperacidity, peptic ulcers, or inflammatory bowel conditions — avoid in active flares; use with soothing anupana (ghee, coconut milk) when prescribed.
  • Duration of internal use: Limit continuous internal use to 2–4 weeks. The tannin and diterpene load of prolonged high-dose consumption may cause GI irritation or hepatic stress. Follow with a rest period.
  • Confusing with other Euphorbias: Many Euphorbia species look superficially similar but vary significantly in toxicity — E. tirucalli (pencil cactus) and E. antiquorum are far more toxic. Ensure correct botanical identification before use; when in doubt, consult a herbalist or Ayurvedic practitioner with the plant in hand.
  • Drug interactions: Diuretic activity may potentiate antihypertensives and diuretic medications; antidiarrhoeal astringent action may reduce absorption of oral medications taken simultaneously — separate by at least 2 hours.
