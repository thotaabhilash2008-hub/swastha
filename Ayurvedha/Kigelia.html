Kigelia
The Sausage Tree — Mti wa Sosej
Kigelia africana (Lam.) Benth.  ·  Bignoniaceae

Fig. 1 — Enormous fibrous fruits (1–2 kg) dangling on cord-like stalks — the hallmark of Kigelia africana  ·  Fig. 2 — Deep crimson, bat-pollinated flowers opening at night in long pendulous racemes

❧

Known across sub-Saharan Africa as the Sausage Tree for its extraordinary elongated fruits — sometimes exceeding 60 cm and weighing up to 10 kg — Kigelia africana is one of Africa's most revered medicinal trees, used for centuries by over 30 ethnic groups to treat skin conditions, tumours, venereal diseases, rheumatism, and post-partum ailments, and now generating significant pharmaceutical interest for its potent phytochemical arsenal.

01

Botanical Profile

Kigelia africana is a large, spreading, semi-evergreen tree of the Bignoniaceae family — relatives of jacaranda and trumpet vine — native to tropical sub-Saharan Africa from Senegal and Sudan south through the Congo basin to South Africa, and westward to Madagascar. Trees typically grow 15–25 m tall with broad, spreading crowns and rough, grey-brown bark that flakes in irregular patches on mature specimens.

The pinnately compound leaves bear 7–9 leaflets, leathery and dark green, with serrate margins. Flowers are large, tubular, and deep velvet-crimson to maroon — borne in pendulous racemes up to 2 m long and pollinated at night by bats and hawk-moths attracted to the musky scent. The fruit — the defining characteristic — is an enormous, woody, elongated berry, grey-brown, indehiscent, and fibrous inside, hanging individually on long cord-like stalks and persisting on the tree for months. It is inedible raw but prepared and consumed in various traditional contexts.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Kigelia africana is extraordinarily rich in biologically active secondary metabolites — its fruit, bark, and leaves yielding a complex mixture of iridoids, naphthoquinones, coumarins, flavonoids, sterols, and terpenoids. The iridoids (particularly specioside, verminoside, and minecoside) and the naphthoquinone kigelinone are considered the primary bioactive drivers of its anti-tumour, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activity.

Ethnobotanical Traditions

Few African trees match the breadth of Kigelia's ethnomedicinal use. Across more than 30 documented ethnic groups — from the Zulu and Sotho of Southern Africa to the Luo of East Africa and the Bété of West Africa — virtually every part of the tree finds medicinal application, with the fruit occupying a position of special significance in skin, gynaecological, and oncological treatments.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological investigation — particularly over the past two decades — has validated a remarkable proportion of Kigelia's traditional claims, with especially strong evidence for its anti-tumour, skin-therapeutic, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activity. Extracts of the fruit are now incorporated into commercial skincare products worldwide.

Anti-tumour & Anti-cancer
Iridoid glycosides — particularly specioside and verminoside — and the naphthoquinone kigelinone have demonstrated cytotoxic activity against multiple cancer cell lines including melanoma, breast, liver, and lung carcinoma in vitro. The National Cancer Institute (USA) has identified Kigelia extracts as having significant anti-melanoma potential.
Skin Therapy & Dermatology
Kigelia fruit extract is clinically used in topical formulations for eczema, psoriasis, acne, and photo-damaged skin. Its compound kigelin inhibits melanin synthesis, giving skin-brightening effects. Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions reduce erythema, oxidative damage, and skin ageing markers. Several cosmeceutical brands now feature standardised Kigelia extract.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Bark and fruit extracts show strong inhibitory activity against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Candida albicans, Trichophyton, Cryptococcus, and Mycobacterium species. Naphthoquinones (pinnatal, isopinnatal) are primary agents — particularly relevant for drug-resistant dermatophyte infections and tropical fungal conditions.
Antimalarial
Lapachol, isopinnatal, and pinnatal derived from Kigelia bark and fruit show significant activity against Plasmodium falciparum in vitro, corroborating widespread traditional use for malaria fever across East and Central Africa. Activity is comparable to quinine at tested concentrations in several studies.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Ethanolic and aqueous extracts significantly inhibit COX-2 and pro-inflammatory cytokines in animal models, validating topical application to arthritic joints and inflamed tissue. Oleanolic and ursolic acids are the principal contributors to systemic anti-inflammatory activity alongside the iridoid fraction.
Gynaecological Support
Post-partum use across East Africa for uterine recovery and contraction is supported by documented uterotonic activity. Traditionally also used for dysmenorrhea (painful periods) and menopausal symptoms — though clinical human data on these applications remains preliminary.
Antioxidant & Photoprotection
Ferulic acid, caffeic acid, quercetin, and luteolin in the fruit provide substantial free-radical scavenging capacity. DPPH and ABTS assay results indicate photoprotective potential — Kigelia extracts are incorporated into UV-protective sunscreens and after-sun formulas by multiple cosmeceutical manufacturers.
Antidiarrheal & Digestive
Bark decoctions are widely used for dysentery, diarrhea, and intestinal infections across West and Central Africa. Tannin-mediated astringency combined with demonstrated antimicrobial activity against enteric pathogens (Salmonella, E. coli, Shigella) provides a pharmacological basis for this application.
05

How Kigelia Is Used

Kigelia is predominantly used topically for skin conditions, tumours, and joint ailments. Internal use — particularly of raw fruit — carries known risks and should never be self-administered. Commercially standardised Kigelia extracts in skincare products represent the safest modern-day access route for most users. Traditional internal preparations are always under supervision and often involve roasting or fermenting to reduce toxic constituents.

Safety & Precautions

Raw, unprocessed Kigelia fruit is considered toxic when consumed internally in significant quantities — traditional use always involves roasting, drying, or fermenting the fruit before any internal application. Topical use of standardised commercial extracts is generally safe and well-tolerated. Internal preparations from bark, leaf, or root require qualified traditional or medical supervision.

  • Raw fruit — never eat: Fresh, unprocessed Kigelia fruit is toxic if consumed. Internal use always requires roasting, fermentation, or controlled extraction to reduce harmful constituents.
  • Pregnancy: Strictly contraindicated internally. Uterotonic activity documented — internal use during pregnancy carries risk of miscarriage or premature labour.
  • Topical patch test: Although generally well tolerated, Kigelia's phenolic content can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals. Always patch-test commercial extracts before full-face application.
  • Drug interactions: Potential interaction with anticoagulants and immunosuppressants — the naphthoquinone fraction may affect platelet function. Discuss with a physician if on these medications.
  • Photo-sensitisation: Some furanocoumarins in the plant may increase UV sensitivity — avoid strong sun exposure immediately after applying non-standardised topical preparations.
  • Muratina beer: Fermented Kigelia beverages (traditional Kikuyu muratina) should be understood as culturally specific preparations — not reproduced without proper traditional knowledge guidance.
  • Children (internal use): Internal preparations are not suitable for children without specialist traditional medicine supervision. Topical cosmeceutical extracts are generally considered safe.
  • Breast enlargement claims: Traditional use for breast enhancement lacks clinical evidence. Commercial products claiming this benefit based on Kigelia should be treated with scepticism absent rigorous trials.
