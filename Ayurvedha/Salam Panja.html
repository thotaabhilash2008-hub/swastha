Salam Panja
The Five-Fingered Orchid — Himalayan Hand of Healing
Dactylorhiza hatagirea (D.Don) Soó  ·  Orchidaceae

Fig. 1 — The distinctive palmate tuber of Salam Panja with its five finger-like lobes, resembling a human hand — the source of its name and its primary medicinal part  ·  Fig. 2 — Flowering spike of Dactylorhiza hatagirea in an alpine Himalayan meadow, 3,000–4,500 m above sea level

❧

Named for its most arresting feature — a palmate tuber with five spreading lobes shaped unmistakably like an open human hand (panja = hand/fist in Urdu/Hindi) — Salam Panja is one of the Himalayas' most revered, rarest, and most threatened medicinal orchids. Harvested from alpine meadows above 3,000 metres across Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Nepal, and Bhutan, it is the source of Salep — a prized tonic that has nourished royal courts, sustained soldiers on mountain campaigns, and anchored the folk medicine of three great Asian healing traditions for centuries.

⚠
Conservation Status — Endangered
Dactylorhiza hatagirea is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List and is protected under Schedule VI of the Indian Wildlife Protection Act (1972), making its collection from the wild a criminal offence in India. It is also listed on CITES Appendix II, restricting international trade. Decades of over-harvesting for medicinal trade — the tubers are extracted before the plant can set seed — have caused dramatic population declines across its Himalayan range. Only cultivated or legally sourced material should be used. When purchasing Salep or Salam Panja products, verify their origin and legality.
01

Botanical Profile

Dactylorhiza hatagirea (D.Don) Soó is a terrestrial orchid of the family Orchidaceae, native to the alpine and subalpine meadows, moist grasslands, and open slopes of the Himalayas and Hindu Kush ranges at elevations of 2,500–4,800 m. The genus name Dactylorhiza derives from the Greek daktylos (finger) and rhiza (root) — a direct reference to the palmate, finger-lobed tubers that define the genus and give Salam Panja its common name.

The plant grows 15–45 cm tall with 3–6 oblong-lanceolate, sometimes spotted leaves sheathing an erect stem. The flowering spike bears a dense raceme of 10–30 pink to purple, occasionally white, orchid blooms with a three-lobed lip and prominent nectar spur — flowering from May to August depending on altitude. The subterranean part consists of two palm-shaped (palmate) tubers — the current season's plump, fresh tuber and the previous year's shrunken, spent one. It is the fresh tubers, harvested before or just after flowering, that are dried and traded as Salam Panja / Salep. A single plant produces only 2 tubers; since collection destroys the plant, unregulated wildcrafting is devastatingly non-sustainable.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

The dried tubers of Salam Panja have a distinctive, remarkably mucilaginous composition dominated by glucomannans — long-chain polysaccharides that form a thick, viscous gel when hydrated. This mucilage constitutes 48–55% of the dry weight and is responsible for the tuber's classical nutritive, demulcent, and tonic properties. Beyond the polysaccharides, the tubers contain a range of phenanthrene derivatives (dactylorhin A and B, militarine), flavonoids, amino acids, and trace minerals that underpin its antioxidant and adaptogenic activities.

03

Three Traditions of Healing

Salam Panja occupies the rare position of being a cornerstone medicine in three distinct Asian healing traditions — Ayurveda, Unani (Greco-Islamic), and Tibetan medicine — each assigning it to the highest tier of tonic and restorative herbs. Its identity under different traditional names and its therapeutic emphases vary subtly across these traditions, but its nutritive, aphrodisiac, and adaptogenic core is unanimously shared.

Ayurveda
Munjalaka / Riddhi
Classified as a Rasayana and Vajeekarana herb; the tuber is among the classical Ashtavarga (group of eight prized Himalayan herbs) used in high-potency rejuvenative preparations like Chyawanprash. Properties: Madhura rasa, Sheeta virya, Guru-Snigdha guna — nourishing all seven dhatus, especially Shukra dhatu and Ojas. Indicated in debility, emaciation (Shosha), and general Vata disorders.
Unani — Greco-Islamic
Salab Panja / Khasiyul-Tha'lab
One of Unani medicine's foremost aphrodisiacs (Muqawwi-e-Bah) and general tonics (Muqawwi). Ibn Sina (Avicenna) described Salep orchid tubers in the Canon of Medicine as strengthening to the body and useful in sexual debility. The famous Turkish hot drink Sahlep descends directly from this tradition. Mizaj (temperament): Hot and Moist in second degree — warming, nourishing, semen-promoting.
Tibetan Medicine
Wang-lag / dBang-lag
In the Gyushi (Four Tantras), Salam Panja appears as dBang-lag — used in strengthening formulas for rLung (wind/Vata) disorders, wasting diseases, and male reproductive weakness. Combined with other high-altitude Himalayan herbs in precious pill formulations. The palm shape of the tuber is associated with the five fingers of the Medicine Buddha's healing hand in Tibetan iconography.
Ottoman — Levantine
Sahlep / Salep
The dried powder of Dactylorhiza and allied Orchis species was carried westward along trade routes to become Sahlep — once the most popular hot beverage of the Ottoman Empire, preceding coffee, consumed from Istanbul to Cairo for warmth, digestion, and virility. The traditional Turkish dondurma (stretchy ice cream) owes its unique elastic texture to Salep's glucomannan polysaccharides. Sahlep is still sold as a warming drink across Turkey, Lebanon, and Greece.
04

Ayurvedic Significance

In Ayurveda, Salam Panja is identified with the Ashtavarga group — the eight most prized Himalayan herbs cited in the Charaka Samhita and used in the formulation of Ashtavarga Kwatha and classical Chyawanprash. The Ashtavarga herbs are: Jivaka, Rishabhaka, Meda, Mahameda, Kakoli, Kshirakakoli, Riddhi, and Vriddhhi — all believed to be high-altitude orchid or liliaceous tubers, most of which have been lost, substituted, or remain botanically disputed. Salam Panja (D. hatagirea) is the most commonly accepted botanical source for several of these Ashtavarga names, particularly Riddhi and Vriddhidaru.

Classical description: "Riddhi madhura sheeta cha balyaa shukrala rasayana, vrishya jivaniya snigdha guru vatapittaghni uttama" — Sweet, cooling, strength-giving, semen-promoting, rejuvenating, aphrodisiac, life-sustaining, unctuous, heavy, supreme in pacifying Vata and Pitta.

05

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological investigation of Dactylorhiza hatagirea and allied Salep orchids has confirmed their classical nutritive, adaptogenic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties — concentrated in the glucomannan polysaccharide fraction and the phenanthrene glycosides unique to the Orchidaceae.

Adaptogenic & Anti-fatigue
Glucomannans and phenanthrene glycosides (militarine, dactylorhin A) confer significant anti-fatigue and stress-adaptation activity in rodent models — increasing swim endurance, reducing serum lactate, and restoring adrenal cortisol rhythms. Classically, Salep preparations were given to soldiers, travellers, and pilgrims making high-altitude journeys as an endurance-sustaining food-medicine.
Nutritive Tonic & Wasting Diseases
Its extraordinarily high mucilage and starch content makes Salam Panja one of the most easily digestible and calorie-dense medicinal foods — ideal for patients with severe debility, post-illness wasting (Shosha), post-operative recovery, and paediatric malnutrition. A single cup of Salep drink provides highly bioavailable carbohydrates, proteins, and minerals in a soothing, easily absorbed form.
Aphrodisiac & Male Reproductive
The most consistently documented classical use across all three traditions — Salam Panja's Shukrala (semen-promoting) activity is attributed to its steroidal precursor phytosterols and its Ojas-building Rasayana properties. Animal studies show improved sperm count, motility, and testosterone levels with Salep extract supplementation. Used in oligospermia, erectile dysfunction, and premature ejaculation in Unani and Ayurvedic clinical practice.
Gut Demulcent & Anti-ulcer
The glucomannan mucilage forms a thick, protective gel layer on the gastric and intestinal mucosa — physically coating and soothing inflamed, ulcerated, or irritated surfaces. Used classically in gastritis, peptic ulcer, gastroenteritis, and inflammatory bowel disease. Modern studies confirm that Salep polysaccharides significantly reduce ethanol- and indomethacin-induced gastric ulceration in animal models.
Prebiotic & Gut Microbiome
Salep glucomannans are non-digestible by human enzymes and reach the colon intact, where they selectively stimulate the growth of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species. This prebiotic activity supports a healthy gut microbiome, reduces pathogen colonisation, improves bowel regularity, and enhances immune function — a modern scientific dimension to the classical use of Salep in gastroenteritis and chronic bowel disorders.
Neuroprotective & Anti-depressant
Militarine and dactylorhin B demonstrate neuroprotective activity against oxidative stress in neuronal cell lines, with militarine showing antidepressant-like effects via monoaminergic pathway modulation in forced swim and tail suspension tests. Consistent with classical use of Salep as a Medhya (mind-nourishing) and Manasa Rasayana (mental rejuvenative) in Ayurveda and Tibetan medicine for mental exhaustion and anxiety.
Antioxidant & Anti-inflammatory
Dactylorhin A and B and quercetin from the tubers show strong DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP radical-scavenging activity. Anti-inflammatory action via COX inhibition and pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression has been confirmed in LPS-stimulated macrophage models. These properties support the classical use of Salam Panja in fever, joint inflammation, and wasting inflammatory conditions.
Demulcent for Respiratory Tract
The mucilage-rich Salep drink coats and soothes inflamed mucous membranes of the throat and respiratory tract — used classically in dry cough, chronic bronchitis, and pharyngitis. Its warm preparation (hot Sahlep drink) is used in Turkish tradition specifically for cold, sore throat, and winter respiratory ailments — a demulcent mechanism analogous to honey-ginger preparations in other traditions.

How to Use Salam Panja

Salam Panja is almost exclusively used as the dried, powdered tuber — consumed as a warm drink (Salep / Sahlep), incorporated into milk preparations, or included in classical compound formulas. It is one of the few medicinal substances that is simultaneously a food and a medicine — the glucomannan content makes it remarkably nourishing in small quantities. Only legally sourced, cultivated material should be used.

Safety & Precautions

Salam Panja has an outstanding safety record across centuries of food and medicinal use in multiple cultures — the dried, cooked tuber powder is essentially a food-grade nutritive substance with very low acute toxicity. The primary concerns are its heavy quality in Kapha conditions, its oxalic acid content in raw form, and above all, the critical legal and ethical obligation to use only sustainably sourced material.

  • Legal obligation — wildcrafted material: Collection of Dactylorhiza hatagirea from the wild is a criminal offence in India under Schedule VI of the Wildlife Protection Act. Do not purchase wildcrafted Salam Panja. Verify that any product is cultivated or derived from legal ex-situ sources. This is both an ethical and legal imperative.
  • Kapha constitution / Obesity: Its heavy, unctuous, sweet properties may aggravate Kapha — potentially worsening weight gain, mucus congestion, sluggish digestion, and oedema. Use alongside digestive spices (cardamom, cinnamon, ginger) in Kapha-dominant individuals.
  • Diabetes & blood sugar: Salep's high carbohydrate content (starch + glucomannans) requires careful monitoring in diabetic patients — glucomannans themselves can improve glycaemic control, but total carbohydrate load should be factored into diet planning. Consult a physician before use in diabetes.
  • Kidney stones — oxalic acid: Raw tubers contain oxalic acid — always consume dried, cooked, or properly processed powder, never raw. Individuals with a history of calcium oxalate kidney stones should use cautiously and ensure adequate hydration.
  • Pregnancy: Generally considered safe at food doses in classical texts as a nutritive. However, therapeutic doses during pregnancy should be supervised by a qualified Ayurvedic or Unani practitioner.
  • Adulteration and substitution: The scarcity and high value of genuine Salam Panja makes it one of the most frequently adulterated herbal products — commonly substituted with other starchy tubers, cheaper Orchis species, or synthetic thickeners. Purchase only from authenticated, reputable suppliers with third-party testing.
  • Drug interactions: The thick mucilage may slow the absorption of simultaneously administered oral medications — maintain at least a 2-hour gap between Salep preparations and prescription medicines.
