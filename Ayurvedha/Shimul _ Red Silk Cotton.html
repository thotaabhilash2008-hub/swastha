Shimul
Red Silk Cotton — Shalmali, the Forest of Fire
Bombax ceiba L.  ·  Family: Malvaceae (Bombacaceae)

Fig. 1 — Blazing scarlet blooms of Bombax ceiba erupting from bare thorned branches in late winter  ·  Fig. 2 — Conical trunk thorns and bursting seed pods releasing white silk cotton

❧

Called Shalmali in Sanskrit — from the root meaning "the tree that shelters" — Bombax ceiba ignites the late-winter landscape with an extraordinary spectacle: enormous scarlet flowers blazing from completely bare, thorn-armoured branches, leafless and unapologetic, like fire held aloft on spears. Revered across Ayurveda, Unani, and tribal medicine from the Himalayas to Bengal, from Gujarat to Tamil Nadu, every part of this towering tree — thorn, root, bark, flower, gum, seed — is a medicine, making Shalmali one of the most comprehensively utilised medicinal trees of the Indian subcontinent.

01

Botanical Profile

Bombax ceiba L. (syn. Salmalia malabarica, Bombax malabaricum) is a large, deciduous tree of the family Malvaceae, formerly placed in Bombacaceae, capable of reaching 30–40 m in height with a straight, cylindrical trunk that may exceed 3 m in girth at maturity. The trunk and younger branches are densely armed with stout, pyramidal, woody thorns — one of the tree's most distinctive and symbolic features. The bark is grey-green, becoming rough and fissured with age.

The leaves are large, palmate, and compound — five to seven leaflets radiating from a single stalk like an open hand — and are shed entirely in November–December before flowering. The flowers appear January to March on completely bare branches: 8–12 cm across, with five thick, fleshy, vivid scarlet petals and a dense brush of prominent stamens. After flowering, large, oblong capsules (10–15 cm) develop, splitting into five valves to release the seeds embedded in white, lustrous silk cotton (kapok) — the plant's second great gift to human civilisation. The tree grows across tropical Asia, northern Australia, and is naturalised widely across the tropics.

02

Phytochemical Composition

Bombax ceiba is a phytochemically rich tree across all its organs. The root bark is the most extensively studied, yielding a complex array of sterols, fatty acids, alkaloids, and phenolics. The gum resin — Mocharas or Semul gum — is among the most prized Ayurvedic astringents, containing tannins and polysaccharides. The flowers yield flavonoids and anthocyanins responsible for their vivid scarlet pigmentation, while the thorns contain silica and flavonoids with wound-healing properties. The seed oil is rich in palmitic, oleic, and linoleic acids.

03

Ayurvedic Significance

Shalmali occupies a distinguished place across all the major classical Ayurvedic compendia. Charaka's Sutrasthana lists it among the ten trees of the Nyagrodhadi Gana — the group of astringent bark trees used to promote healing, strengthen tissues, and arrest discharges. Sushruta classifies it in Sleshmatakaadi Gana and details its specific surgical applications — noting the thorn paste for wound closure and the gum as a haemostatic. The Ashtanga Hridayam places its root in formulations for Shukrala (semen augmentation) and Vajeekarana (virilification).

The gum resin — Mocharas — is catalogued as a separate drug entry in virtually every Nighantu, valued as one of the finest naturally occurring astringents. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu describes Shalmali under Vatadi Varga, noting its tridosha-balancing properties with special affinity for Pitta and Kapha. The classical shloka notes: "Shalmali grahi sheetala raktapittaharani" — Shalmali is absorbent, cooling, and destroys disorders of Rakta (blood) and Pitta.

04

Key Health Benefits

Modern pharmacological research has validated Shalmali's classical profile across multiple organ systems — from its anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective root sterols to the haemostatic gum, aphrodisiac root, antioxidant flowers, and wound-healing thorns. It is one of the few trees where every morphological part — including the thorn — is medicinally documented.

Haemostatic & Anti-haemorrhagic
Mocharas (Shimul gum) is one of Ayurveda's premier haemostatics — used internally for bleeding from any orifice: haematemesis, haematuria, metrorrhagia, rectal bleeding, and haemoptysis. Its tannin-rich astringency contracts mucosal vessels and promotes rapid clot formation. Validated in multiple in vivo studies showing significantly reduced bleeding and clotting times.
Reproductive Tonic & Aphrodisiac
The root is Ayurveda's classical Shukrala — augmenting semen quality and quantity — and a premier Vajeekarana (virilification) herb. Bombacin and β-sitosterol in the root support testosterone modulation, spermatogenesis, and sexual endurance. Tribal traditions across Rajasthan and Bengal use fresh root decoction in milk for impotence, oligospermia, and low libido. Clinical studies show significant improvement in sperm count and motility.
Anti-inflammatory & Anti-arthritic
Lupeol and lupeol acetate from the root bark demonstrate potent inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis and TNF-α — validated across carrageenan, formaldehyde, and cotton pellet granuloma models. Traditional application of root decoction for rheumatoid arthritis, gout, and musculoskeletal pain is well-supported pharmacologically. The shamimin flavone adds antioxidant synergy to the anti-inflammatory profile.
Wound Healing & Thorn Therapy
In classical Sushruta tradition, the powdered thorn was used to close wounds — a practice now understood through the silica-rich, haemostatic, and antimicrobial nature of the thorn material. The bark decoction is used to wash chronic ulcers and infected wounds. Modern studies confirm significant acceleration of wound contraction and epithelialisation by bark extracts in excision and incision wound models.
Anti-diarrheal & Dysenteric
Mocharas gum and root bark are classical Grahi (absorbent, astringent) preparations for chronic diarrhoea, dysentery, and irritable bowel syndrome. The combination of tannins (mucosal astringency), antimicrobials (gallic, ellagic acid), and demulcents creates a comprehensive anti-diarrheal action. Root decoction with buttermilk is a traditional Rajasthani preparation for amoebic dysentery.
Women's Health & Gynaecology
Shalmali is a comprehensive gynaecological herb: Mocharas arrests menorrhagia and metrorrhagia; the root supports hormonal balance and treats leucorrhoea; the flowers are used in post-partum recovery. Its phytoestrogenic β-sitosterol modulates oestrogen receptor activity. Classical texts describe specific formulations for every stage — menstrual, reproductive, menopausal — of a woman's hormonal life.
Hepatoprotective & Diuretic
Root bark extracts show significant hepatoprotective activity in CCl₄ and paracetamol-induced liver injury models — reducing AST, ALT, and bilirubin while increasing hepatic glutathione. Folk traditions across Bengal and Assam use the root decoction for jaundice and liver enlargement. The plant also shows moderate diuretic activity, supporting its classical use in urinary disorders and oedema.
Antioxidant & Immunomodulatory
Flower extracts rich in anthocyanins and shamimin show excellent DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activity. The bark and root exhibit significant immunostimulant effects — increasing macrophage phagocytosis, NK cell activity, and spleen index in experimental models. The flower decoction is traditionally used in seasonal fevers and as a spring tonic across Bengal during the Shimul blooming season.

How to Use Shimul

Different parts of the Shimul tree require different preparations and serve distinct therapeutic purposes. The root is the primary internal tonic; the gum (Mocharas) is the astringent-haemostatic; the flowers are gentle and broadly usable; the thorn powder is topical only. All internal preparations beyond culinary use of flowers should be taken under qualified Ayurvedic guidance due to the potency of root and gum preparations.

Safety & Precautions

Shimul is well-tolerated at classical doses across centuries of traditional use. The flowers are entirely safe and can be freely used as seasonal food and medicine. The root and gum are potent and require measured dosing; the seeds and seed oil are safe externally but warrant caution internally in excess.

  • Pregnancy — root preparations: The root has documented oxytocic (uterus-stimulating) properties in classical texts and some animal studies. Root decoctions and root powder must be avoided during all trimesters. Flower and gum preparations in small doses are considered generally safe but should be supervised.
  • Excessive root dosage: High doses of root bark may cause gastrointestinal irritation, nausea, and loose stools. Classical dose is 3–6g of dry root or 30–50ml of decoction — do not exceed without physician guidance.
  • Thorn — internal use strictly contraindicated: Thorn powder is for topical haemostatic use only. The silica-rich, sharp material must never be consumed internally. A clear contraindication.
  • Silk cotton (kapok) inhalation: Individuals with asthma, allergic rhinitis, or respiratory sensitivity should avoid prolonged exposure to airborne kapok fibres during the seed-burst season (April–May) as they can trigger bronchospasm and allergic reactions.
  • Kapha-dominant individuals — root in milk: The root-in-milk preparation is heavy and unctuous — best suited for Vata-dominant individuals. Those with obesity, sluggish digestion, or high Ama should take with warming digestives (ginger, trikatu) to prevent Kapha aggravation.
  • Drug interactions — anticoagulants: Mocharas gum's haemostatic activity may antagonise anticoagulant medications (warfarin, heparin, aspirin). Monitor carefully if using therapeutically alongside blood-thinning drugs.
  • Hypotensive medications: Some root extracts show mild hypotensive activity. Individuals on antihypertensive drugs should monitor blood pressure when consuming root preparations therapeutically.
  • Flower consumption — quantity: Fresh flowers are edible and traditionally used in Bengali and tribal cuisines, but should be consumed in moderate quantities — 2–3 flowers at a time. Excessive consumption may cause mild gastric bloating in sensitive individuals due to their mucilaginous content.
