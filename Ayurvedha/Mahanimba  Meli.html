Mahanimba
Persian Lilac — The Great Neem of the Ancients
Melia azedarach L.  ·  Meliaceae

❧

Known in Sanskrit as Mahanimba — "the great neem" — and in modern botany as Melia azedarach, this majestic deciduous tree bridges ancient Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia and modern pharmacology. Revered in the Charaka Samhita for its Kapha-Pitta pacifying virtues and catalogued in nearly every regional Nighantu, its bitter bark, insecticidal berries, and aromatic flowers have served civilisations from the Indus to the Mediterranean for over three millennia.

01

Botanical Profile

Melia azedarach is a fast-growing deciduous tree of the Meliaceae family, reaching 7–15 m (occasionally 45 m) in height, with a broad spreading crown. Native to the Himalayan foothills, northern India, and Southeast Asia, it has naturalised across subtropical and warm-temperate regions worldwide — from the Mediterranean to Australia — and is widely planted as an ornamental and avenue tree.

Its leaves are large (25–50 cm), bipinnate or tripinnate, with serrate leaflets — a visually distinctive foliage that clearly sets it apart from the true neem (Azadirachta indica). Flowers appear in spring as dense, fragrant axillary panicles of pale lavender to lilac petals with a purple staminal tube — among the most elegant of any Indian medicinal tree. Fruits are drupes 1–2 cm in diameter, turning from green to bright yellow upon ripening, and persist ornamentally through winter after leaf fall. The bark is dark reddish-brown with longitudinal fissures. All parts of the plant contain active limonoid compounds; the berries and bark are considered toxic in quantity.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Mahanimba is one of the richest known sources of limonoids — a class of highly oxygenated triterpenoids unique to the Meliaceae and Rutaceae families. Over 90 limonoids have been isolated from various parts of the tree, with toosendanin, azadirachtin analogues, nimbolide, and melianone among the most pharmacologically significant. These compounds are responsible for its remarkable pesticidal, antifeedant, antitumour, and antimalarial activities.

The bark is rich in tannins, coumarins (fraxinellone), and alkaloids. The fruits contain tetranortriterpene limonoids, neurotoxic resins (in pericarp), and fatty acid-rich seeds. Leaves yield flavonoids including rutin and quercetin, as well as phenolic acids. The flowers produce a complex essential oil dominated by linalool, geraniol, and α-terpineol.

Ayurvedic Significance

Mahanimba appears prominently in Charaka Samhita (Sutrasthana, Chikitsasthana) among herbs indicated for kushtha (skin diseases), krimi (parasites), and jwara (fever). Sushruta classifies it in Aragvadhadi gana — the group of potent purifying herbs — while Vagbhata employs it in preparations for worm infestation, skin disorders, and inflammatory conditions. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu places it in Vatadi Varga; Dhanvantari Nighantu in Aamradi Varga; Kaiyadeva Nighantu in Aushadi Varga.

The classical shloka from Bhavaprakasha reads: "Mahanimbo mahateekshno drekha pichumardakah, tikto ushno laghu ruksha kapha pittakrimijwaran" — describing Mahanimba as intensely sharp, bitter, hot in potency, light and rough in quality, destroying Kapha, Pitta, parasites, and fever.

Key Health Benefits

A century of modern pharmacological investigation has validated the core Ayurvedic claims for Mahanimba — establishing robust scientific evidence for its antiparasitic, antimicrobial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and emerging anticancer activities through in vitro and in vivo studies worldwide.

Antiparasitic & Anthelmintic
Mahanimba's most celebrated classical and modern application. Limonoids — particularly toosendanin and deacetylsendanin — demonstrate potent activity against intestinal worms, liver flukes, and malarial parasites. Bark decoction is a primary Ayurvedic treatment for krimi (helminthiasis); seed oil historically used against head lice and mange.
Skin Disorders (Kushtha Chikitsa)
Among the Aragvadhadi herbs for skin purification. Leaf paste and bark decoction are applied to eczema, psoriasis, scabies, ringworm, and chronic ulcers. Antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, and dermatophytes provides the pharmacological basis for this classical application.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Ethanolic bark extract significantly inhibits carrageenan-induced paw oedema and prostaglandin synthesis in animal models. Classical use in arthralgia, rheumatic pain, and neuralgia is supported by the anti-inflammatory activity of melianone, quercetin, and limonoid fractions. Trunk bark poultice is applied to swollen joints.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Leaf, bark, and seed extracts show broad-spectrum activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria including E. coli, Salmonella, Klebsiella, and Pseudomonas. Antifungal activity against Candida and Aspergillus is documented in multiple studies. The flower essential oil exhibits notable antimicrobial potential via linalool and geraniol.
Hepatoprotective Action
Paradoxically, while the fruits in excess cause hepatotoxicity, standardised bark and leaf extracts demonstrate hepatoprotective activity in CCl₄-induced liver injury models — attributed to fraxinellone, catechin, and gallic acid. Bark decoction is a traditional remedy for jaundice in several ethnomedical traditions.
Anticancer Potential
One of the most active research fronts: nimbolide and other limonoids from Melia azedarach induce apoptosis in human cancer cell lines including HeLa (cervical), MCF-7 (breast), and A549 (lung) with IC₅₀ values comparable to standard chemotherapy agents in vitro. Mechanism involves mitochondrial pathway activation and NF-κB inhibition.
Antidiabetic Effects
Aqueous and methanolic leaf extracts reduce fasting blood glucose significantly in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat models. Proposed mechanisms include alpha-glucosidase inhibition, pancreatic beta-cell regeneration, and improved peripheral glucose uptake — mirroring the anti-diabetic applications documented in Unani and folk traditions.
Natural Pesticide (Biopesticide)
Widely used in organic farming: Melia seed oil and leaf extracts disrupt insect development, moulting, and reproduction through azadirachtin-related limonoids. Unlike neem (A. indica), Melia-based biopesticides are more water-soluble, facilitating preparation of aqueous sprays. Used against aphids, locusts, whiteflies, and stored-grain pests.

How to Use Mahanimba

Internal preparations of Mahanimba must always be prepared and dosed under the guidance of a qualified Ayurvedic physician. The bark and leaves are the safest internal medicinal parts; fruits and seeds carry significant toxicity at uncalibrated doses and are primarily used externally or in highly processed classical formulations. Topical use is generally safe and widely practiced.

Safety & Precautions

Mahanimba is classified as a Tikshna dravya (sharp herb) in Ayurveda — powerful and requiring precise dosing. The fruit (berry/drupe) is the most toxic part: ingestion of 6–8 fruits can be fatal in children; adult toxicity begins at approximately 185 g of fruit pulp. The neurotoxic and gastrointestinal toxic resin in the pericarp causes vomiting, bradycardia, paralysis, and seizures in overdose. Internal use of any part should be strictly supervised.

  • Fruits — strictly avoid internal use: The berries (drupes) are toxic to humans, dogs, cats, and livestock. Do not consume or allow children near fallen fruit. Even ornamental planting in family gardens warrants caution.
  • Pregnancy: Contraindicated internally. Classical texts and modern evidence suggest uterotonic and abortifacient activity. External topical use may be acceptable with caution.
  • Children: Internal use is contraindicated due to narrow therapeutic window and heightened fruit toxicity risk in children.
  • Vata-predominant constitutions: Tikshna, ruksha, ushna properties can aggravate Vata. Always combine with unctuous anupana (ghee, sesame oil, warm milk) when used in Vata-type individuals.
  • Prolonged internal use: Not recommended beyond 4–6 weeks without physician review. Periodic liver function tests are advisable during extended therapeutic courses.
  • Hepatic conditions: While protective at low doses, the overall alkaloid and terpenoid load may stress an already compromised liver. Avoid in decompensated liver disease.
  • Drug interactions: Potential interaction with anticoagulants (quercetin may augment effect), antidiabetic drugs (additive hypoglycemia risk), and immunosuppressants. Inform your physician of all concurrent medications.
  • Topical allergic reaction: Rarely, leaf or bark paste may cause contact dermatitis. Patch-test before broad application, especially in atopic individuals.
  • Seed oil — avoid ingestion: Cold-pressed seed oil is for external use only. Oral ingestion of seed oil is associated with toxicity.
