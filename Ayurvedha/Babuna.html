Babuna
Chamomile — The Earth Apple of the Ancient World
Matricaria chamomilla L. (German)  ·  Chamaemelum nobile (L.) All. (Roman)

Fig. 1 — Matricaria chamomilla in full bloom; white ray florets reflexing backward from the characteristic hollow golden receptacle — the mark distinguishing German from Roman chamomile  ·  Fig. 2 — The deep azure-blue essential oil of German chamomile, its colour entirely absent in the fresh flower and formed only upon steam distillation through conversion of matricine to chamazulene

❧

The ancient Greeks called it Chamaimelon — ground apple — for the sweet, clean scent of its crushed flowers. Ancient Egyptians dedicated it to the sun god Ra and used it to treat malaria. For five thousand years across Europe, the Islamic world, and Asia, a cup of chamomile tea has been the universal prescription for anxiety, insomnia, stomach cramps, and a feverish child. Yet beneath this reputation as the gentlest of all herbal remedies lies a surprisingly complex pharmacology — and a vivid blue essential oil that does not exist in the flower at all, formed only in the moment of distillation.

01

Botanical Profile

Two distinct species are used as medicinal chamomile, sharing similar appearance, aroma, and therapeutic properties but differing in botanical origin, geographic range, essential oil composition, and key constituents. Both belong to the Asteraceae (daisy) family and produce the characteristic white-petalled, yellow-centred flower heads that have made chamomile one of the most recognisable medicinal herbs on earth.

German Chamomile — Primary Medicinal
Matricaria chamomilla L.
The principal medicinal species worldwide, also called Blue Chamomile, Wild Chamomile, or Hungarian Chamomile. Native to southern and eastern Europe; widely naturalised globally. Annual herb, 20–80 cm. Hollow, conical receptacle (key ID feature). Produces deep blue essential oil rich in chamazulene and bisabolol. The species used in most clinical research, European pharmacopoeias, and standardised extracts.
Roman Chamomile — Aromatic & Cosmetic
Chamaemelum nobile (L.) All.
Also called English or Noble Chamomile. Native to western Europe, especially Britain, France, and Spain. Perennial, low-growing, creeping (5–30 cm). Solid (not hollow) receptacle. Essential oil is pale blue to colourless, richer in isobutyl angelate and isoamyl angelate esters — fruitier, more apple-like aroma. Preferred in aromatherapy and cosmetics. Traditional herb of English cottage gardens and lawn chamomile paths.

In Indian medicine, chamomile entered primarily through Unani (Greco-Islamic) medical tradition under the Persian name Babuna (بابونه) — derived from the Greek Chamaimelon via Arabic Babunaj. It is listed in Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine as Uqhuwan and described as a nervine, digestive, and anti-spasmodic. In contemporary Indian ethnobotany it grows naturally in the higher altitude meadows of Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand, where it is harvested for both internal and topical use.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Chamomile flower heads contain one of the most pharmacologically rich and well-studied phytochemical profiles of any medicinal herb — encompassing terpenoids, flavonoids, coumarins, mucilages, and phenolic acids in a matrix whose synergistic action produces effects greater than any single constituent. The essential oil — 0.3–1.5% of the dried flower — is the most commercially significant fraction, and German chamomile's oil is unique in the plant kingdom for its vivid deep-blue colour, which develops entirely through the distillation process.

Chamazulene — The Blue Compound That Doesn't Exist in the Flower
German chamomile flowers contain no blue pigment. The distinctive deep azure-blue colour of the essential oil forms only during steam distillation, when heat converts the colourless sesquiterpene lactone matricine (a prodrug) into chamazulene — one of the most potent natural anti-inflammatory terpenoids known. Chamazulene inhibits leukotriene B4 formation, scavenges reactive oxygen species, and inhibits COX-2 — all in a vivid blue molecule that literally comes into existence at the moment of healing. Roman chamomile oil contains very little chamazulene; German chamomile oil is 5–15% chamazulene by composition, determining its characteristic indigo colour and superior anti-inflammatory profile.

03

Traditional Medical Significance

Chamomile's position in world medicine is unique — it is one of very few plants that occupies a primary therapeutic role across four entirely distinct medical traditions: Greco-Roman medicine, Unani (Islamic) medicine, European folk and monastic medicine, and contemporary Western phytotherapy. Each tradition arrived independently at broadly similar indications — digestive complaints, anxiety, fever, and skin inflammation — a convergence that reflects genuine, multi-system pharmacological activity.

"Babuna mulathhim, muhallil, musakkin lil-alam, nafi lil-mafasil wal-aswaam, yusakhkhin wa yulayyin" — Ibn Sina, Al-Qanun fi'l-Tibb (Canon of Medicine), Book II — On the properties of Babunaj (Chamomile)

Ibn Sina's description — "Babuna is a softener, a dissolver of inflammation, a pacifier of pain, beneficial for joints and swellings, it warms and softens" — encapsulates the Unani understanding of chamomile as a mulathhim (tissue-softening), muhallil (inflammation-resolving), and musakkin (pain-sedating) drug. This characterisation aligns precisely with modern understanding of bisabolol's wound-healing and chamazulene's anti-inflammatory mechanisms. In European monastic medicine, chamomile was one of the nine sacred herbs of the Anglo-Saxon Nine Herbs Charm. Hildegard von Bingen, writing in the 12th century, prescribed it for digestive and uterine complaints. The German common name Alles zutraut — "capable of anything" — reflects its reputation as a universal remedy.

Key Health Benefits

Apigenin & the Benzodiazepine Receptor — Why Chamomile Tea Actually Works for Anxiety
For decades, chamomile's anxiolytic reputation was dismissed as placebo or expectation. The molecular mechanism was clarified in 1994 when Viola et al. demonstrated that apigenin — chamomile's principal flavone — binds competitively to the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor complex, the same receptor site targeted by diazepam (Valium), producing sedation and anxiolysis without the dependency or respiratory depression of pharmaceutical benzodiazepines. A landmark randomised clinical trial by Amsterdam et al. (2009) in 57 generalised anxiety disorder patients showed chamomile extract produced significantly superior anxiety reduction versus placebo on the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale. A follow-on 2016 RCT by the same group confirmed that long-term chamomile use significantly reduced relapse of GAD symptoms. This makes chamomile one of the very few herbal medicines with both a clear receptor-level mechanism and replicated RCT evidence for anxiety.
Anxiety & GAD
Apigenin's binding to GABA-A benzodiazepine receptors produces dose-dependent anxiolytic effects without the dependence or withdrawal associated with pharmaceutical benzodiazepines. Two RCTs in generalised anxiety disorder (Amsterdam et al., 2009 and 2016) show significant symptom reduction and relapse prevention. Chamomile tea is the most evidence-supported single herbal beverage for anxiety management available without prescription.
Sleep & Insomnia
Apigenin's GABAergic sedation and chamomile's mild muscle-relaxant antispasmodic action combine to reduce sleep onset latency and improve sleep quality. A clinical trial in postnatal women (Forster DA, 2006) showed chamomile tea significantly improved sleep quality and reduced depression symptoms. Animal studies demonstrate increased sleep duration with apigenin administration. Widely used in paediatric sleep disturbance as a safe alternative to pharmaceutical sedatives.
Digestive — IBS, Spasm & Colic
α-Bisabolol and bisabolol oxides relax smooth muscle in the GI tract, reducing cramping and spasm. Polysaccharide mucilages coat and protect the gastric and intestinal mucosa. Anti-inflammatory chamazulene reduces mucosal inflammation in IBS. A systematic review (Srivastava JK, 2010) confirmed chamomile's efficacy in infantile colic, abdominal cramp, flatulence, and IBS. Chamomile tea is among the safest digestive antispasmodics for children and infants.
Skin — Wound Healing & Eczema
α-Bisabolol is licensed as a cosmetic active ingredient in Europe for anti-irritant, skin-soothing, and wound-healing formulations. It accelerates re-epithelialisation, reduces TEWL (trans-epidermal water loss), and inhibits inflammatory mediators in the dermis. Clinical studies confirm bisabolol-containing chamomile preparations superior to 0.25% hydrocortisone in mild eczema with equivalent results in contact dermatitis. Chamomile compress is classical European treatment for weeping wounds, burns, and dermatitis.
Oral Health & Mucositis
Chamomile mouthwash significantly reduces chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis — a painful, debilitating side effect of cancer treatment — demonstrated in a clinical trial (Mazokopakis EE, 2005). Anti-inflammatory bisabolol, antimicrobial apigenin, and astringent flavonoids reduce mucosal inflammation, bacterial load, and ulcer formation. Classically used for mouth ulcers, gingivitis, sore throat gargle, and teething inflammation in infants.
Anti-inflammatory & Analgesic
Chamazulene inhibits COX-2 and 5-LOX (leukotriene synthesis), producing anti-inflammatory effects comparable to indomethacin in some assay systems at equivalent concentrations. α-Bisabolol inhibits substance P-mediated neurogenic inflammation. Essential oil steam inhalation and topical compress used classically for rheumatic joint pain, neuralgia, sinusitis, and muscle aches. Particularly effective in heat-application (hot chamomile compress over painful joints).
Anti-diabetic & Glycaemic
Apigenin inhibits aldose reductase — the enzyme responsible for diabetic complications including neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy — through sorbitol pathway inhibition. A clinical trial in type 2 diabetic patients (Zemestani M, 2016) showed chamomile tea consumed 3× daily for 8 weeks significantly reduced HbA1c, serum insulin, and HOMA-IR versus water control. Apigenin also activates PPAR-γ, improving insulin sensitivity.
Antipyretic & Antimicrobial
One of the oldest recorded fever remedies — used in Egyptian, Greco-Roman, and medieval European medicine. Apigenin and luteolin inhibit prostaglandin E2 synthesis (the primary fever mediator). Chamomile essential oil shows significant antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis, E. coli, and Candida albicans. Bisabolol oxides specifically inhibit Helicobacter pylori growth — relevant to chamomile's classical use in gastric complaints and peptic ulcer.

Cultural & Historical Heritage

Few herbs carry as continuous and geographically diverse a history as chamomile. Its therapeutic record begins with ancient Egypt — chamomile is mentioned in the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) for treatment of malaria and as an offering to Ra. The ancient Romans used it as a battlefield wound dressing and as a flavouring for beer and mead. In Anglo-Saxon England, it was one of the Nine Sacred Herbs of the tenth-century Lacnunga manuscript. Peter Rabbit's mother gave him chamomile tea after his escapade in Mr McGregor's garden — a literary reference so culturally embedded that Beatrix Potter needed no explanation of why chamomile was the remedy of choice.

How to Use Babuna

Chamomile is among the most versatile and safest of all medicinal herbs — appropriate for use from infancy through old age, with a multi-millennial record of safe daily consumption as both food and medicine. The flower heads deliver the full therapeutic profile; the essential oil concentrates the terpenoid fraction for topical use. Quality matters significantly — dried chamomile should be a bright gold-yellow, strongly aromatic, and stored away from light and heat to preserve the volatile oil fraction.

Safety & Precautions

Chamomile has one of the most favourable safety profiles of all medicinal herbs and is approved for use in infants, pregnant women (with caveats), and the elderly. The primary clinical concern is allergic cross-reactivity within the Asteraceae family — a documented but relatively uncommon phenomenon that receives disproportionate attention relative to chamomile's overall safety record.

  • Asteraceae allergy (ragweed cross-reactivity): The most important safety consideration. Chamomile belongs to the Asteraceae family — the same family as ragweed (Ambrosia), chrysanthemum, marigold (Tagetes), and echinacea. Individuals with known ragweed pollen allergy have a 20–30% chance of cross-reactivity to chamomile, potentially experiencing oral allergy syndrome, urticaria, or — in rare cases — anaphylaxis. Skin-prick testing before therapeutic use is advisable in atopic individuals with strong Asteraceae sensitivities.
  • Pregnancy: Chamomile tea at culinary doses (1–2 cups daily) is widely used in pregnancy and is not considered a significant risk. However, high therapeutic doses have traditionally been classified as uterine stimulants — classical Unani texts note chamomile as mudir-i-haiz (emmenagogue). Avoid therapeutic-dose supplementation (capsule extracts) in the first trimester as a precaution.
  • Anticoagulant drug interaction: Chamomile coumarins (herniarin, umbelliferone) have mild anticoagulant activity. A case report documented significantly elevated INR in a warfarin patient consuming large quantities of chamomile tea. Individuals on warfarin, heparin, or antiplatelet therapy should limit consumption to moderate culinary levels and inform their anticoagulation clinic.
  • Sedative drug interaction: Apigenin's GABA-A agonism may potentiate pharmaceutical sedatives, anxiolytics, and hypnotics — benzodiazepines, zopiclone, barbiturates. This interaction is dose-dependent and largely beneficial (allowing dose reduction) but can cause excessive sedation at high combined doses. Inform prescribing physician of regular chamomile supplement use.
  • Hypoglycaemic drug interaction: Chamomile extract significantly reduces blood glucose in clinical studies. Concurrent use with metformin, insulin, or sulfonylureas may produce additive glucose lowering — monitor blood glucose carefully and discuss with physician before starting supplementation.
  • Essential oil — internal use: Chamomile essential oil should not be consumed internally. The concentrated terpenoid fraction, while safe topically at diluted doses, can cause nausea, vomiting, and mucous membrane irritation if swallowed undiluted. Use only the infusion or standardised extract internally.
  • Eye compresses: Chamomile eye washes and compresses are widely practised but carry a small risk of introducing bacterial contamination or triggering contact conjunctivitis in sensitive individuals. Always use freshly prepared, cooled, strained infusion; never use commercial essential oil near the eyes.
  • Quality and adulteration: Chamomile is frequently adulterated or substituted with Anthemis cotula (stinking chamomile / mayweed), which lacks the therapeutic anthraquinone and bisabolol profile and can cause skin blistering. Purchase German chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) from reputable suppliers; confirm species on label. The distinctive hollow, conical receptacle and backward-reflexed ray florets identify genuine German chamomile.
