Star Anise — The Eight-Pointed Spice of the East
Illicium verum Hook. f.  ·  Schisandraceae (formerly Illiciaceae)

Fig. 1 — Whole dried star anise fruit (Illicium verum) with characteristic eight-pointed follicle arrangement  ·  Fig. 2 — Ground spice and expressed essential oil rich in trans-anethole (~90%)

❧

Called Teja in Sanskrit — "that which is brilliant, sharp, and luminous" — Star Anise is one of the most chemically potent of all culinary spices, yielding an essential oil of extraordinary purity. Honoured in Chinese medicine for over 1,300 years, introduced to Europe via the Silk Road in the 16th century, and embraced by Ayurvedic and Unani physicians alike, Illicium verum sits at a rare convergence of gastronomy, pharmacy, and perfumery — its principal compound trans-anethole serving as the molecular basis of anise-flavoured liqueurs worldwide and, more recently, as the precursor to the antiviral drug oseltamivir (Tamiflu).

01

Botanical Profile

Star Anise is a medium-sized evergreen tree native to the limestone hills of southwest China (Guangxi, Yunnan) and the adjoining highlands of northeast Vietnam — a narrow, specific native range that makes it one of the more geographically restricted major spice trees. The tree grows to 8–15 metres, bearing lustrous, lanceolate-elliptic leaves, pale yellow flowers, and the unmistakable fruit: a woody, eight-follicled star-shaped structure (a schizocarp) that turns from green to rust-brown as it dries. Each follicle contains one hard, shiny, amber-coloured seed.

The plant belongs to Schisandraceae (formerly its own family Illiciaceae) and is completely unrelated to common anise (Pimpinella anisum) — the similar taste arises purely from convergent production of the same aromatic compound, trans-anethole. The tree begins fruiting at 6 years and can remain productive for over 100 years. China accounts for approximately 90% of global production; the spice is harvested twice yearly — July to August and February to March — with the fruit collected just before full ripening.

02

Phytochemistry & Composition

Star Anise is one of the most phytochemically elegant of all spices: its essential oil (yield 5–8% from dried fruit) is dominated by a single compound — trans-anethole — to a degree unusual in the plant kingdom. This chemical simplicity belies an extraordinary range of biological activity rooted in anethole's structural properties as a phenylpropanoid. The remaining fraction includes a suite of terpenoids, flavonoids, phenylpropanoids, and fixed oils contributing to antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects.

Crucially, shikimic acid — extracted from the dried fruit at yields of 3–7% — became globally significant in 2005 as the primary precursor for the synthesis of oseltamivir phosphate (Tamiflu®), causing a worldwide shortage of star anise during avian influenza outbreaks. The fruit also contains quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin (flavonoids) alongside fixed oils rich in linoleic and oleic acids in the seeds.

Ayurvedic & Classical Significance

Star Anise entered Ayurvedic practice relatively late compared to its long history in Chinese medicine — it is referenced in later mediaeval texts and Nighantu compilations rather than the foundational Charaka and Sushruta Samhitas. Bhavaprakasha Nighantu lists it as Takkola or Phanijatak in the Haritakyadi Varga; Raj Nighantu and Dhanvantari Nighantu also record it. It is often grouped with other aromatic Katu-Tikta spices used as carminatives, digestive stimulants, and respiratory herbs.

In Unani medicine it is a premier drug under the name Badyan — prescribed for digestive complaints, colic, respiratory congestion, and as a galactagogue (promoting breast milk production). In Traditional Chinese Medicine, bā jiǎo is one of the five spices in Wu Xiang Fen, warming the kidney and spleen Qi, dispersing cold, and relieving pain.

04

Key Health Benefits

Star Anise bridges the ancient and modern — many of its classical applications in digestive and respiratory medicine have been confirmed by contemporary pharmacology, while its antiviral and oestrogenic properties represent newer research frontiers.

Carminative & Digestive
The premier classical indication: trans-anethole relaxes smooth muscle in the GI tract, expelling trapped gas and relieving bloating, colic, and intestinal spasm. Used in flatulence, indigestion (ajirna), abdominal distension, and hiccough. Works synergistically with black pepper and long pepper in classical digestive formulations.
Antispasmodic & Anti-colic
Anethole's calcium-channel-modulating and smooth-muscle-relaxant properties make it effective in spasmodic colic, irritable bowel syndrome, and dysmenorrhoea. Infant colic preparations in traditional Mediterranean and Asian medicine have used dilute star anise tea — though caution is essential regarding correct species identification.
Respiratory & Expectorant
Used in bronchitis, cough, asthma, and upper respiratory infections. Acts as a mucolytic (thins mucus) and bronchospasmolytic (opens airways). The volatile oils stimulate ciliary movement in the bronchial mucosa, facilitating expulsion of secretions. Combined with honey and long pepper in classical cough preparations.
Antiviral — Oseltamivir Precursor
Star anise's shikimic acid is the primary industrial precursor for the synthesis of oseltamivir phosphate (Tamiflu®), the leading antiviral drug against influenza A and B. While consuming star anise does not provide therapeutic antiviral levels of oseltamivir, the whole-plant shikimic acid and anethole show direct antiviral activity in vitro against influenza, herpes simplex, and hepatitis C viruses.
Antimicrobial & Antifungal
Essential oil and anethole show broad-spectrum activity against E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella, Listeria, Candida albicans, and Aspergillus. Particularly useful in food-borne pathogen contexts; anethole disrupts bacterial cell membranes and inhibits biofilm formation. Used topically in traditional medicine for oral infections and skin fungal conditions.
Oestrogenic & Galactagogue
Dianethole and photoanethole (anethole polymers) bind weakly to oestrogen receptors — explaining classical use for amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, and promotion of breast milk (galactagogue). Used in Unani and folk medicine for female hormonal complaints. This oestrogenic activity also underpins caution in oestrogen-sensitive conditions.
Antioxidant & Anti-inflammatory
Quercetin, kaempferol, rutin, and anethole collectively deliver significant antioxidant capacity — DPPH radical scavenging in extracts rivals established antioxidants. Anti-inflammatory activity is mediated through NF-κB pathway inhibition and reduced prostaglandin synthesis. Used in traditional formulations for arthritis and inflammatory pain.
Oral Health & Breath Freshener
Chewing whole star anise is a traditional post-meal breath freshener and oral antiseptic across South and Southeast Asia. The antimicrobial volatile oils suppress oral bacteria including S. mutans and Porphyromonas gingivalis (implicated in periodontitis). Used in classical Gandusha (oil-pulling) preparations and tooth powders.

Classical Formulations & Culinary Use

Star Anise occupies an important place in both culinary and medicinal traditions across Asia. It is one of the five spices in Chinese Wu Xiang Fen, a cornerstone of Vietnamese phở broth, and a key flavouring in European anise-based liqueurs (pastis, sambuca, ouzo, arak). In Ayurvedic and Unani pharmacy it appears in digestive, respiratory, and reproductive preparations.

How to Use Teja

Star Anise is versatile — used whole, ground, as an infusion, as essential oil, or as a decoction. Culinary use at typical food amounts is safe for most people. Medicinal doses involve larger quantities or concentrated forms and should be used mindfully, particularly in pregnancy, oestrogen-sensitive conditions, and infants.

Safety & Precautions

Star Anise at culinary doses is among the safest of all spices, with an extensive history of food use. Medicinal doses require more care. The single most critical safety concern is adulteration with Japanese Star Anise (Illicium anisatum), which is highly toxic (neurotoxic anisatin) and visually similar to untrained eyes — always source from reputable suppliers.

  • Toxic lookalike — Japanese Star Anise: Illicium anisatum contains anisatin — a potent GABA-antagonist causing seizures, respiratory failure, and death. It visually resembles Illicium verum. Several poisoning cases in Europe have resulted from mislabelled herbal teas. Always verify species; Japanese star anise has more than 8 follicles (typically 10–13) and a more pointed appearance.
  • Pregnancy (medicinal doses): Oestrogenic anethole polymers (dianethole, photoanethole) can stimulate uterine contractions at high medicinal doses. Culinary amounts in food are safe; medicinal-dose teas, decoctions, or essential oil use should be avoided in pregnancy.
  • Infants — special caution: Several cases of neurotoxicity (seizures, jitteriness, vomiting) in infants given star anise tea have been reported in Europe — largely traced to Japanese star anise contamination. Do not give star anise tea to infants under 12 months due to both toxicity risk and the difficulty of reliably distinguishing species.
  • Oestrogen-sensitive conditions: Due to oestrogenic activity, caution is warranted in oestrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer, uterine fibroids, endometriosis, and in patients on hormonal therapies or tamoxifen — at medicinal doses only; culinary use is unaffected.
  • Essential oil — internal use: Star anise essential oil is not for internal consumption except under specialist direction. Oral EO can cause nausea, vomiting, seizures, and pulmonary oedema in doses exceeding a few ml. Topical use must always be diluted.
  • Pitta constitution: The ushna (hot) virya may aggravate Pitta in susceptible individuals — add fennel or coriander as Pitta-cooling co-spices if GI burning, acid reflux, or skin flushing occurs.
  • Drug interactions: Possible potentiation of anticoagulants (aspirin, warfarin) due to anethole's anti-platelet activity; potential interaction with oestrogen-containing medications; may enhance sedative effect of CNS depressants at high essential oil doses.
  • Allergic reactions: Contact dermatitis to anethole is documented, particularly in atopic individuals. Oral allergy syndrome has been reported in people with sensitivity to other Apiaceae spices (anise, fennel, caraway). Patch test before regular topical EO application.
